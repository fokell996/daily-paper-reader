# Gaia neutron stars: demographics, birth rates, and connections to other neutron star populations 

Cheyanne Shariat, 1 Natsuko Yamaguchi, 1 Kareem El-Badry, 1 and Thomas J. Maccarone 2

> 1Department of Astronomy, California Institute of Technology, 1200 E. California Blvd, Pasadena, CA, 91125, USA
> 2Department of Physics & Astronomy, Box 41051, Texas Tech University, Lubbock TX 79409-1051, USA

ABSTRACT Gaia DR3 revealed a new class of neutron stars (NSs) in au-scale orbits with solar-type companions. How these ‘Gaia NSs’ form remains unclear, and constraining their intrinsic population properties is a necessary step toward understanding their origin. However, the current sample of 27 Gaia NS candidates is strongly biased by Gaia’s astrometric sensitivity. In this work, we carefully model the Gaia selection function and incorporate it into a hierarchical Bayesian framework to infer the intrinsic 

demographics of the Gaia NS population. We find that the population favors a decreasing period distribution with power-law slope αP = −0.75 ±0.35, a visible-star mass distribution peaked near M1 ≈

0.85 M ⊙ (σM1 ≈ 0.2 M ⊙), an NS mass distribution peaked near MNS ≈ 1.3 M ⊙ with a tail towards higher masses, and an eccentricity distribution weighted toward moderate-to-large eccentricities. The intrinsic space density is n ≈ 120 kpc −3 within 2 kpc, implying that only ∼ 3% of the local Gaia NS population has been discovered so far. Our forecasts predict that Gaia DR4 will increase the sample to ∼ 300 – 650 NS candidates, including access to longer orbital periods that will provide direct tests of formation models. Finally, we place Gaia NSs in the context of the broader Galactic NS population by comparing their intrinsic birth rate, lifetime, and size to other classes of isolated and binary NSs. Only 0.01% – 1% of all NSs are born into au-scale orbits with solar-type companions, making the birth rate of Gaia NSs 10 – 100 times smaller than that of high-mass X-ray binaries and broadly comparable to symbiotic X-ray binaries.

1. INTRODUCTION Neutron stars (NSs) are the remnants of most mas-sive stars ( ≳ 8 M ⊙) after they undergo supernovae. 80 – 100% of NS progenitors, massive OB-type stars, are born in binary or higher-order multiples (Sana et al. 2012; Moe & Di Stefano 2017), yet most ( ≳ 99%) young Galactic radio pulsars are isolated (Lorimer 2008). This contrast implies that over the course of binary evolution and NS formation, most massive stars merge or become unbound from their companions. Indeed, the space velocities of isolated pulsars support the notion that most NSs are born with large recoil kicks, often hundreds of km s −1 (Hobbs et al. 2005; Faucher-Gigu` ere & Kaspi 2006; Igoshev 2020; Disberg & Mandel 2025), presumably imparted due to mass loss during the supernova explosion (Blaauw 1961; Hills 1983; Kalogera 1996; Kalogera & Webbink 1998; Kalogera 2000). The subset of NSs that remain in binaries after core collapse, while a minority of the population, provides direct con-straints on the physics of massive-star binary evolution and NS formation. To date, several classes of NSs in binaries have been discovered in the Galaxy, including NSs with compan-ions that are substellar objects, low-mass main-sequence (MS) stars ( ≲ 2 M ⊙), high-mass MS stars ( ≳ 8 M ⊙), white dwarfs (WDs), and other NSs. NS–MS binaries with high-mass MS companions are often X-ray-selected and identified as (a) detached binaries or (b) actively accreting binaries. Young pulsars with confirmed stel-lar companions have almost exclusively massive OB-star companions (Kaspi et al. 1996; Lyne & Lorimer 1994), and detached X-ray-selected NS–MS binaries are clas-sified as high-mass X-ray binaries, commonly with Be-star or supergiant donors (Reig 2011; Chaty 2011; Fortin et al. 2023, 2024; Liu et al. 2006, 2007). NS–MS binaries with low-mass MS companions, on the other hand, are commonly identified in various classes of close accreting binaries but have historically rarely been found in wide ( Porb ≳ 50 days) detached arXiv:2610.00454v1 [astro-ph.SR] 30 Sep 2026 2binaries. In low-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs), the NS accretes from a Roche-lobe-filling MS donor and exhibits periodic outbursts, allowing them to be identified in X-ray surveys. In spider pulsars, a recycled NS irradiates and ablates a very low-mass companion, creating a ‘day’ and ‘night’ side on the MS star that may be identified through photometric variability (Bhattacharya & van den Heuvel 1991; Podsiadlowski et al. 2002; Tauris et al. 2012; Roberts 2013; Tauris et al. 2017). Recently, Gaia Data Release 3 (DR3) unveiled an entirely new NS population: detached NS–MS bina-ries containing solar-type companions in au-scale orbits (El-Badry et al. 2024b,c, 2026). Unlike other known NSs 1 with low-mass companions, these Gaia NS bina-ries show no evidence of past accretion, present accre-tion, or present pulsar activity (El-Badry et al. 2024b,c; Gao et al. 2026). Their present-day orbits are wide and thus effectively identical to their post-supernova config-urations, providing a probe of the core collapse process. The formation history of Gaia NSs remains a puzzle. Given their current separation of ∼ 1 au, the NS pro-genitor is expected to have interacted with the ∼ 1 M ⊙

companion when it was a red supergiant or super-AGB star. At such extreme initial mass ratios ( q ≲ 0.1), most models predict mass transfer to be dynamically unstable, leading to dramatic orbital shrinkage during a rapid common-envelope stage and, in many cases, a merger (Paczynski 1976; Webbink 1984; Ivanova et al. 2013; Tauris et al. 2017). How the Gaia NS progeni-tors avoided significant orbital contraction and remained wide upon emerging from mass transfer remains unclear. Even so, a wide post-mass-transfer binary must also sur-vive NS formation, during which sudden mass loss and natal kicks can unbind the system or substantially re-shape its orbit (Blaauw 1961; Hills 1983; Kalogera 1996; Kalogera & Webbink 1998). Dynamical formation in clusters could in principle by-pass some of these difficulties, but this channel also ap-pears inefficient for Gaia NSs. Open-cluster calculations find that most NSs are ejected by natal kicks before they can form Gaia NSs (Tanikawa et al. 2024), and dis-rupted dense-cluster models predict essentially no de-tectable au-scale NSs in Gaia DR3 (Schiebelbein-Zwack et al. 2026b), inconsistent with the 27 observed candi-dates. Furthermore, several metal-poor Gaia NS can-didates have extremely lithium-enhanced companions,

Despite the challenges posed by binary evolution, the Gaia NS binaries survived and emerged at wide sep-arations with moderate eccentricities. They therefore represent a novel outcome of extreme-mass-ratio bi-nary evolution, distinct from the classical story of com-pact LMXBs, where NS–MS binaries previously under-went unstable common envelope evolution. A promising pathway to disentangle the formation history of Gaia NSs is to constrain the intrinsic properties of their pop-ulation: how common are Gaia NSs, and what is their distribution of orbital periods, eccentricity, MS masses, and NS masses. However, the N = 27 observed Gaia NS candidate sample is biased. The Gaia non-single-star (NSS) cat-alog, from which Gaia NS candidates were selected, is shaped by the sensitivity of Gaia DR3, which reflects both intrinsic limitations of the data and cuts made by the non-single-star selection pipeline (Gaia Collabora-tion et al. 2023; Holl et al. 2023; Halbwachs et al. 2023; El-Badry et al. 2024a). The published Gaia NS sample also includes additional cuts imposed during candidate selection and spectroscopic follow-up (El-Badry et al. 2024b, 2026). These selection effects, while biasing the data, can be quantified and leveraged to study the in-trinsic properties of the underlying Gaia NS population. El-Badry et al. (2024a) recently built a forward model of Gaia epoch astrometry and the astrometric-orbit pipeline, allowing the selection effects of Gaia DR3 bi-naries to be calculated and used to bias-correct the pop-ulations. The ability to accurately characterize the se-lection function of Gaia NSs makes them unique among other NS binaries. For example, the primary selection bias for NS-LMXBs, their X-ray recurrence timescale, is highly uncertain and varies widely from object to object (e.g., Maccarone et al. 2022). In this paper, we carefully model the Gaia astrometric selection function to infer the intrinsic demographics of the Gaia NS population. Throughout the study, we as-sume the dark companions are NSs. If some candidates are instead massive WDs or unresolved WD+WD bina-ries, the inferred Gaia NS space densities and birth rates should be interpreted as upper limits. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 outlines our methodology, including the selection function character-ization and hierarchical Bayesian inference model. Sec-tion 3 shows the best-fit results, including the inferred distribution of orbital period, eccentricity, visible-star 3mass, NS mass, and intrinsic space density of Gaia NS binaries. We also forecast the yields of Gaia NSs in 

Gaia DR4. Section 4 discusses the mass distribution of Gaia NSs in the context of other NS binaries. Section 5 compares Gaia NSs to other Galactic NS populations, including isolated NSs, X-ray binaries, recycled pulsars, double neutron stars, and compact-object mergers. Sec-tion 6 summarizes the main conclusions. Additional de-tails are presented in Appendix A, B, C, and D.

METHODOLOGY 2.1. The Gaia NS Sample 

The observational sample studied in this paper is the 27 Gaia NS candidates identified by El-Badry et al. (2024b, 2026). These detached NS–MS candidates were first selected from the Gaia non-single-star (NSS) cata-log using the astrometric mass-ratio function (AMRF), which combines the astrometric orbit, parallax, period, and visible-star mass to place a lower limit on the com-panion mass as a function of its light contribution (Sha-haf et al. 2019, 2024). In the AMRF diagram, ordinary single-MS binaries follow a main-sequence companion lo-cus, while dark massive companions lie above this locus because they induce a large astrometric orbit without contributing much optical light. The Gaia NS candi-dates occupy this dark-companion region, with inferred companion masses in the NS range ( ≳ 1.25 M ⊙). From this initial selection, El-Badry et al. (2024b) and El-Badry et al. (2026) obtained RV follow-up to validate the astrometric solution and derive joint Gaia +RV or-bital solutions. Several candidates were also identified as compact object candidates in previous Gaia DR3 sam-ples (e.g., Andrews et al. 2022). The published Gaia NS sample from El-Badry et al. (2024b) and El-Badry et al. (2026) retained only sources with (a) G < 15 to allow for precise RV follow-up, (b) a joint-fit companion mass MNS > 1.25 M ⊙, (c) RV coverage over at least half an orbit, and (d) published quality cuts that remove spurious astrometric solutions. They measure the orbital period ( Porb ), eccentricity ( e), luminous-star mass ( M1), and NS candidate mass ( MNS )for every Gaia NS candidate using their joint-fitting re-sults. The final sample spans Porb = 189 – 1405 d, 

M1 = 0 .64 – 1 .28 M ⊙, MNS = 1 .28 – 1 .88 M ⊙, and 

e = 0 .12 – 0 .79, with typical uncertainties of ≈ 5% on 

M1, ≈ 3% on MNS , < 0.3% on periods, and ≈ 1% on eccentricities. As discussed in the discovery papers (El-Badry et al. 2024b; El-Badry 2024; El-Badry et al. 2026), these bi-naries are NS candidates because the nature of the dark companion is uncertain. Fundamentally, the selection process constrains them to be dark companions (relative to the luminous star at optical wavelengths) with masses larger than 1 .25 M ⊙. Plausible astrophysical sources satisfying these constraints are NSs, ultramassive WDs, or compact WD+WD binaries hidden in a hierarchical triple (Shahaf et al. 2019, 2024; Hallakoun et al. 2024, 2026; Garbutt et al. 2024). Compact double-WD bi-naries are generally difficult to explain on evolutionary grounds because the inner binary would need a near-Chandrasekhar total mass and to remain dynamically stable while the inner binary progenitors lost mass and expanded the outer orbit (El-Badry et al. 2024b,c; Too-nen et al. 2016, 2020; Shariat et al. 2023, 2025, 2026). A single ultramassive WD is only a possibility for the 11 objects below the Chandrasekhar limit ( ≈ 1.37 M ⊙;Althaus et al. 2022), and these stars would need to have been drawn from the extreme high-mass tail of the WD mass distribution 2. 70% (19 /27) of the candidates, in-cluding Gaia NS1 with MNS = 1 .88 ± 0.04 M ⊙ (El-Badry et al. 2024c, 2026), lie above 1 .35 M ⊙, making them likely single NSs (El-Badry et al. 2024b). To test the impact of possible contamination from WDs, we re-peat our analysis using only this high-mass subset of 

MNS > 1.35 M ⊙ (Appendix C), finding that it does not change our qualitative conclusions. The eccentricities of Gaia NSs provide an additional clue: the candidates are more eccentric than ordinary WD–MS binaries at simi-lar periods (Shahaf et al. 2023; El-Badry et al. 2024b). Here, we apply similar methods to model the selection function for the Gaia NS sample. To model the selection function of Gaia NSs, we ex-ecute a large injection–recovery campaign to estimate the probability that a binary with given physical pa-rameters would be recovered by Gaia and pass the sub-sequent Gaia NS candidate cuts. The assumed distri-butions of the injected population are not important so long as they provide adequate coverage over the param-eter space spanned by the observed sample (e.g., Essick et al. 2025). Our method is thus distinct from the clas-sical forward model approach. We start by injecting 140 ,000 synthetic detached NS– MS binaries within 2 kpc of the Sun. The parent stars are drawn from a Galaxia Milky Way model (Sharma et al. 2011), which assigns their sky position ( ra , dec ), proper motion ( pmra , pmdec ), and parallax ( ϖ). The apparent G-band magnitudes are derived for each in-jected source using MIST synthetic photometry, assum-ing no light contribution from the dark NS secondary. Line-of-sight extinction estimates are obtained using the 

combined19 dust map (Drimmel et al. 2003; Marshall et al. 2006; Green et al. 2019) from the mwdust package (Bovy et al. 2016). The metallicity ([Fe/H]) of each star is sampled from the skew-normal distribution of Zhang et al. (2023), derived using the metallicity of stars in the solar neighborhood. The age is chosen uniformly between 0 and 12 Gyr to reflect a roughly constant star formation history during this period. The maximum age of a binary is chosen such that the primary is on the MS at present, given its M1 and [Fe/H], with an upper bound of 12 Gyr. The MS lifetimes for the companion stars are evaluated via interpolation of MIST evolution-ary tracks (Morton 2015; Choi et al. 2016). This figure highlights the key features of the Gaia DR3 selec-tion function for NS-harboring binaries. 

Gaia DR3 is most sensitive to astrometric binaries with periods that are well sampled over its ∼ 10 3 day observing baseline (Figure 1). There is reduced sensi-tivity at short periods ( ≲ 300 days) because the angular on-sky orbit is small and at long periods ( ≳ 900 days) because the orbit becomes poorly covered as the pe-riod approaches or exceeds the baseline (Gaia Collab-oration et al. 2023; Halbwachs et al. 2023; Holl et al. 2023). A discrete drop in sensitivity is observed near 

Porb ≈ 365 days, which arises from the degeneracy with parallactic motion. The astrometric sensitivity is constant from low to moderate eccentricities (Figure 1), but decreases sharply at high eccentricities ( e ≳ 0.7). At high eccentricity, most of the photocenter motion is concentrated into a small fraction of the orbit near periastron, making the astrometric solution more sensitive to the scanning, which depends on location. Sensitivity increases toward larger visible-star masses ( M1) because these systems are typically brighter and thus have smaller astrometric uncertainties. The selection function also mildly favors larger NS masses ( MNS ). This trend reflects both the larger astrometric signal induced by more massive com-panions and the AMRF selection cuts. Namely, systems with larger inferred MNS induce more astrometric mo-tion at fixed orbital period and M1, and they also rule out a larger range of possible luminous companions, sat-isfying the AMRF cut. Comparing the observed Gaia NS candidates in Figure 1 to the detection probability reveals intrinsic features of the population. Detections in low-sensitivity regions carry more weight in an inference model because they suggest that many systems likely intrinsically reside in that region of parameter space, given some were ob-served. For example, there are three Gaia NS candidates with Porb < 350 days, despite the substantially reduced sensitivity at short periods, suggesting that compact systems may be intrinsically more abundant than im-plied by the raw detections alone (Figure 1). Conversely, only 5 candidates have MNS > 1.5 M ⊙, even though the selection function modestly favors higher NS masses through both larger astrometric signals and the AMRF cuts. The mock observations gener-ally follow the procedure outlined by Nagarajan et al. (2025) for Gaia BH forecasts, but with the intrinsic bi-nary population supplied by our Gaia DR3 NS model. Each NS-hosting star is assigned an observed sky po-sition, distance, proper motion, parallax, and appar-ent G magnitude using the simulated galaxy m12i from the FIRE-2 simulation suite (Wetzel et al. 2016; Hop-kins et al. 2018). 3D Galactic positions are assigned to stars based on the ananke framework of Sanderson et al. (2020), which generates mock catalogs of syn-thetic stars from cosmological baryonic simulations (see Chawla et al. 2022, for more details). Gaia NSs are then randomly assigned to star particles. Extinction is eval-uated with the combined19 dust map in mwdust , which combines the maps of Drimmel et al. (2003), Marshall et al. (2006), and Green et al. (2019) . The dark com-panion is assumed to contribute no optical light, so the simulated astrometric signal is the photocenter orbit of the luminous star. For each binary in the DR4 realization, we draw the orbital orientations and phases at random: cos i is uni-form, ω (argument of periastron), Ω (longitude of the ascending node), and the periastron time are uniform over their allowed ranges. gaiamock then uses the Gaia 

scanning law to generate epoch astrometry with the ap-propriate along-scan sampling and astrometric uncer-tainties, including the orbital displacement of the photo-center (Lindegren 2022; Holl et al. 2023; El-Badry et al. 2024a). The mock epoch astrometry is passed through the Gaia astrometric model cascade, which tests increas-ingly complex solutions and returns whether the source receives a single-star, acceleration, or orbital solution, following the logic of the DR3 non-single-star catalog construction (Halbwachs et al. 2023; El-Badry et al. 2024a). Hence the large 1 σ scatter in this region. Rather, within the mass range sampled by the observed candidates, the likelihood favors an intrinsic distribution that decreases toward higher MNS . In the fiducial Gaussian model, this decreasing trend is represented by a mean near the lower edge of the observed mass window. This result is driven by the fact that the Gaia selection function mildly favors higher-mass companions, yet the observed sample contains relatively few high-mass NS candidates. Thus, after correcting for selection, the intrinsic Gaia NS mass distribution is pushed toward lower masses. This preference persists when we restrict the analysis to the high-mass subset, MNS > 1.35 M ⊙, and when we use alternative mass parameterizations beyond a truncated Gaussian (Appendices C and B). Notably, the Gaia NS sample provides a rather prac-tical new population for measuring the NS mass dis-tribution. Most precise NS masses prior to Gaia were derived from pulsars in compact binaries or accreting X-ray binaries ( ¨Ozel & Freire 2016; Antoniadis et al. 2016). These measurements are generally precise but can be biased tracers of the birth mass due to prior ac-cretion (e.g., You et al. 2025). The NSs in wide Gaia NS binaries, on the other hand, are thought to have had no significant accretion onto the NS, making them a relatively pristine measure of the NS birth-mass dis-tribution. Gaia DR4 Forecasts 

Using the inferred model for the underlying Gaia NS population, we predict how many Gaia NS binaries will be discovered in Gaia DR4. Gaia DR3 was the first re-lease to publish non-single-star orbital solutions, but the current NS sample is shaped by the 34-month observ-ing baseline and astrometric-orbit quality cuts, many of which are relatively conservative (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023; Halbwachs et al. 2023). Gaia DR4 will be based on 66 months of data with improved photomet-ric and astrometric precision. Imperatively, Gaia DR4 will also include epoch astrometry, allowing for closer in-spection of the astrometric orbits. All these factors will improve sensitivity to longer periods, fainter sources, and larger distances. The subset with luminous stars brighter than G ≃ 14 mag will also have usable epoch radial velocities from Gaia RVS spectra (e.g., Katz et al. 2023; Sartoretti et al. 2023). The properties of Gaia NS binaries in DR4 will test major uncertainties left by DR3, including the behavior of the period distribution beyond Porb ∼ 1000 d and properties of the luminous companions. The long-period tail of Gaia NSs is par-ticularly important for testing binary evolution models and prescriptions for NS natal kicks. In this section, we forecast the yield of NS binaries in 

Gaia DR4. Because our DR3-calibrated model is con-strained only out to Porb = 1600 d, we extrapolate the period distribution to Porb = 7000 d to match the longer DR4 baseline. The long-period tail is currently uncon-strained, so we consider four simple models: one extends the DR3-inferred single power law over the full period range, while the other three keep the DR3-inferred slope for Porb ≤ 1600 d and adopt independent long-period slopes of β = 0, 0 .5, and 1 for 1600 < P orb /d ≤ 7000 (Equation 13). These choices span declining, flat, and rising long-period distributions. In all cases, the visible-star mass, NS mass, and eccentricity distributions are drawn from the DR3-inferred posterior (Figure 3). Although the long-period tail of Gaia NSs is presently unconstrained, other NS populations suggest that it is likely well-populated. Gaia NSs will eventually evolve into symbiotic X-ray binaries (SyXBs), once the present-day MS companion ascends the giant branch and the NS accretes from its wind (e.g., Yungelson et al. 2019). This X-ray-bright phase is short-lived compared to the detached NS lifetime, typically ∼ 10 6 yr, so only a hand-ful of SyXBs are known in the Galaxy (Bahramian & Degenaar 2023; Nagarajan et al. 2024). Curiously, the closest confirmed SyXB (4U 1700+24, d = 540 pc) has an orbital period Porb = 4391 d (Hinkle et al. 2019). Its progenitor would have been a detached NS–MS binary at a similarly wide, or somewhat shorter, orbit that is well beyond the DR3 detection limit but potentially ac-cessible to Gaia DR4. Hence, it stands to reason that long-period Gaia NSs exist and may even be abundant.

NEUTRON STAR MASSES NS masses probe the physics of core-collapse, and most precise NS mass measurements are derived from recycled pulsars or X-ray binaries, where accretion can increase the observed mass relative to its birth mass (Timmes et al. 1996; ¨Ozel & Freire 2016; Antoniadis et al. 2016; Alsing et al. 2018; You et al. 2025). Gaia NS candidates provide a complementary, non-accreting sample. Their masses are dynamically measured from joint Gaia astrometry+RVs, and their detached orbits imply little subsequent mass growth (El-Badry et al. 2024b). Thus, their present-day masses are close to their birth masses. In this section, we compare the intrinsic NS mass distribution in Gaia NSs to other NS popula-tions. Previous studies have found that NS mass distribu-tions differ across populations. Double neutron stars (DNSs) have masses concentrated near ≈ 1.3 M ⊙, while more strongly recycled NSs extend to higher masses, likely reflecting accretion after birth ( ¨Ozel et al. 2012; Kiziltan et al. 2013; Antoniadis et al. 2016; Alsing et al. 2018; Farrow et al. 2019). You et al. (2025) compile dynamical NS mass measurements from several binary populations and infer the corresponding birth-mass dis-tribution by accounting for the mass accreted by recy-cled pulsars. They infer a birth-mass distribution that begins at 1 .1 M ⊙, peaks near ≈ 1.27 M ⊙, and declines steeply toward higher masses 9. The decline towards higher masses, they emphasize, is steep but not necessar-ily a hard cutoff. Namely, several dynamically measured NSs have masses > 1.5 M ⊙ in systems where substantial accretion is unlikely, showing that the NS birth-mass distribution has a genuine high-mass tail (e.g., Barziv et al. 2001; Rawls et al. 2011; Martinez et al. 2015; Tau-ris et al. 2017). Schiebelbein-Zwack et al. (2026a) recently compared the original 21 observed Gaia NS masses to the first-born NS masses in 13 Galactic DNS systems. The recy-cled DNS component is chosen for comparison because, like the NSs in Gaia NS binaries, it usually formed first and underwent little subsequent accretion (e.g., Tauris et al. 2017; Farrow et al. 2019). Their study shows that the observed Gaia and recycled-DNS mass distribu-tions are similar, with a narrow component near 1 .3 M ⊙

and a broader high-mass tail (Schiebelbein-Zwack et al. 2026a). However, as mentioned in their work, the com-parison uses the observed Gaia NS sample without cor-recting for selection biases. Isolated radio pulsars lack pre-cise mass measurements, while binary-selected samples can be biased if NS mass, natal kick, and binary sur-vival are correlated (Burrows et al. 2024; Kaczmarek et al. 2026). Although individual microlensing masses will generally be less precise than pulsar-timing or joint astrometric–RV masses (Lam et al. 2022; Sahu et al. 2022; Mr´ oz et al. 2025), combining Gaia NSs, recycled DNSs, X-ray binaries, recycled pulsars, GW NSs, and isolated microlensing NSs—with separate treatment of selection effects and accretion histories—offers a promis-ing path toward cementing the NS birth-mass distribu-tion. For example, if higher-mass NSs receive stronger kicks and are therefore more likely to become isolated, the selection-corrected microlensing mass distribution 17 may be broader or shifted toward higher masses than the Gaia NS distribution.

A GLOBAL VIEW OF NEUTRON STAR POPULATIONS By carefully modeling the Gaia selection function and applying hierarchical Bayesian inference, we inferred the intrinsic demographics of Gaia NS binaries. We now place Gaia NSs in the broader context of NS forma-tion and binary evolution by comparing their present-day Galactic number, observable lifetime, and birth rate with those of other NS populations. Previous studies have compared birth rates among isolated NS populations (Keane & Kramer 2008) and between LMXBs and recycled pulsars (Kulkarni &Narayan 1988; Maccarone et al. 2022). Here, we ex-tend these comparisons across both isolated and binary NS populations, including the newly measured Gaia NS population. Gaia NSs are one of the cleanest NS pop-ulations for demographic inference, owing to the well-characterized Gaia selection function for astrometric or-bits (e.g., El-Badry et al. 2024a; Lam et al. 2025). The selection function for other NS classes can also be quan-tified. Young radio pulsars are selected by beamed spin-powered emission, magnetars by a short-lived ac-tive phase, X-ray binaries by accretion duty cycle, and LVK binary neutron stars by the LIGO sensitivity. For each population, we compare the total number of systems in the Galaxy ( NGal ), the lifetime of the rele-vant observable or evolutionary phase ( τ ), and the cor-responding formation rate ( R). These quantities are related by 

NGal ≈ R τ. (14) For each class, we adopt the two quantities that are best constrained by the literature and derive the third from Equation 14. This scaling assumes an approxi-mately steady formation rate over the relevant lifetime. For example, isolated NSs and radio pulsars have well-characterized birth rate R through the CCSNe rate and active lifetime τ , so NGal is derived. For the binary populations, NGal and τ are usually best constrained, so R is derived. We specifically try to avoid using the estimates from population synthesis models and instead rely mostly on empirical arguments. The adopted ranges are intentionally broad, since the selection functions and observable lifetimes differ substantially across isolated NSs, radio pulsars, magnetars, X-ray binaries, DNSs, and LVK mergers. We outline the assumptions and reasoning behind each estimate in Appendix D. We emphasize that most esti-mates should be interpreted as conservative, plausible ranges rather than precise demographic measurements. Summary 

The majority of NS progenitors (OB stars) are born in binary or higher-order systems (e.g., Sana et al. 2012; Moe & Di Stefano 2017; Offner et al. 2023) and experi-ence rich evolutionary histories. The outcomes of these complex evolutionary histories are observed today as a diverse population of NS-harboring systems throughout our Galaxy, and Figure 8 summarizes empirical con-straints on their birth rates, lifetimes, and sizes. These constraints serve as checkpoints that models of NS evo-lution can be anchored to, within uncertainties. Subsets of this global picture can be interpreted as continuous evolutionary sequences. Most NSs are born isolated, with a birth rate set by the Galactic CCSNe rate ( ∼ 1 per century). A fraction of NSs will instead remain bound to their companions, with HMXBs having the highest formation rate. As the HMXB evolves, only a small fraction of them will survive the second stage of binary interaction and the core collapse of the donor into a compact object. Those that survive this stage will become DNSs. The subset of DNSs that merge within a Hubble time connects this Galactic evolutionary se-quence to the LVK BNS merger rate. Wide low-mass companions are rarer, but Gaia NSs now provide the first intrinsic constraints on detached, post-supernova NS binaries in au-scale orbits. They trace the wide-surviving branch of extreme-mass-ratio binary evolution, while NS LMXBs and spider pulsars trace the compact branch that likely underwent sub-stantial orbital shrinkage during mass transfer. As Gaia NS companions evolve off the main sequence, these sys-tems should pass through a SyXB phase and eventually become wide PSR–WD binaries, setting a lower limit on the formation rate of long-period PSR–WD systems. The relative numbers and rates of Gaia NSs, SyXBs, NS LMXBs, spiders, and PSR–WD binaries therefore constrain how often extreme-q binaries survive at wide separations, shrink into compact interacting systems, or merge. 21 As new populations are discovered and the statistics on existing populations are refined, we anticipate sig-nificant updates to the rates and understanding of NS evolution. More uniform surveys with well-defined se-lection functions provide a promising avenue to con-strain intrinsic demographics, shrink the uncertainties in Figure 8, and quantify the connections between pop-ulations. As Gaia DR4 extends the census of au-scale and wider NS–MS binaries, uniform X-ray monitor-ing constrains the hidden population of faint and long-recurrence NS LMXBs, next-generation radio surveys increase the known pulsar sample by factors of several, and LVK observes more BNS/NSBH mergers, the loca-tions and uncertainties of the populations in Figure 8 will sharpen, and a global view on NS populations will become clearer.

CONCLUSIONS The third Gaia data release revealed a new class of neutron stars (NSs) in au-scale binaries with solar-type companions (El-Badry et al. 2024b,c, 2026). The dis-covery of these 27 Gaia NS candidates presents a chal-lenge for binary-evolution models, because it is unclear how these systems avoided merger or dramatic orbital shrinkage when the NS progenitors were red supergiants, and how they remained bound when the NSs formed. A promising path forward to constrain the formation history of Gaia NSs is to characterize their intrinsic population properties: how common are Gaia NSs, and what are the distributions of orbital periods, eccentric-ity, main-sequence masses, and neutron star masses? Fortunately, unlike previous samples of NSs with low-mass companions such as NS-LMXBs, the selection bi-ases of the Gaia NS population are well-characterized and mainly governed by Gaia ’s astrometric sensitivity (El-Badry et al. 2024a). On the other hand, contam-ination from ultramassive WDs or WD+WD binaries may exist and is difficult to characterize (see also Ap-pendix C). In this study, we carefully model the Gaia 

selection function to determine the intrinsic properties of the Gaia NS population, including their distribution of orbital periods, eccentricities, neutron star masses, companion masses, space densities, and birth rates. Us-ing these results, we then place the new class of Gaia NSs in the broader NS population, connecting them to isolated NSs, X-ray binaries, recycled pulsars, double neutron stars, and compact-object mergers. Our main results are summarized as follows: 1. Intrinsic properties: By modeling the Gaia DR3 selection function with gaiamock (El-Badry et al. 2024a) and applying a hierarchical Bayesian model, we infer the intrinsic properties of the Gaia NS population. We consider two classes of actively accreting NS bi-naries with high-mass companions: Be X-ray bina-ries (BeXRBs) and supergiant high-mass X-ray binaries (SgHMXBs). BeXRBs comprise about half of the known Galactic HMXB catalog. The Galactic HMXB catalog contains 74 Be XRBs among 152 known HMXBs (Fortin et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024), providing the strict lower limit for NGal . Because BeXRBs are transient, concen-trated near the Galactic plane, and require both X-ray detection and optical/infrared counterpart classification (Reig 2011), the total number in the Galaxy is expected to be larger. The Magellanic Clouds provide a useful an-chor on the total size of the BeXRB population because their distances are known, Galactic extinction is less se-vere, and they can be monitored in a single pointing, making their HMXB samples more complete. The Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) provides the best probe. Its SFR has been relatively steady for the past several Gyr with SFR=0 .2 M ⊙ yr −1 (Harris & Zarit-sky 2009), about 10 × smaller than the Milky Way (1 .5– 2 M ⊙ yr −1; Licquia & Newman 2015), with a slightly lower metallicity (0 .5× solar; Kaltenbrunner et al. 2026). Among the 40 classified LMC HMXBs from Antoniou & Zezas (2016), 33 (83%) are BeXRBs and 4 (10%) are su-27 pergiant systems, corresponding to 83% and 10% of the total HMXBs. Scaling the classified LMC BeXRB sam-ple to the Milky Way using the ratio of SFRs estimates 

∼ 300 active BeXRBs in the Milky Way, before cor-recting for LMC incompleteness. XMM-Newton obser-vations of the LMC also discovered new HMXBs, which are mainly Be systems (van Jaarsveld et al. 2018; Haberl et al. 2022). The all-LMC eROSITA catalog now lists 53 HMXB objects/candidates, including 28 confirmed HMXBs and 27 new eROSITA detections with complete-ness for sources brighter than LX ∼ few × 10 34 erg s −1

(Kaltenbrunner et al. 2026). Quiescent BeXRBs, low-duty-cycle transients, and optically ambiguous systems are still missing and not considered in scaling the LMC values to the Milky Way. The SMC provides another reference point, but is likely more difficult to scale directly to the Milky Way. Namely, its metallicity is lower, and the HMXB pop-ulation is much larger per unit mass than the Milky Way: Haberl & Sturm (2016) compile 148 confirmed and candidate SMC HMXBs and identify 121 relatively high-confidence systems, the vast majority with Be com-panions. Since the Milky Way SFR is roughly 30 – 50 times larger than the SMC’s, a direct SMC-to-Milky Way scaling would imply several thousand BeXRBs or more. However, it has been noted that the SMC is unusually efficient at producing BeXRBs, probably be-cause of its recent star-formation history ∼ 50 Myr ago and low metallicity (Antoniou et al. 2010; Antoniou & Zezas 2016; Vinciguerra et al. 2020). Symbiotic X-ray binaries (SyXBs) are binaries where a NS accretes from the wind of an evolved red-giant companion. While they comprise only a small observed sample, they provide a spotlight on the fate of the Gaia NSs. Only a handful of SyXBs are known (e.g., Yun-gelson et al. 2019; Bahramian & Degenaar 2023), and Nagarajan et al. (2024) list six systems with secure or candidate SyXB classifications. This observed number is a lower limit because SyXBs are often wind-fed and X-ray faint compared to Roche-lobe-overflow LMXBs, and often lack measured orbital solutions due to their long periods (Hinkle et al. 2006, 2019, 2024). We estimate the present-day Galactic SyXB popula-tion in two ways. First, using the nearby SyXBs com-piled by Nagarajan et al. (2024), we find that there are three known systems within 3 kpc, with the clos-est one at d ∼ 0.5 kpc. These two estimates imply local space densities of n = 0 .027 kpc −3 (d ≤ 3 kpc) and n = 2 .7 kpc −3 (d ≤ 0.5 kpc), corresponding to 

NGal ∼ 8 – 600 after multiplying by the thin-disk ef-fective volume. Second, Nagarajan et al. (2024) argue that IGR J16194–2810, at d ≈ 2.1 kpc and Porb = 192 .7d, is probably the nearest SyXB with Porb ≲ 1000 d, i.e., the subset whose orbits should be detectable by 

Gaia DR3. Comparing this distance to the distance of the closest Gaia NS, d ∼ 250 pc, implies a local SyXB density roughly ∼ 100 times lower than the detached Gaia NS population, which also roughly reflects the ra-tio of their lifetimes. Since we infer n ≈ 120 kpc −3 for the Gaia NS–MS binaries (Figure 4), this scaling gives 

nSyXB ∼ 1 kpc −3, or NGal ∼ 3 × 10 2. This lies within the range from the direct nearby-SyXB estimate, so we adopt NGal ∼ 3 × 10 2 with a broad range 8 – 600. The observable lifetime for SyXBs is relatively short compared to Gaia NSs. Nagarajan et al. (2024) use MESA models for IGR J16194–2810 and find that the red giant will begin Roche-lobe overflow in 5 – 10 Myr. 28 Their fiducial model enters RLO in 5 .9 Myr and remains in the high mass-transfer phase for ≈ 3.6 Myr before leaving behind a ≈ 0.4 M ⊙ He WD in a ∼ 900 d or-bit. We therefore adopt an X-ray-bright SyXB lifetime of order τ ∼ 1 – 50 Myr to be conservative and allow for a broad range in orbital period, donor evolutionary state, and X-ray duty cycle. Low-mass X-ray binaries 

Neutron star low-mass X-ray binaries (NS LMXBs) comprise the majority of LMXBs, with a few hundred systems observed in the Galaxy (e.g., Bahramian & De-genaar 2023). The recent Fortin et al. (2024) LMXB catalog lists 339 Galactic LMXBs and candidates, while XRBcats contains 349 systems, including black-hole sys-tems and candidates (Avakyan et al. 2023). Of the XR-Bcats sources, 179 have secure NS identifications from thermonuclear bursts or coherent X-ray pulsations, and nine more are classified as NS systems from their X-ray spectra (Niang et al. 2024). Thus, the observed NS-LMXB sample is already ≳ 200 systems, with new LMXB transients continuing to be discovered regularly (Fortin et al. 2024) and additional unclassified LMXBs likely containing NS accretors. This provides the lower limit for NGal .This lower limit underestimates the intrinsic popula-tion. Many NS-LMXBs are transient and spend most of their time in quiescence, where they would be missed by all-sky X-ray monitoring (e.g., Bahramian & Degenaar 2023; Heinke et al. 2025, and references therein). Fur-thermore, Maccarone et al. (2022) argue that the unseen NS-LMXB population may be much larger than the cat-alogued sample. First, they argue that about half of the known transient NS-LMXBs with secure NS accretors have recurrence times longer than a decade for outbursts detectable by all-sky monitors such as MAXI. Second, the Galactic millisecond-pulsar (MSP) population gives an independent birth rate constraint. Recycled MSPs are expected to descend from NS-LMXBs (or, less fre-quently, SyXBs) and live for over a Hubble time. The total Galactic MSP population is about 40 ,000 (Lorimer 2008, 2013) with a typical age of ≈ 5×10 9 yr. Thus, it is expected that NNS −LMXB ≈ 40 ,000( τLMXB /5 × 10 9 yr). For τLMXB ≳ 10 8 yr, this implies ≳ 800 NS-LMXBs, while NNS −LMXB ∼ 200 would require an X-ray-bright lifetime of only ∼ 2 × 10 7 yr (Lorimer 2008; Podsiad-lowski et al. 2002; Maccarone et al. 2022). These es-timates suggest that the cataloged NS-LMXB sample likely significantly underrepresents the total Galactic NS-LMXB population. The arguments of Maccarone et al. (2022) suggest that there should be ∼ 20 – 100 undiscovered NS LMXBs for every one that is known. Given these large uncertainties, we adopt an intrin-sic present-day NS-LMXB population of NGal = 300 – 30 ,000, where the lower limit is the size of the known sample and the upper limit is the upper range from Maccarone et al. (2022) to account for the abundance of MSPs. For a mass-transfer lifetime of order τ = 5 × 10 8

– 10 10 yr, this gives a broad birth-rate range of R =3 × 10 −8 – 6 × 10 −5 yr −1. For NS-LMXBs, we thus use X-ray-active lifetime τ and estimated intrinsic number 

NGal as inputs to derive R. The NGal of NS-LMXBs is arguably the least constrained parameter among the NS populations discussed here, owing to uncertainties in recurrence timescales and quiescence properties of the X-ray-selected population. The LMXB estimate used here includes known ultra-compact X-ray binaries (UCXBs), but we do not esti-mate their population separately. Population-synthesis calculations predict a large, mostly X-ray-faint UCXB population whose formation rate may exceed that of hydrogen-rich LMXBs (van Haaften et al. 2013, 2015). Their uncertain lifetimes and distinct formation chan-nels prevent a comparably empirical rate estimate, and most proposed UCXB channels do not pass through the detached, au-scale Gaia NS phase. D.6. Spider pulsars 

Spider pulsars are compact binary millisecond pulsars (MSPs) where the recycled NS irradiates and ablates a low-mass companion (Fruchter et al. 1988; Roberts 2013; Blanchard et al. 2025). They are usually sepa-rated into two classes by companion mass: (1) black widows, with very low-mass ( Mc ≲ 0.05 – 0 .1 M ⊙), often degenerate brown dwarf or white dwarf compan-ions, and (2) redbacks, with more massive, often non-degenerate companions ( Mc ∼ 0.1 – 0 .5 M ⊙; Roberts 2013; Ginzburg & Quataert 2021; Koljonen & Linares 2025). SpiderCat lists 111 Galactic-field spiders and candidates: 50 confirmed black widows, 30 confirmed redbacks, five black-widow candidates, 23 redback can-didates, and three rarer systems/candidates (Koljonen & Linares 2025). This observed count is a lower limit of 

NGal because radio eclipses, beaming, optical follow-up, and Galactic-plane selection all bias the observations. A significant fraction of the known spider sample resides in globular clusters 13 , which we choose to include in our

The outcome depends also on the orbital period at the onset of mass transfer, where a bifurcation near ∼ 10–20 hr based on the evolutionary state of the donor separates shortening systems that can form spi-ders or compact PSR–WD binaries from widening sys-tems that form wider PSR–WD binaries (Podsiadlowski et al. 2002; Istrate et al. 2014; Bellm et al. 2016). Comparing their current observed rates provides ad-ditional support that the observed NS-LMXB sample likely underestimates the underlying parent population (Kulkarni & Narayan 1988). D.7. Recycled pulsar–WD binaries 

White dwarfs (WDs) are the most commonly observed companions to MSPs (e.g., Kilic et al. 2018). These bi-naries are the end products of mass transfer onto a NS accretor: LMXB progenitors with sufficiently evolved low-mass donors generally leave He-WD companions af-ter they are stripped, while intermediate-mass X-ray bi-nary progenitors can leave more massive CO or ONeMg WDs (Camilo et al. 1996; Bhattacharya & van den Heuvel 1991; Rappaport et al. 1995; Tauris et al. 2000; Podsiadlowski et al. 2002; Lorimer 2008; Tauris et al. 2012). He-WD companions dominate the binary MSP population, with typical companion masses MWD ≈ 0.15 – 0 .45 M ⊙ and final orbital periods from < 1 day to 

∼ 100 days, following the standard MWD – Porb rela-tion (Rappaport et al. 1995). The detached Gaia NS binaries provide a related, but probably subdominant, route to forming PSR–WD bi-naries. Chattopadhyay et al. (2026) evolve the Gaia NS binaries and find that all terminate as wide NS–WD systems through stable mass transfer. The degree of recycling depends on the treatment of mass transfer: models that assume circularization before Roche-lobe overflow produce MSPs, while models that allow eccen-tric, periastron-concentrated mass transfer produce only mildly recycled pulsars (Chattopadhyay et al. 2026). All of their final PSR–WD binaries have Porb ≳ 200 days. Most of the ∼ 200 known MSP–WD binaries outside globular clusters occupy shorter-period, nearly circular orbits, with Porb < 100 days (Bhattacharya & van den Heuvel 1991; Kiziltan & Thorsett 2010; Chattopadhyay et al. 2026), although a small long-period tail of mildly recycled PSR–WD systems is known at Porb ∼ 500 – 1000 d and likely descended from Gaia NSs (e.g., Sto-vall et al. 2014; Lorimer & Freire 2005; Lorimer et al. 2006; Knispel et al. 2013; Gonzalez et al. 2011; Martinez et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2026; Tan et al. 2026). These results suggest that Gaia NSs are likely asubdominant formation pathway for the full Galactic PSR–WD population. The dominant channel is more likely the standard recycled-pulsar pathway through NS LMXBs or IMXBs with evolved donors, which natu-rally produces MSPs with He-, CO-, or ONeMg-WD companions over the observed range of compact binary periods (Bhattacharya & van den Heuvel 1991; Rappa-port et al. 1995; Podsiadlowski et al. 2002; Tauris et al. 2012; Istrate et al. 2014). Additional compact MSP–WD systems may also descend from Gaia NSs with some-what more massive MS donors that undergo common-envelope evolution with the NS and shrink, as inferred for Cyg X-2-like systems (Podsiadlowski & Rappaport 2000; Podsiadlowski et al. 2002; Tauris et al. 2012; Chat-topadhyay et al. 2026). The observed PSR–WD sample is radio-selected and therefore incomplete because of beaming, flux lim-its, sky coverage, interstellar dispersion, and orbital-acceleration smearing in short-period binaries (Ransom et al. 2003). We therefore estimate the intrinsic num-ber by scaling from the completeness-corrected Galactic MSP population. The Galactic double neutron star (DNS) population provides one of the best empirical constraints on the for-mation of compact NS binaries. Most previous work has focused on the subset that will merge within a Hubble time, because those systems provide an observational anchor for binary neutron-star merger rates from grav-itational wave detectors. Here, however, we are inter-ested in estimating the present-day Galactic DNS pop-ulation as a whole, including wide systems with merger times longer than the age of the Universe. Galactic DNSs are identified through radio pulsar tim-ing, so the observed sample is strongly affected by radio-selection effects, including beaming, luminosity limits, sky coverage, interstellar dispersion and scattering, and reduced sensitivity to highly accelerated compact bi-naries (Ransom et al. 2003; Lorimer 2008). The to-tal DNS population is therefore less directly measured than the DNS merger rate. Nevertheless, the nearby radio sample gives a useful observational lower limit. Several long-lived DNSs are known within ∼ 1 kpc: PSR J1518+4904 has Porb = 8 .63 d and d = 625 pc, PSR J0453+1559 has Porb = 4 .07 d and a gravitational-wave merger time 1 .43 × 10 12 yr, and PSR J1411+2551 and PSR J1829+2456 have Porb = 2 .62 and 1 .18 d at distances of ∼ 1 kpc (Janssen et al. 2008; Martinez et al. 2015, 2017; Haniewicz et al. 2021; Ding et al. 2024). Four such systems within ∼ 1.1 kpc imply an observed local density ∼ 0.7 kpc −3, before correcting for radio selection effects.
