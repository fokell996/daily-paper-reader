If strange quark matter (SQM) is absolutely stable, hadronic neutron stars (NSs) and strange quark stars (QSs) may coexist in the so-called two-families scenario. A key issue is explaining how hadronic NSs can survive as long-lived metastable objects, rather than promptly converting into stable QSs. Since all NSs are born as hot proto-neutron stars (PNSs), a necessary condition for their existence is that the conditions for conversion, triggered by the nucleation of the first critical SQM droplet, are not reached in every PNS. We investigate the thermal nucleation of critical SQM droplets under representative PNS conditions, accounting for flavor-composition fluctuations and finite-size effects on color superconductivity by suppressing pairing in droplets smaller than the diquark coherence length. We define the nucleation conditions as the thermodynamic state at which a critical SQM droplet is expected to nucleate statistically within the characteristic dynamical timescale of the system. Under these conditions, the reduced nucleation barrier is nearly universal, with W∗/T ≃ 167 to within a few units. Exploring the SQM parameter space, we show that requiring a canonical 1 .4 M ⊙ NS to survive the PNS stage sets a lower bound on the hadron–quark surface tension σ ≳ 67 MeV fm −2.

I. INTRODUCTION 

The Bodmer–Witten hypothesis (BW) posits that bulk three-flavor strange quark matter (SQM), composed of up, down, and strange quarks in weak equilibrium, is absolutely stable: at zero pressure and temperature its energy per baryon would lie below that of the most stable nucleus, ( E/N B )|SQM < (E/N B )|56 Fe ≃ 930 MeV [1, 2]. The consequences of such a hypothesis are far-reaching and would provide signatures in astrophysics and cosmol-ogy [3, 4] (see [5] for a recent reappraisal). The BW hypothesis does not conflict with the exis-tence of ordinary nuclei. Converting a nucleus into SQM would require the near-simultaneous weak conversion of a large number of light quarks into strange quarks, an extremely high-order process with negligible rate. More-over, small strangelets are energetically penalized by finite-size effects, so that there exists a minimum baryon number below which SQM lumps are unstable [4, 6]. The path in configuration space is thus not downhill, and or-dinary hadronic matter is metastable : it sits in a local minimum of the free energy, is protected from the true SQM ground state by an energy barrier, and has a life-time far exceeding the age of the Universe. The same reasoning, applied to compact stars, is far less restrictive. If SQM is absolutely stable, every neu-tron star (NS) is in principle metastable against conver-sion to a strange quark star (QS) [3]. Two outcomes are then possible: either the conversion conditions are gener-ically met during stellar evolution, so that all compact stars are QSs, or they are met only in part of the popula-tion, so that NSs and QSs coexist as assumed within the

so-called two-families scenario [7, 8]. The all-QS option is disfavored because some observed phenomenology, such as glitches [9–12] and quasi-periodic oscillations [13], is naturally accounted for by the nuclear crust of NSs, and not by QSs, which are either bare or endowed with a crystalline color-superconducting crust whose properties differ from those of an ordinary nuclear crust [14–16]. It is therefore more plausible that at least a fraction of com-pact stars are hadronic objects, as realized in the two-families scenario. In this respect, the two-families sce-nario shares some similarities with a well-known fact in nuclear astrophysics: not all main-sequence stars are able to synthesize iron (the most stable nucleus) in their cores, but only those which are massive enough. Stars with different internal compositions therefore coexist. Also, the idea of metastability is not at all new in nuclear as-trophysics: most of the Universe is made of hydrogen and only under specific conditions (which are met inside stars) hydrogen can undergo fusion reactions leading to the formation of more stable nuclei. The scenario of coexistence is further motivated by the tension between the stiffness requirements that differ-ent observations impose on the equation of state (EOS). On the one hand, the existence of very massive com-pact stars [17–21] calls for a stiff EOS. On the other hand, the appearance of hyperons and ∆ isobars softens the EOS [22], and several observations, such as heavy-ion flow data [23], the tidal deformability inferred from GW170817 [24], and the small radii measured at ordinary compact-star masses ( ∼ 1.4 M ⊙) [25, 26], point instead to a soft EOS. In the two-families scenario this tension is resolved by decoupling the two regimes: the soft hadronic branch accounts for the canonical-mass small-radius ob-jects, while the stiff SQM branch accounts for the most massive ones. This picture has recently received quanti-arXiv:2610.00699v1 [nucl-th] 30 Sep 2026 2tative support from the first Bayesian analysis of the two-families scenario against astrophysical data [27]. The sce-nario also offers an interpretation of subsolar-mass com-pact objects as QSs 1, such as the compact object associ-ated with HESS J1731–347 [30, 31] or the sources of the candidate subsolar-mass gravitational-wave events [32– 34]; see [35] for the first numerical simulation of subsolar-mass QS–QS mergers. A central open question for the two-families scenario then concerns the metastability itself: can NSs persist as long-lived metastable objects without decaying into QSs, and how is the QS branch actually populated? An-swering this question requires identifying the thermody-namic conditions under which a hadronic object converts into a QS, and the astrophysical systems in which those conditions are naturally realized. Specifically, the very existence of the two families demands that during their lifetime at least some compact stars survive as long-lived metastable NSs while others convert into QSs. Assuming that deconfinement at high density is a first-order phase transition, a nonzero hadron–quark surface tension σ separates the two phases and gives rise to a free-energy barrier. As a consequence, the system can remain trapped in the metastable local minimum even when a more stable phase exists, instead of decaying into it instantaneously. The decay occurs only when a spon-taneous thermal or quantum fluctuation creates a droplet of the stable phase which is large enough to overcome the free-energy barrier, i.e. large enough to keep expanding: this is the process of nucleation 2 [37–39]. Once a critical droplet is formed, the conversion proceeds through subse-quent stages: the droplet grows, individual bubbles grow and coalesce 3, until reaching a macroscopic size, after which a combustion front is formed, eventually convert-ing the entire star [43–48]. In many studies of the two-families scenario, the nucle-ation conditions are identified through a simple geomet-ric criterion [27, 49, 50]: the conversion is assumed to be triggered once the mean distance between strange quarks in the hadronic phase becomes comparable to the aver-age inter-nucleon distance in nuclear matter, i.e. once the strange quarks are close enough to mutually interact and

Previous literature has explored various aspects of nucleation in astrophysics, employing a range of un-derlying EOSs, environmental conditions (such as zero or finite temperature, and the presence or absence of trapped neutrinos), and assumptions about the compo-sition of the nucleating droplet, which is determined by the interplay between deconfinement and weak reaction timescales [37, 39, 51–65]. Building on this, it was re-cently examined how thermal fluctuations of the con-served charges (i.e., flavor composition) impact the nu-cleation of two-flavor quark matter [66]. The formalism developed there has been extended to the case of three-flavor matter [67]. Another key microphysical ingredient one has to con-sider in this context is related to the phenomenon of color superconductivity. At asymptotically large baryon density the color-flavor-locked (CFL) phase is the ener-getically favored ground state of three-flavor quark mat-ter [68]; its relevance at the densities reached in compact stars is less certain, but since pairing lowers the energy of quark matter, a color-superconducting phase may be a good candidate for being the absolutely stable SQM within the BW hypothesis. This raises a problem spe-cific to nucleation: the diquark coherence length [68] is comparable to, or larger than, the size of the earliest sub-critical droplets, so that the smallest seeds cannot sus-tain a fully developed condensate and are predominantly unpaired. The color-superconducting phase can develop only once the droplets are large enough to allow the for-mation of diquark pairs [68, 69]. While past literature has included color-superconducting EOSs to evaluate nucle-ation rates [70–72], the impact of incomplete diquark-pair formation in small systems has generally been neglected. Here, we account for this phenomenon through a simple scheme in which the thermodynamics of the quark phase depends on the droplet radius. The aim of this work is to assess whether SQM nucle-ation conditions are reached during the early evolution of a hadronic proto-neutron star (PNS), producing QSs. Specifically, we compute the nucleation rate under typical PNS conditions and investigate whether, for physically motivated values of the model parameters, the result-ing timescales prevent nucleation in a fraction of PNSs, which then can survive as metastable NSs once cooled. Since all NSs are born as PNSs, their existence requires that not all PNSs convert into QSs. However, note that QSs may also form through other channels, such as in bi-nary compact-star mergers (BCSMs) [49], during a core-collapse supernova (CCSN), or later during accretion or spin-down of a cold NS [50]. The absence of nucleation in at least some hadronic PNSs is therefore a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for the two-families scenario. The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. > II. EQUATIONS OF STATE

A microscopic QCD description of strongly interacting matter across the relevant range of densities, tempera-tures, and conserved-charge content is not yet available, as QCD remains intractable at finite baryon chemical po-tential. We therefore adopt the standard strategy of de-scribing the confined hadronic phase and the deconfined quark phase using two separate effective models. It is worth emphasizing that, in scenarios where SQM is not absolutely stable, compact stars with and without a deconfined quark matter core are governed by the same equilibrium EOS. Under the BW hypothesis, however, the hadronic and the SQM branches are described by two distinct EOSs, corresponding to a metastable and an absolutely stable solution of QCD, respectively. In this section, we summarize the characteristics of the EOS models employed in this work; see [73] and refer-ences therein for a more complete description. Electrons, positrons, electron neutrinos, and antineu-trinos are treated as non-interacting fermions with masses me = 0 .511 MeV and mν = 0 MeV, and degen-eracies of 2 and 1, respectively. We also include thermal photons, treated as an ideal boson gas with a degeneracy of 2.  

> A. Hadronic EOS

The hadronic phase is described by the relativistic mean-field (RMF) model SFHo-H∆, in which baryons interact through the exchange of the scalar σ and vec-tor ω, ϕ, ρ mesons. It is built on the SFHo parametriza-tion [74], extended to the full baryon octet [75] and the ∆-isobar quartet [76], and has been widely employed in the two-families scenario [8, 49, 50, 77]. The nucleonic sector follows [74]; the hyperon cou-plings are fixed by SU(6) relations for the vector mesons [75] and by fitting the scalar couplings to the em-pirical potential depths UΛ = −28 MeV, UΣ = +30 MeV, and UΞ = −18 MeV [78, 79]. The ∆ couplings are fixed with xσ∆ = gσ∆/g σN = 1 .15 and xω∆ = xρ∆ = 1 .0. Light pseudoscalar mesons are added as a thermal ideal Bose gas with effective chemical potentials tied to the same mean fields [80, 81]. The EOS model presented here is employed for the nuclear and supra-nuclear regimes in NSs and hadronic PNSs. > III. EVOLUTION OF PROTO-NEUTRON STARS

Hadronic PNSs are one of the possible remnants of CC-SNe [88]. When stars with initial masses above ∼ 8 M ⊙

exhaust their nuclear fuel, their cores can no longer be supported against gravity and begin to collapse. The col-lapse proceeds until the core baryon density approaches nuclear saturation, nB ≃ nsat ≃ 0.16 fm −3, where the repulsive component of the nuclear force together with neutron degeneracy pressure halts the collapse, causing the core to bounce. Immediately after bounce, the hadronic PNS is formed. It is characterized by a relatively low mass ∼ (0 .5–1) M ⊙,a high lepton fraction YL ≃ 0.35–0 .4, and an entropy per baryon of S/N B ≃ 1–1 .5 (with precise values depending on the progenitor, on the EOS, and on weak-interaction microphysics) [85, 88–90]. Over the subsequent ∼ (0 .5– 1) s, the PNS accretes matter from the overlying layers. The amount of accreted mass depends on the progenitor star characteristics (mainly its mass). If accretion is modest (e.g., for progenitors ≲ 20 M ⊙), a neutrino-driven CCSN explosion happens, leaving a hadronic PNS remnant. For more massive progenitors, sustained accretion can be large enough that conditions for quark deconfinement may already be met during this early phase. Indeed, deconfinement has been proposed as a mechanism that can trigger CCSN explosions in cases where the standard mechanism fails [89, 90]. This pos-sibility, in the context of the two-families scenario, will be investigated in a forthcoming paper. For even more massive progenitors, continued accretion can drive the PNS above its stability limit, leading to black hole (BH) formation. In the present work we focus on the scenario in which a hadronic PNS survives these initial stages and becomes the remnant of a successful CCSN. After explosion, the newly formed hadronic PNS core (typically S/N B ≃ 1–1 .5 and YL ≃ 0.35–0 .4) is no longer strongly accreted, so its baryonic mass MB can be taken as approximately constant for the subsequent evolution. Within ∼ 10 s, the core entropy per baryon commonly rises toward S/N B ≃ 1.5–2, while the lepton fraction decreases to YL ≃ 0.25–0 .3 owing to neutrino diffusion (deleptonization). Finally, the PNS cools by neutrino emission, eventually reaching the cold neutrinoless β-equilibrium NS configuration after a few tens of seconds. The specific values of S/N B and YL depend on the PNS baryonic mass (hence on progenitor properties), on the EOS and on the neutrino-transport treatment adopted [91–96].  

> IV. CONVERSION INTO A STRANGE QUARK STAR

The conversion of a hadronic PNS into a QS can be schematically divided into several steps [97]: (i) Nucle-ation : a droplet of SQM large enough to keep expanding is formed; (ii) the first seed of SQM expands further or merges with other droplets until reaching a macroscopic size; (iii) the subsequent propagation of the conversion front can be described hydrodynamically and proceeds in two phases: an initial rapid burning (lasting a few milliseconds), whose velocity is greatly enhanced by hy-drodynamical instabilities, which converts the central re-gion of the star [44, 46, 47]; then a slower burning regime (lasting tens of seconds), driven by the production and diffusion of strangeness, converts the outer stellar lay-ers [43, 46, 48]. In this work, we focus on identifying the thermo-dynamic conditions required for conversion to begin, namely under which conditions nucleation occurs, and on comparing them with the typical thermodynamic condi-tions of a PNS.  

> A. Nucleation

If a phase transition is of first order, a surface tension exists between the two phases. From a thermodynamic point of view, the surface tension is the extra energy per unit area required to create an interface between the two phases, representing the cost of forming a finite-size vol-ume of the new phase rather than an infinite bulk con-figuration, since particles located at the boundary of a finite volume interact differently from those deep in the interior. In general, nucleation theory aims at computing the rate at which a critical volume of a new phase Q5 forms inside a system initially in a metastable phase H, thereby triggering the decay of the metastable state through a first-order phase transition.

> 1. Fluctuations

Besides VQ, the other thermodynamic variables can also fluctuate spontaneously [100]. It is therefore cru-cial to identify which configurations of the system can be explored. In the context of quark nucleation, the spontaneous fluctuations that generate quark droplets through deconfinement are mediated by the strong in-teraction and occur on the typical timescale τstrong ∼

10 −23 s. Because the weak interaction acts on much longer timescales, it cannot play a role during nucleation. As a consequence, only configurations with the same fla-vor content as the initial state, or equivalently the same baryon number NB = 13 Nu + 13 Nd + 13 Ns, non-leptonic electric charge NC = 23 Nu − 13 Nd − 13 Ns, and strangeness 

NS = Ns, can be explored during nucleation. While this consideration is generally accepted in the literature, it is not fully clear whether flavor conservation should be enforced locally or globally (see [67, 73] for a discussion). In several works (see e.g. [37, 39, 64]), the critical droplet of deconfined quark matter is assumed to be in an out-of-chemical-equilibrium state with the same flavor composition as the bulk hadronic phase ( Y Qi = Y Hi

for i = u, d, s , or equivalently i = B, C, S ). At finite temperature, however, the flavor composition fluctuates around its bulk average. Since nucleation is a local pro-cess, the first critical droplet could form within a sub-system of the hadronic medium where the local compo-sition differs from the average and is more favorable for nucleation. A complete treatment should take this into account, as suggested in [54, 101, 102]. In this work, we adopt the framework introduced in [66, 67, 73], which explicitly incorporates thermal fluctuations of the com-position. As discussed above, if the initial hadronic phase is metastable, a fluctuation in VQ can lower the energy of the system provided that VQ is large enough. Conversely, if the initial system is already in chemical and thermal equilibrium with respect to the interaction governing nu-cleation, a fluctuation in any other variable always in-creases the total energy. Therefore, the most favorable critical configuration is a saddle point, where the energy is a maximum along the VQ axis and a minimum along all other directions [38, 103].

> 5. Nucleation in a proto-neutron star

In this work we do not have access to an astrophysi-cal simulation providing the input ( nHB , {Y Hi }, T ). Since the evolution of a hadronic PNS is slower than the weak-equilibration timescale, we can assume that the hadronic PNS is in chemical equilibrium, so that ( nHB , {Y Hi }, T ) →

(nHB , Y HL , T ). Our strategy is to compute the thermody-namic conditions ( nHB , Y HL , T ) under which a nucleation event is statistically expected to occur in a hadronic PNS before these conditions change, i.e. when the nucleation time τ of Eq. (24) is shorter than the dynamical timescale of the system, τdyn ≃ 10 −3 s. In principle, one should compute the local nucleation rate throughout the entire PNS and then integrate over the volume. However, since the nucleation rate depends exponentially on W∗/T , the integral is dominated by the region where W∗/T lies close to its minimum, i.e. the stellar center. For this reason, we evaluate Γ at the cen-tral conditions and adopt as reference volume a sphere of radius 100 m, i.e. V PNS center = 43 π(100 m) 3 ≃ 4 × 10 51 fm 3,as in [63], and comparable to the cell size of astrophysi-cal simulations (see Sec. > B. Remnant and energy released

Once the nucleation conditions are reached in ahadronic PNS, the seed grows and a conversion front propagates outward, transforming hadronic matter into SQM. The remnant is a hot QS that subsequently cools and deleptonizes via intense neutrino emission un-til it settles into a hydrostatic configuration on the QS branch [44, 46, 97]. During the conversion, both strong and weak pro-cesses in the front conserve the total baryon number, while neutrinos and gravitational waves carry away en-ergy and lepton number but not baryons. > VI. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

The aim of this work was to establish whether a re-gion of the SQM parameter space exists in which NSs and QSs can coexist, as required by the two-families sce-nario, built on the BW hypothesis. The issue that mo-tivates the question is that, if SQM is absolutely stable, a hadronic NS is a metastable stellar object, and one has to explain how it can survive for astronomical times without converting into a QS. Since every compact star begins its life as a hot, lepton-rich PNS, and since the conditions reached at that stage are more favorable to deconfinement than those of a cold star, the existence of NSs requires that at least part of the PNS population complete its cooling and deleptonization without nucle-ating SQM. We have therefore computed the thermal nucleation of a critical SQM droplet under the conditions of two repre-sentative snapshots of PNS evolution, taken at the time of the CCSN explosion and at the epoch of maximum central temperature. Two ingredients distinguish the present calculation from the standard treatment. First, the composition of the droplet is not frozen to that of the surrounding average bulk hadronic matter: it is fixed by a saddle-point condition on the work of formation, which is equivalent to strong chemical equilibrium across the interface and which can be realized either by a prior local fluctuation of the hadronic composition or by the exchange of flavor across the droplet surface. Second, color superconductivity is treated at finite droplet size: because diquark pairs cannot form in a region smaller than their coherence length R∆ = 1 /∆, the droplet is in the unpaired phase below R∆ and in the CFL phase above it. Since the nucleation time varies very fast with the baryon density and temperature, it is meaningful to speak of nucleation conditions , defined as the thermody-namic states at which τ equals the dynamical timescale 

τdyn = 1 ms, and the precise value adopted for the ref-erence timescale turns out to be immaterial: varying it over five orders of magnitude, up to 100 s, an upper limit for the duration of the hot PNS phase, shifts the thresh-old surface tension negligibly with respect to the other uncertainties of the problem. The same holds for the nucleation volume: integrating the rate over the whole star rather than over a central sphere of radius 100 m raises σcrit by only ≃ 2 MeV fm −2 (Sec. V E). Comparing the nucleation conditions with the ones actually reached by PNSs identifies the epoch of maximum central tem-perature tTmax as the bottleneck for the fate of the star: a PNS that has not nucleated by then will cool into a NS. This is what allows the critical surface tension σcrit 

to be defined as the value of σ above which the nucle-ation condition is no longer met at tTmax inside a PNS whose baryonic mass equals that of a cold 1 .4 M ⊙ NS: for σ < σ crit that PNS nucleates, and no 1 .4 M ⊙ NS can exist. The coexistence of the two families requires a hadron– quark surface tension of at least σ ≃ 67 MeV fm −2: be-low that value, every PNS heavy enough to become an ordinary 1 .4 M ⊙ NS converts. This bound depends only marginally on the choice of the reference mass ( ≃ 66– 67 MeV fm −2 between 1 .17 and 1 .6 M ⊙). This minimum is attained by the softest SQM parametrizations still compatible with 2 M ⊙ pulsars, thus it tightens whenever a heavier object is identified with a QS: to ≃ 81, 94 and 110 MeV fm −2 if the QS branch is required to reach 2 .18, 2.35 and 2 .6 M ⊙, the first two being the −1σ and the central mass of PSR J0952 −0607 [17] and the third being the mass of the secondary component of GW190814 [21]. These are lower bounds and not preferred values; they are large compared with the smallest surface tensions consid-ered in the literature, but well inside the range spanned by microscopic estimates. We stress that σcrit is not fixed by the maximum mass of the QS branch alone: along a line of constant M QS max it varies by more than a factor of two, so that two parametrizations indistinguishable by their maximum mass can demand very different surface tensions. The finite-size treatment of pairing has a qualitative impact under some conditions. Depending on density and temperature, the critical droplet is fully unpaired, gap-locked at R∗ = R∆, or fully CFL, and neglecting the geometric suppression of pairing changes the nucleation time by many orders of magnitude and the way the bar-rier responds to the surface tension. Its effect on σcrit is confined to an intermediate gap band of the parameter space, with an impact of at most ≃ 21 MeV fm −2.Two further numbers are worth carrying over. At the nucleation conditions the reduced barrier is nearly uni-versal, W∗/T ≃ 167+ln[( τ / 1 ms)( V /V PNS center )] [Eq. (30)] to within a few units, so that a local thermodynamic state, for instance a simulation cell of volume V over a time step τ , can be approximately tested for deconfinement without computing the rate. Moreover, when conversion does occur it releases several ×10 53 erg, depending on the SQM parametrization and on the mass of the con-verting star. This energy reservoir could power a delayed transient after a CCSN. Several limitations should be kept in mind, and each of them defines a natural direction for future work. First, we have treated σ as a constant free parameter and neglected the curvature term, whereas both should be computed consistently from the underlying models and both depend in general on density and tempera-ture. A fully consistent calculation would require a single potential describing the hadronic and the quark phase, while we are using two separate models; a multiple-reflection-expansion estimate would be a first step, al-though, as discussed in Sec. IV A 2, different microscopic calculations still give widely different values. The treatment of pairing in small droplets is deliber-ately minimal: a sharp switch at R∆ = 1 /∆ stands in for what is really a condensate that turns on gradually with the size of the system. A more complete analysis of di-21 quark pairing in finite systems might modify the results: if the true coherence length were larger than assumed, the critical droplet would remain unpaired over a wider range of conditions, the barrier would be higher and a smaller surface tension would already suffice to prevent nucleation. The extreme case is a first droplet that never pairs at all: for the parametrization of Fig. 4 this lowers 

σcrit at 1 .4 M ⊙ from ≃ 104 to ≃ 99 MeV fm −2.Another, more structural caveat concerns which droplet configurations are actually reachable. The saddle-point treatment of the composition assumes that every configuration compatible with the strong-interaction conservation laws (namely, those that do not require weak interactions) can be realized, either through a prior fluctuation of the hadronic composition or through flavor exchange across the interface while the droplet exists. In a more realistic treatment of such a complicated dynamical system, one should also con-sider a phenomenon not taken into account within a purely thermodynamical approach: rearranging a con-served charge over the size of the droplet takes time, and configurations that would require transporting it further than the available diffusion length should be excluded from the accessible ensemble. Restricting the accessible configurations can only raise the barrier, so in this respect the present results are conservative and may overestimate the ease of nucleation. A preliminary framework for tak-ing care of this effect has been presented in [73], where the restriction is found to matter most precisely when the hadronic phase carries little strangeness; a quantita-tive treatment requires the use of transport properties in hadronic and quark matter that are beyond the scope of this work. We have also relied on a single hadronic EOS parametrization and on one model for the quark phase. Within the αBag description the pairing gap is a con-stant parameter, whereas in a model such as NJL it is a quantity to be computed and is density dependent, which would make R∆ density dependent as well and would modify the extent of the gap-locked regime. We have moreover used the same bag constant for the un-paired and the CFL phase, which is the natural choice but not a necessary one; allowing the two values of the bag constant to differ would open a further direction in parameter space. Testing the framework against other hadronic and quark EOSs is therefore an obvious next step. The thermodynamic conditions of the PNS have been taken from simulations performed with a different hadronic EOS, and summarized by two representative snapshots. The same uniform SH /N B and Y HL enter the whole-star integral of the rate of Sec. V E; in a real PNS both vary with radius, which changes how fast nHB and 

T fall off away from the center and hence the size of the nucleating region, although by Eq. (30) this affects 

σcrit only logarithmically. Dedicated simulations with the EOS and the parametrizations used here would replace this approximation with an actual evolutionary track. Finally, we have imposed only minimal astrophysical requirements, namely that the QS branch support 2 M ⊙

pulsars. A natural development is to embed the present nucleation criterion in a full Bayesian inference of the joint hadronic–SQM parameter space [27], replacing the qualitative assumption adopted there (namely, that any star reaching a central strangeness density nS ≥ nsat 

nucleates and cannot persist as a metastable configura-tion [49, 50, 77]) by the conditions derived in this work. Such an exhaustive exploration is beyond the aim of the present paper and it is an interesting direction for future research. The same framework applies well beyond the PNS stage. Nucleation could also be triggered during the rapid accretion that follows core bounce, in BCSMs, or in ac-creting binaries with a NS companion. Unlike PNSs and mergers, accreting NSs are cold, T ≲ 0.1 MeV, so that quantum tunneling rather than thermal activation would dominate the decay of the metastable phase. Beyond the BW hypothesis, the same finite-size treatment can be used for the formation of hybrid stars, and, with the ap-propriate changes in system size and particle content, for heavy-ion collisions. We close with a caveat of a different kind. We have assumed throughout that the transition proceeds by nu-cleation, that is, that the system crosses a genuine barrier from a metastable state. If instead an external perturba-tion drove the matter into the mechanically unstable re-gion faster than a critical droplet can form, the transition would start by spinodal decomposition, with no barrier to overcome and no critical size: any fluctuation would grow immediately. Our approach is blind to this possi-bility, since a proper treatment requires a single poten-tial describing both phases [36]. In a PNS, however, the thermodynamic conditions evolve slowly enough that the star should remain in the metastable region long enough for critical droplets to form before the unstable region is reached, thus we expect nucleation to be the relevant channel.

> Appendix B: Composition of the critical droplet

The second methodological choice worth isolating is the composition of the droplet. In the frozen-flavor pre-scription, used in much of the literature, the droplet in-herits the average flavor content of the surrounding bulk hadronic matter, Y Q∗ 

> C

= Y HC and Y Q∗ 

> S

= Y HS : the ex-change of strong charges across the interface is assumed to be ineffective on the lifetime of the fluctuation, and the hadronic phase is assumed to be compositionally homo-geneous. In the saddle-point prescription adopted here, both assumptions are relaxed and the composition is the one that minimizes the work of formation, which is equiv-alent to strong chemical equilibrium across the interface. Physically, the configuration that minimizes the bar-rier can be reached with two different mechanisms or a combination of them. The hadronic phase can itself fluc-tuate before the droplet appears, producing a subsystem whose local flavor content differs from the bulk average; nucleation then takes place inside that subsystem, and the total work of formation is the sum of the cost of cre-ating the fluctuation and the cost of creating the droplet within it. Alternatively, the droplet can exchange net flavor with its surroundings across the interface while it exists. Either way, the accessible configurations are those reachable by the strong interaction alone, and a complete calculation of the rate would integrate over all of them. Because the rate is exponential in −W∗/T , that integral is dominated by the single configuration of least work, and it is that configuration, the saddle point, that we use. Thus, the two prescriptions are two limits of the same question: which droplet configurations are actu-ally reachable in a system with a finite lifetime. The saddle-point limit assumes that every configuration al-lowed by the strong-interaction conservation laws can be reached; the frozen limit is the opposite assumption, ap-propriate if the hadronic composition does not fluctuate and the droplet cannot exchange conserved charges with its surroundings by diffusion within the time it exists. A detailed discussion and a preliminary framework are provided in [73]. The difference between the two is not a modest quan-titative shift, see Fig. 7. The frozen curve lies far above all of them, with a difference of several tens of MeV that closes only at much higher densities. Panel (b) shows why, by displaying the composition of the critical droplet itself as a function of nHB at T =30 MeV. The frozen droplet, by construction, is pinned to the strangeness of the bulk average hadronic phase, which is negligible at low density and increases as the hyperon population builds up.
