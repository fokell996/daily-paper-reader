## Composition g-modes and f -modes of neutron stars with gravitationally coupled dark matter: degeneracy with the symmetry energy

(Dated: October 8, 2026) Composition gravity ( g) modes have been proposed as a probe of dark matter in neutron stars, on the basis of one-fluid models in which the dark matter is in chemical equilibrium with the neutrons. We computed in full general relativity the g- and f -modes of stars in which fermionic dark matter couples to the nucleons only through gravity, and inferred the dark mass fraction from NICER and GW170817 data with a prior that comprises 63 nucleonic equations of state (EOSs) from Skyrme and relativistic mean-field functionals, among them the models of Dutra et al. and of the compilation of Sun, Bhattiprolu and Lattimer. The data do not prefer dark matter and bound its mass fraction at 0 .12–0 .26 (95%) for particle masses of 0 .5–2 GeV. Coupled only through gravity, the dark matter reaches the buoyancy that restores a g-mode only through the gravitational field: a dark core holding 10% of the mass raises the fundamental g-mode by 10–15% at fixed EOS, several times less than in the one-fluid picture. The larger effect is indirect: a star with a dark core needs a stiffer nucleonic EOS, and since the g-mode is set by the density dependence of the symmetry energy, its frequency reflects the EOS that the star requires rather than the dark matter it contains; at fixed radius and symmetry energy a core holding 10% of the mass is equivalent to a change of the slope L of 9–12 MeV. The f -mode, which follows the mean density, behaves differently: a dark core breaks the relation between the f -mode and the tidal deformability by about one percent, far outside the posterior spread of nucleonic stars. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Neutron stars can carry dark matter captured from the Galactic halo or produced inside them [1–3]. If the dark matter interacts with ordinary matter only through gravity, the star consists of two fluids with separately con-served stress tensors [3, 4], and the dark matter settles in a core or in an extended halo, depending on its par-ticle mass, self-interaction and amount [5–7]. The mass, radius and tidal deformability do not separate such a star from a nucleonic one: a stiffer nucleonic equation of state (EOS) with a dark core reproduces a softer one without [8–10], and probes beyond the bulk properties are needed [11]. Composition gravity ( g) modes respond to the interior composition rather than to the stiffness. Their restor-ing force is the buoyancy of a fluid element whose pro-ton and lepton fractions stay frozen on the oscillation time scale [12]; their frequencies, ∼ 100–500 Hz, are set by the difference between the frozen-composition sound speed cs and the equilibrium sound speed ceq , and hence by the density dependence of the symmetry energy [13– 15]. The connection is direct: β equilibrium fixes the proton fraction through μn − μp ≃ 4Esym (n)(1 − 2Yp), so a symmetry energy that rises steeply with density, a large slope L, makes the proton fraction climb steeply toward the centre. A fluid element displaced downward then carries too few protons for its new surroundings, is

lighter than they are at the same pressure, and is pushed back [12]; the steeper the proton-fraction gradient, the stronger this buoyancy and the higher the g-mode fre-quency. They can be excited resonantly during a binary inspiral [16–19]. For dark matter produced by neutron decay, which is in chemical equilibrium with the neutrons and forms one fluid with them, Shirke, Chatterjee and Jaikumar [20] found in the Cowling approximation that 

g1 rises by 14 .23 Hz per percent of dark matter, nearly independently of the nucleonic EOS, and noted that a stiffer nucleonic EOS with dark matter can give the same frequency as a nucleonic star. For dark matter coupled only by gravity, the f -modes have been computed in the two-fluid Cowling approximation [21–23], where the f –tidal-deformability relation breaks for dark fractions of 0.2–0 .6 [23], and in full general relativity (GR) [24, 25]. The g-modes of such stars have not been computed. In this paper we compute the g- and f -modes of two-fluid stars in full GR, infer the dark fraction from NICER and GW170817 data, and propagate the posterior to the mode frequencies. The picture is simple. Gravity alone couples the dark matter only weakly to the buoyancy, so the direct effect on the g-modes is small; the larger effect is indirect, through the stiffer nucleonic EOS that the data demand once the star carries dark matter, and it is therefore degenerate with the symmetry energy. > III. RESULTS A. A small direct shift and a larger indirect one

Figure 1(a) shows how much the dark matter itself moves g1. At fixed EOS a dark core that holds ten per-cent of the mass raises the frequency by 10–15%, 2–3 Hz per percent of dark matter at the posterior median, and up to 30% for the EOSs with the strongest buoyancy; a halo moves it by only a few hertz, whatever its mass. Both stay below the 14 Hz per percent of the one-fluid picture [20], and the reason is Eq. (1). A core deep-ens the potential in which the nucleons sit and raises Φ ′,and with it N , but it leaves the composition gradient D

untouched; most of the mass of a halo lies outside the nucleonic fluid, where it does not enter Φ ′ at all. When the dark matter is in chemical equilibrium with the neu-trons, by contrast, its abundance changes with depth and adds to the composition gradient itself [20]. D. The f -mode test 

The f -mode is a different kind of oscillation: it is restored by pressure rather than buoyancy, and its fre-quency follows the mean density of the nucleonic fluid. At fixed radius a core, which compresses the nucleonic fluid, raises it [Fig. 4(b)], and at fixed EOS the core raises νf

by 10–14% ( mχ = 1 GeV) for every functional, whereas the g1 shift varies from 5 to 17%. In the posterior, how-ever, the data again select a stiffer EOS, and the f -mode and radius together separate cores from nucleonic stars only at the level of a few percent. A second test removes the need for a radius. The re-lation between M ω f and ln Λ [47] holds for single-fluid stars to 0 .37% [55], and the posterior of our nucleonic stars follows it to within 0 .05% (Fig. 5). > E. Mode data demand a complete EOS prior

Could a measured mode frequency reveal dark matter? We added to the likelihood a mock g1 frequency of a 1.4 M⊙ star from one of the twin pairs ( mχ = 1 GeV, 

FD ≤ 0.1). With a 5% error, which stands for the theory systematic (the crust–core transition alone moves νg1 by several percent), the admixed star gives ln B = +0 .3 and its nucleonic partner −0.5 at the median; with g1 and 

f measured to 1% the separation becomes decisive (+13 against −2.4). This assumes that the true nucleonic EOS is in the prior. When it is left out, 5% of nucleonic stars give ln B > 1 for dark matter with g1 at 5%, and 27% (up to ln B = 196) with g1 and f at 1%, because a dark core is then the model’s only way to reproduce mode frequencies that no EOS of the prior has. Percent-level mode data can manufacture evidence for dark matter as easily as they find it.  

> F. Detectability

In a binary, the resonant excitation of g1 shifts the gravitational-wave phase [16, 17, 56]. For two 1 .2 M⊙

stars the posterior gives a median of 0 .002 rad with-out and 0.006 rad with dark cores (mχ = 1 GeV, 

FD = 0 .1), and 95th percentiles of 0 .03 and 0 .03 rad, near the ≈ 0.03 rad that the Einstein Telescope can de-tect in favourable events [56]. As free oscillations the 

g-modes are hard to see: gravitational radiation damps them only over 10 7–10 12 s (90% posterior interval for nu-cleonic stars), and the shear and bulk viscosity of normal matter [16, 57], evaluated on the full-GR eigenfunctions, damp them faster at every core temperature we consid-ered. Our frequencies apply above the neutron critical temperature; in superfluid cores the composition g-modes are carried by the muons or disappear [58].  

> IV. LIMITATIONS

Several approximations limit the numbers, though we expect none of them to change the conclusions. (i) Crust. 

All our crusts are unstratified ( cs = ceq ). The crust– core transition alone moves the g1 frequency: for SLy4 at 1 .4 M⊙ the Cowling νg1 is 128 .7, 132 .6 and 139 .8 Hz for a transition at nt = 0 .089, 0 .070 and 0 .051 fm −3. This systematic, comparable to the direct dark-matter shift, is why we took 5% as the theory error of νg1 ; a strati-fied crust treated consistently with the core [59] would reduce it. (ii) Superfluidity. Our modes are those of normal npeμ matter. Below the neutron critical tem-perature the composition g-modes of npe matter disap-pear, and with muons temperature-dependent superfluid 

g-modes of several times higher frequency appear [58] (see also Ref. [60]). The dark matter acts on the nucle-ons only through Φ ′ and through the nucleonic EOS that the data select, neither of which involves the superfluid dynamics, so we expect the degeneracy with the symme-try energy to carry over, but we have not computed it. (iii) EOS prior. The prior is a discrete set of 63 func-tionals, so the posterior summaries are coarse, and the bound on dark cores depends on the stiffest functionals admitted, as shown above. A continuous prior built from nuclear empirical parameters [61], with the proton frac-tion, and hence D, computed self-consistently [62], would remove this dependence; it must control the high-density symmetry energy that sets νg1 . (iv) Pulse profiles. The NICER analyses assume a vacuum exterior. The dark mass of a halo outside RN bends the light [6, 63, 64], so the likelihood of halo models is approximate. (v) Dark-6

matter model. We used one fermionic model with one self-interaction, a single FD for all stars, zero tempera-ture, no rotation and coupling through gravity only. Adark fraction that varies from star to star would weaken the bounds, and other dark sectors, such as bosonic [8] or mirror [65] dark matter, change the dark EOS but not the mechanism, because the g-modes live in the nucleonic fluid and feel the dark component only through Φ ′.

V. CONCLUSIONS 

For dark matter coupled to neutron stars only by grav-ity, the composition g-modes are a poor dark-matter probe. The dark matter reaches the buoyancy only through the gravitational field, so its direct effect on the 

g-modes is several times below the one-fluid estimate. The larger effect comes through the nucleonic EOS that the data demand once the star carries dark matter, and because the g-modes are set by the symmetry energy, it is degenerate with nuclear physics: a core with 10% of the mass is worth 9–12 MeV of L. The f -mode, which follows the mean density, responds to a dark core in the same way for all functionals, and the f –Love relation, which nucleonic stars obey closely, is broken by about one percent, a test that uses observables of a single star. A dark-matter interpretation of any mode frequency re-quires a nucleonic EOS prior that is complete at the level of the measurement.
