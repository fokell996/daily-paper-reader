ABSTRACT Radio pulsars exhibit frequency-dependent variations in the shape of their pulse profiles that intro-duce systematic delays in the observed pulse times of arrival (TOA) across an observing band. The North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves (NANOGrav) models these delays using a frequency-dependent (FD) log-polynomial function whose coefficients are fit to TOA measure-ments at different radio frequencies. However, FD parameters are prone to absorbing power from other chromatic effects, such as interstellar dispersion, leading to fitted FD values that overestimate profile evolution and vary significantly across datasets. To separate these effects, we introduce two new mathematical formulations of profile evolution: a monomial expansion (IFD) and a Legendre polynomial expansion (LIFD). We test these models on observations of 11 pulsars in the NANOGrav 15-year dataset and on simulated data, and also compare their performance to that of frequency-resolved pulse templates. We find that Legendre-based models generally predict timing delays that more closely replicate the true profile evolution, reducing the residual offset relative to FD by as much as 85% for the simulated data. Additionally, we find that the choice of profile evolution model can bias the fitted dispersion model, with FD-based models overestimating the injected DM by 0 .013 pc cm −3

compared to 0 .0002 pc cm −3 for LIFD. These results support the use of Legendre bases to mitigate covariances between chromatic effects, directly benefiting targeted timing studies of these effects and the development of advanced noise-modeling techniques for nanohertz gravitational-wave detection.

INTRODUCTION 1.1. Profile Evolution With Frequency 

Pulsars are rapidly rotating neutron stars that emit highly regular pulsed radio emission. In particular, mil-lisecond pulsars (MSPs) are extremely stable rotators, whose pulsed emission has been measured with sub-microsecond precision (e.g., Agazie et al. 2023b). By comparing the observed times of arrival (TOAs) of these pulses with the predictions from sophisticated mathe-matical models known as timing models , we can detect timing offsets generated by a myriad of astrophysical phenomena and probe their underlying physical proper-ties. Notably, long-term monitoring of an array of the most stable MSPs has led to strong evidence for a signal consistent with nanohertz-frequency gravitational radi-ation from a population of supermassive black hole bi-naries (e.g., Agazie et al. 2023a; Antoniadis et al. 2023; Reardon et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023).

High-precision pulsar timing experiments require care-ful modeling of noise sources that change the TOAs and obscure underlying timing signatures. Some of these are sources extrinsic to the pulsar, including the interstel-lar medium (ISM; e.g., Keith et al. 2012; Geiger et al. 2025), Solar wind (e.g., Madison et al. 2019; Tiburzi et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024), and Solar System ephemeris (e.g., Lazio et al. 2017; Vallisneri et al. 2020; Dai et al. 2024). Other noise sources are intrinsic to the pulsar emission processes, such as pulse jitter (e.g., Oslowski et al. 2011; Shannon et al. 2014; Lam et al. 2019), mode changes (e.g., Bartel et al. 1982; Miles et al. 2022), and spin noise (e.g., Shannon & Cordes 2010; Lam et al. 2016d) including glitches (e.g., Zubieta et al. 2024). A known intrinsic pulsar phenomenon that can con-tribute to timing noise is the evolution of the observed pulse-profile shape with radio frequency. Most pulsars exhibit a systematic increase in pulse width and a sepa-ration of pulse-profile components at lower frequencies, unrelated to ISM effects (e.g., Kramer et al. 1998), as exemplified in Fig. 1. Different physical mechanisms have been proposed to explain pulse profile evolution (PE) with frequency. Komesaroff (1970) first suggested that lower-frequency emission originates higher in the magnetosphere, where dipolar field lines subtend a larger angular region, caus-ing the emission beam to sweep across the observer’s line of sight for a longer fraction of the pulsar’s rota-tion and thus producing wider pulses. This idea, known as radius-to-frequency mapping (RFM), is theoretically grounded in the polar cap model of Ruderman & Suther-land (1975) and was first proposed for canonical pul-sars by Cordes (1978). Shang et al. (2017) proposed that these frequency-dependent emission heights arise because radio emission at different frequencies is pro-duced most efficiently in distinct regions of the magne-tosphere. In contrast, Dyks & Rudak (2014) argued that spectral gradients within different emission beam geome-tries (cones or streams) can change how the line of sight samples the emission at different frequencies, producing component evolution without requiring RFM’s emission at different altitudes. Other explanations include in-trinsic magnetospheric effects, including refraction and coupling between orthogonally polarized modes (Craft & Comella 1968; Oswald et al. 2023), as well as extrinsic contributions from propagation through the interstellar medium (Jaroenjittichai et al. 2025). More wideband studies are needed to test and constrain the underlying radiation mechanisms (e.g., Xu et al. 2021). Since observations for high-precision timing typically span one or more broad frequency bands, PE across that band can impact TOA calculation in different ways: 

• In narrow-band timing, each band is divided into multiple sub-bands (Desvignes et al. 2016) or fre-quency channels (e.g., Agazie et al. 2023b). A sep-arate TOA is measured for each sub-band or chan-nel by estimating the time shift of its pulse pro-file relative to an idealized noise-free pulse shape, known as a “template” (Taylor 1992). However, a frequency-constant template will not correctly capture PE-induced effects and will thus deviate from the true pulse shape at different frequen-cies. These mismatches between the data and the template result in small but systematic frequency-dependent TOA offsets (e.g., Brook et al. 2018a). 

• Some studies frequency-average the data across each observing band and compare the resulting in-tegrated profile to a template to produce a single TOA for the full receiver band (e.g., Manchester et al. 2013). However, if the bandwidth is suffi-4ciently large, PE introduces frequency-dependent timing offsets across frequency channels, which, when averaged, smear the integrated pulse profile and reduce TOA precision. 

• In wideband timing, a single TOA is produced for the entire bandwidth by fitting all frequen-cies simultaneously using a two-dimensional tem-plate, known as a “pulse portrait,” which encodes pulse evolution as a sum of either Gaussian compo-nents (Pennucci et al. 2014), channelized Fourier transforms (Liu et al. 2014), or low-degree piece-wise polynomial spline functions (Pennucci 2019). Even then, residual frequency-dependent structure has been reported in the timing data from wide-band timing (e.g., Curylo et al. 2023; Alam et al. 2021b), similarly to that seen in narrowband anal-yses with frequency-averaged templates. In either case, mis-modeled PE results in reduced TOA precision and reduced sensitivity to effects targeted in high-precision pulsar timing array (PTA) experiments, including gravitational-wave (GW) detection (Oslowski et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2011; Lentati et al. 2017). 1.2. Accounting for Profile Evolution 

The European Pulsar Timing Array typically splits wideband data into discrete frequency bands within cer-tain bandwidths, such as 140 or 240 MHz for the L-band (1.4 GHz), 300 MHz for S-band (2.2 GHz), and 500 MHz for C-band (4.8 GHz) (EPTA Collaboration et al. 2023). A template is independently created and used to match the data within each band. Because the TOAs are mea-sured relative to this local template, PE-induced shifts are minimized at the TOA-generation level. To align the data from different observing systems, an arbitrary time-constant offset, also known as a “jump,” is fitted for each sub-band (Desvignes et al. 2016). Similarly, the Parkes Pulsar Timing Array averages narrowband observations over the full bandwidth (64 MHz, 256MHz, or 1024MHz) of each of its three re-ceivers. Then, they use different template profiles to measure a TOA for each band and fit “jumps” between bands (Manchester et al. 2013; Kerr et al. 2020; Zic et al. 2023). With the Ultra-Wideband Low receiver (Hobbs et al. 2020), the bandwidth is large enough that the pulse profile can evolve significantly across this fre-quency range, so they use pulse portraits to model PE continuously across the band (Curylo et al. 2023). The North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves (NANOGrav) in its 5-year dataset (Demorest et al. 2013) included a “jump” for each fre-quency channel (rather than each frequency band) in the observations, which is then adjusted as a free parame-ter in the timing fit. However, starting in their 9-year dataset (NANOGrav Collaboration et al. 2015), they re-placed this scheme with FD parameterization. In this approach, band-specific templates are created for each frequency receiver (e.g., the 820- and L- bands at the Green Bank Telescope), and additional timing correc-tions are incorporated into the timing models using a log-polynomial function of frequency to remove PE fre-quency structure. A possible explanation for these shortcomings is that FD parameters are estimated through a global least-squares fit of the timing model to minimize the timing residuals; however, chromatic effects other than PE can introduce frequency-dependent delays, such as disper-sion and scattering, that also contribute to the TOAs (e.g., Brook et al. 2018a). Because these chromatic ef-fects are all fitted simultaneously to the observed TOA frequency dependence, without explicitly separating the physical origin of those TOAs, the resulting fit is suscep-tible to parameter covariances. In other words, we do not know a priori what the timing shifts due to profile evolution and due to other effects are; we can only fit them empirically a posteriori. While it is generally assumed that the largest compo-nent of the frequency-dependent time delay accounted for by FD parameters is due to intrinsic PE (Zhu et al. 2015), due to these covariances, power from other im-perfectly modeled chromatic effects can be redistributed and absorbed into the FD term during the timing fit, changing the inferred FD k parameters away from val-ues that reflect intrinsic PE alone (e.g., van Haasteren & Levin 2013). The combined noise model may ade-quately describe the global chromatic noise, but indi-vidual models associated with specific physical processes may be imperfectly constrained, complicating the phys-ical interpretation of individual chromatic terms. The most significant chromatic effect other than PE is dispersion. FD model describe PE using a polynomial in log( ν), while dispersive delays scale as ν−2. These functional forms are not orthogonal, so the two effects have partial overlap. Similarly, FD parameters will also account for the average scattering broadening over the course of a data set (e.g., Shapiro-Albert et al. 2021). As a result, the timing fit can partially redistribute dis-persion and scattering delays into the FD coefficients. In this paper, we aim to develop a formulation of PE that minimizes covariances with other chromatic effects during the timing fit. In particular, we study PE polyno-mial formulations in monomial and Legendre bases. In Sec. 2, we describe the chromatic effects affecting pulsar TOA measurements and how NANOGrav accounts for them, and we introduce our own PE formulations. In Sec. 4, we fit our new PE models to NANOGrav’s 15-year dataset and to simulated observations, and compare our results to current FD predictions. Finally, in Sec. 5, we discuss the implications of this work, especially con-cerning the accuracy of our noise models. Processed data products presented here are publicly available as of the date this work is published. 

2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND In this section, we describe the chromatic effects on the TOAs, and how they are accounted for in NANOGrav’s timing models. Then we lay out the math-ematical formalism for our novel PE models. 2.1. Frequency-Dependent Effects 

Pulsar observations are never monochromatic, but rather performed over a finite bandwidth comprised of several frequencies. To obtain a TOA, these multi-frequency measurements are usually averaged across the observing bandwidth. However, TOAs exhibit fre-quency dependence arising from a variety of astrophysi-cal sources. These include effects intrinsic to the pulsar 6(e.g., pulse jitter and PE), the interstellar medium (e.g., dispersion and scattering), and the Solar System (so-lar activity and ionospheric delays). As a result, pulses with frequency ν will arrive at Earth at a time tν that is delayed with respect to the expected arrival time t∞

for a signal of infinite frequency (i.e., in the absence of chromatic effects). Pulsar timing applications must then account for these chromatic delays. 2.1.1. Dispersion 

As the pulsar’s radio signal propagates through the in-terstellar medium (ISM), which is an ionized plasma, it encounters a frequency-dependent index of refraction. For a cold, homogeneous, unmagnetized plasma, the cold-plasma dispersion law predicts that a pulse emitted at frequency ν is delayed relative to an infinite-frequency signal by a time tDM = K × DM /ν 2. As pulsar radiation encounters density gradients on scales larger than the Fresnel scale, the coherent ray deviates from straight-line propagation, bending toward regions of higher refractive index (just like light bending at a water/air interface, but applied to a large, extended, slowly-varying gradient). This has two consequences: 

Angle-of-arrival delays : The change in the angle of arrival of the ray at the Earth shifts the apparent direc-tion to the pulsar. Since the timing model assumes a single fixed direction to calculate the Roemer delay, this causes a chromatic delay that varies sinusoidally with an annual period (Foster & Cordes 1990). Since the bending angle, θr , is a first-order effect in the refractive index perturbation ( ∝ ne/ν 2), the induced timing delay is ∝ θr ∝ ν−2 (Lam et al. 2016c). 

Geometric delays : A ray deviated from straight-line propagation has an increased path length and suffers a geometric delay, arriving later than photons traveling in a straight path. This is called refractive scintillation ,but is usually not explicitly modeled in PTA pipelines. 2.1.3. Diffractive Effects 

When a ray encounters a density structure smaller than the Fresnel scale, it is no longer described by a sin-gle deterministic bent ray. Instead, different patches of the medium send a coherent sub-ray to the observer, re-sulting in multiple, simultaneous, stationary-phase rays (Cordes et al. 1986). This results in two effects: 

Scattering : Photons deflected at small angles travel longer paths and arrive at later rotational phases. The resulting pulse profile is the convolution of the intrinsic pulse and an exponential scattering tail , the timescale of which is τscat ∝ ν−α (e.g., Lewandowski et al. 2013; Bansal et al. 2019). This produces a deterministic, chro-matic timing delay that is commonly modeled using a thin-screen approximation (Shannon & Cordes 2017), assuming an isotropic, turbulent medium with a Gaus-sian ( α = 4; Lang 1971) or Kolmogorov ( α = 4 .4; Ro-mani et al. 1986) distribution of inhomogeneities. Most MSPs used in PTAs are at relatively high Galac-tic latitudes, so their lines of sight pass through less turbulent ISM and their τscat at observing frequencies (∼1–2 GHz) is sub-microsecond or sub-nanosecond (e.g., Turner et al. 2021). Therefore, scattering does not have dedicated deterministic parameters in NANOGrav’s timing model, and it is instead included in the noise models as a time-variable stochastic chromatic process with a free spectral index (Hazboun et al. 2025). 

Diffractive scintillation : When photons scattered through different angles recombine at the observer, they will no longer be in phase and will thus interfere with one another, generating stochastic intensity variations across frequency and time known as scintles . For a Kol-mogorov medium, the bandwidth and timescale of these variations are ∆ νd ∝ ν4.4 and ∆ td ∝ ν1.2 (Cordes et al. 71985), although studies have empirically found shallower power indices (Liu et al. 2022; Brown et al. 2025). When averaged over the frequency band, the intensity modulation can cause epoch-to-epoch TOA variations, rather than a deterministic timing delay. Therefore, scintillation is treated as a white-noise contribution that is empirically determined per pulsar (using the white-noise parameters EFAC, EQUAD, and ECORR) rather than following a fixed power law (Lam et al. 2016b). 2.1.4. Advantages and Limitations of a Monomial Basis 

The IFD formulation presents a major advantage over FD parameters: an excess dispersive K ∆DM ν−2 delay is not exactly representable by a finite log-polynomial basis (as in Eq. 1), so it cannot be isolated into spe-cific PE terms. Instead, modeling it requires a linear combination of several logarithmic terms. As a result, a misestimated DM can result in DM-induced timing delays not captured by the dispersion model, being pro-jected onto multiple FD logarithmic terms, biasing the fitted values of multiple FD k parameters. In contrast, when PE is modeled in a monomial ba-sis, a dispersive delay is represented exactly by a sin-gle basis element, λ2, so it is only covariant with IFD 2.Similarly, under the assumptions above that the refrac-tion/scattering contributions scale as λ4, those effects couple only to IFD 4. All remaining coefficients describe a chromatic structure distinct from propagation effects, and we can attribute it to real PE. Therefore, IFD models isolate dispersion- and scattering-induced covari-ances to components geometrically distinguishable and physically interpretable in the chromatic model. Nevertheless, this formulation still has a major limi-tation: the monomial basis functions, ϕk(λ) = ( λ/λ 0)k,are not orthogonal over a finite observing band. As a re-sult, the associated design matrix can be ill-conditioned, and the fitted coefficients become statistically corre-lated. In the presence of realistic chromatic structure beyond pure dispersion, this correlation means that un-certainty in one parameter is statistically coupled to un-certainty in others, thereby reducing the precision with which individual coefficients can be determined. Fur-thermore, in the presence of noise, the projections of small noise components onto strongly correlated basis elements can be amplified, leading to larger fluctuations in the estimated parameter. Although this does not in-troduce systematic biases, it degrades the numerical sta-bility and overall precision of the timing fit. From a geometric perspective, we can think of the monomial basis elements as vectors that are not fully orthogonal. An excess dispersive delay is a vector that lies exactly in the direction of the basis element propor-tional to λ2, so including this signal will only change the fitted value of a2. However, because the basis vec-tors are not orthogonal, small perturbations from the

NANOGrav’s timing models account for interstellar dispersion by fitting a fiducial DM value and DMX cor-rections (see Sec. 2.1). However, correlations between dispersion and PE terms can cause the least-squares fit to redistribute spectral power between these two com-ponents (see Sec. 1.3). Furthermore, estimating the DM by fitting a timing model to TOA measurements over a finite frequency band can yield biased estimates due to incomplete frequency sampling (Sosa Fiscella et al. 2024). In either case, the fitted DM value can be shifted away from the true dispersion measure. Since both the a′

To reduce the magnitude of the DMX corrections from the fiducial DM, and the excess residual amplitude that can be redistributed to the PE parameters, we fit-ted more robust DM values independently from the tim-ing fit. To that end, for each pulsar in our sample: 1. We took an interval [DM 0 − ∆DM , DM 0 + ∆DM] around the NG15 nominal dispersion measure DM 0, where we took ∆DM as 25% of DM 0.2. We selected a set of trial DM values within this range. However, instead of blindly testing DM values, we used the Brent optimization algorithm (Brent 1973) from the SciPy optimize module (Virtanen et al. 2020). In this approach, trial DM values are updated iteratively by successively nar-rowing the range of values on the interval. 3. For each trial DM value, we used PyPulse (Lam 2017) to de-disperse and frequency-average each of the pulsar’s profiles, thus obtaining a time- and frequency-integrated pulse profile per observing epoch, which we cross-correlated to the pulsar’s NG15 template (at the observation’s frequency band) to obtain its signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio. 4. Lastly, we averaged the S/N of all the observations de-dispersed with a given trial DM value. Since ⟨S/N⟩ is computable for all the DM within [DM 0 − ∆DM , DM 0 + ∆DM], the Brent optimization algorithm should converge to the DM value that maxi-mizes ⟨S/N⟩ (Brent 1973). This physically motivated approach takes advantage of the fact that using incorrect DM estimates to de-disperse frequency-resolved data results in a smearing of the integrated pulse profile, reducing the peak amplitude and, therefore, the resulting S/N. Therefore, maximizing the S/N effectively finds the DM that best aligns the pulses across frequency. This provides a DM estimate tied directly to the profile morphology and independent of timing model assumptions. The original and new DM values are presented in Ta-ble 1. Model Implementation and Significance Testing 

After changing the nominal DM value for each pul-sar to the value found in Sec. 3.2, we performed tim-ing fits of its TOAs to timing models that account for PE using either an FD (Eq. 1), IFD (Eq. 7), or LIFD (Eq. 16) model. PINT implements the FD model in its 

FD class. For the other two models, we implemented new 

PINT classes named IFD and LIFD that can be attached to a timing model in replacement of the FD class (see Appx. A). In brief, these new classes operate as follows: 12 1. Given a set of frequency-resolved TOA measure-ments, the IFD /LIFD classes transform the ra-dio frequencies ν to inverse λ or normalized-inverse frequencies x, respectively. For the LIFD 

class, we have defined two new timing parameters, 

LIFD FMIN and LIFD FMAX , representing the maxi-mum and minimum frequencies of the dataset, and which can be readily converted to the maximum and minimum wavelengths defining the λ → x

mapping (see Eq. 12), so the model is reproducible and portable across TOA subsets. 2. PE is modeled as a new frequency-dependent delay term, given either by Eq. 7 or Eq. 16, included in the timing model’s global TOA estimation. 3. Finally, the polynomial coefficients are included as free parameters when using a weighted least-squares algorithm to fit the model to the pulsar’s TOA measurements (e.g., Luo et al. 2021). These PINT classes can be set up with an arbitrary polynomial order. However, fitting an arbitrarily high order increases the number of free parameters, which is both computationally inefficient and can lead to overfit-ting. Instead, an optimal number of parameters should reflect the significance of PE for each pulsar. In the NANOGrav data releases, the optimal num-ber of FD parameters is found iteratively using an F -statistic test (NANOGrav Collaboration et al. 2015). This test is valid when one model is a subset of another, so the data releases have required that all FD param-eters up to the highest-order significant FD parameter be included in the fit, even if the lower-order parameters are not found to be significant. For example, if FD3 is significant but FD2 is not, then FD1, FD2, and FD3 are included (Alam et al. 2021a). However, IFD and LIFD models isolate chromatic ef-fects into specific terms, so we decided to allow non-contiguous combinations of parameters. Therefore, to find the optimal number of IFD/LIFD parameters, we used the Akaike information criterion (AIC) since, un-like the F -test, it does not require the models to be nested. Given a model with best-fit parameters, the AIC is defined as AIC = 2 k − 2 ln( L), where k is the number of free parameters in the model and L is the model likelihood (Burnham & Anderson 2004). Since PE varies smoothly over our observing band-width, higher-order polynomial terms are not expected to contribute significantly, as they would capture fine-scale frequency structure that is not physically consis-tent with PE and also exceeds our frequency resolution, making such terms poorly constrained. We therefore re-stricted the AIC test to IFD/LIFD polynomials of order 

N ≤ 6 as an upper bound, and considered all possi-ble combinations of its 7 terms. The number of com-binations is simplified by considering that dispersion is covariant with IFD2 (see Sec. 2.2.1) and with LIFD0, LIFD1, and LIFD2 (see Sec. 2.3.1), so these terms can always be assumed to be relevant. For each of the parameter combinations, we per-formed a timing fit of the IFD/LIFD model to the pulsar’s NG15 TOAs, and then applied the 

akaike information criterion function from the 

pint.utils module to compute the model’s AIC value. The preferred model is the one that minimizes the AIC. Generally speaking, we found that: 

• The favored LIFD models preserve lower-order (LIFD3 and LIFD4) but remove higher-order co-efficients (LIFD6 and, in some cases, LIFD5). 

• IFD models including IFD0 are usually disfavored. This can be interpreted as IFD0 being a frequency-independent timing offset, which is completely degenerate with other parameters in the timing model (e.g., the pulsar’s phase or period). We exemplify these findings for two representative pulsars in Fig. 3, but we obtained similar AIC results for all MSPs we analyzed. Therefore, in all the subse-quent timing fits, we adopted LIFD polynomials with all terms up to the 5 th order, and IFD polynomials of degree 5 that remove the degenerate IFD0 term.

One possible explanation is that the PE and dispersion model always have some degree of degeneracy in the ν−2

direction, so the timing fit can approximate the same observed chromatic delay with different combinations of PE and DMX parameters. In particular: 

• Since the FD covariance matrix is ill-conditioned, one such possible combination is a large and posi-tive FD delay compensated by fitting a large and negative dispersive delay, or vice versa. The mod-eled FD + DM chromatic delay remains consistent with the data, but the individual components are inflated in opposite directions. 

• In contrast, since LIFD models have more terms orthogonal to a dispersive ν−2 delay, they have lower degeneracy with the DMX parameters. 4.3. Stability Across Datasets 

As explained in Sec. 1.3, one of the main indicators of mis-estimations in the FD parameters is that the cor-responding PE delay curves show unrealistically large amplitudes and also flip concavity depending on the NANOGrav dataset used to fit them (see Fig. 2). Ro-bust estimations of pulse profile evolution should give delay curves that are not only realistic in amplitude but also relatively stable across datasets for a given pulsar. To test the stability of the IFD- and LIFD-derived PE delay curves across datasets, we repeated the timing fits from Sec. 4.1 but using the NANOGrav 9-, 11-, 12.5-, and 15-year datasets (from now on referred to as the NG9, NG11, NG12, and NG15 datasets, respectively). We present the results for the four MSPs in Fig. 7. The FD curves predict unrealistically large delay am-plitudes and flip concavity across datasets. For PSRs J1643 −0719 and J2302+4442, the NG11/15 curves, which use 5 FD parameters, show significantly larger errors and different concavity than the NG9/12 curves, which use 2 FD parameters. A 5-parameter polynomial can fit a more complex PE behavior, so it may capture real chromatic structure that the 2-parameter fits could not model, explaining the concavity flip. However, with 5 parameters, the higher-order terms become partially degenerate, inflating individual parameter uncertainties. The IFD curves have smaller delay amplitudes, do not change dramatically in shape, and are largely consistent within each other’s uncertainties. The error bars also di-minish towards later datasets and, consequently, larger observing baselines. Their drawback is large error bars, especially at larger wavelengths. This indicates that the IFD parameters are non-orthogonal, leading to large, poorly constrained fitted coefficients. The LIFD curves have stable concavities and smaller delay amplitudes than the IFD models. Moreover, the errors are significantly better-constrained across fre-quency than in the IFD model for the same pulsars and datasets, even though both use order-6 polynomi-als. This can be attributed to the base orthogonality, which means that the fitter can adjust each coefficient separately, keeping individual uncertainties small. 17 0.8 1.0 1.2 1.4 1.6 1.8

4.4. Performance on Simulated Data 

In real pulsar data, the exact contributions from dis-persion and PE to the total chromatic delay can never be fully disentangled a posteriori due to their degener-acy in the ν−2-direction of vector space. To reliably quantify the ability of different models to separate the two effects, we created simulated data where the contri-butions of dispersion and PE can be set a priori. The simulated data consist of time-, frequency-, and phase-resolved observations of a synthetic pulsar with a period of 5 .16 ms to mimic that of PSR J1024-0719. We controlled the number, widths, positions, and relative amplitudes of the pulse components to emulate PE ef-fects. In particular, we used a unimodal Gaussian profile with a PE sweep of 110 μs (see Fig. 8). Moreover, we applied a ν−2-chromatic timing delay to each frequency channel to emulate a slow-varying DM time series in the range 18 .412 ± 0.0005 pc /cm 3 (see red curve Fig. 11b). We also injected white noise with a median of 2 .8 μs. Using these parameters, we simulated 477 observations spanning 10.38 years with weekly cadence; each obser-vation has 32 evenly spaced frequency channels between 728.1 and 921.9 MHz, resulting in 15264 unique TOAs. We simulated three datasets, incorporating different combinations of chromatic delays: 1) PE and disper-sion, 2) PE and no dispersion, and 3) dispersion and no PE. For each dataset, we created frequency-resolved TOAs and fit them using a timing model with either an FD, IFD, or LIFD term. We plotted the timing de-lay predicted by the resulting PE and DM models as a function of observing frequency in Fig. 9. We see that: 

• When we injected both PE and dispersive delays in the simulated TOAs (Fig. 9a), FD and IFD models fitted PE delay amplitudes significantly larger than the injected PE sweep, compensated by large, oppositely-signed DM delay. Only the LIFD model resulted in realistic delay amplitudes. 

• For the dataset with no injected dispersion (Fig. 9b), the DM delay curve should be consistent with zero. However, FD and IFD still produced significant DM-induced timing delays. LIFD re-sulted in a significantly smaller dispersive delay than FD or IFD models, and in a PE delay whose amplitude, 110 μs, is in remarkable agreement with our injected PE delay sweep. 

• When we simulated pulse profiles with no PE (Fig. 9c), both FD and IFD models still produced a timing fit with a non-negligible PE term. Only the LIFD model fitted a PE delay that, while not strictly zero, has zero mean and small amplitudes. For the dataset with PE and dispersion delays, Fig. 8 shows the PE predicted by each model, overlaid on the actual frequency-resolved pulse profiles, and the offset of each model’s delay curve with respect to the injected PE. We find that the FD model overestimates the in-jected PE evolution by as much as 1191 μs. By con-trast, the LIFD model resulted in a fitted PE delay curve that, in the worst case, overestimates the injected PE by 174 .5 μs, thus reducing the PE error by 85%. In Fig. 10, we present the DM time series recovered from the two datasets with injected dispersion. We see that all FD, IFD, and LIFD models result in similar temporal trends in the fitted DM( t) (i.e., their peaks and valleys line up). This is reasonable, since these PE models are functions of frequency only and do not in-clude time-dependent corrections; therefore, any time-dependent structure in the injected signal must be ab-sorbed by the DM( t) model, which is the same in all three cases. An alternative to accounting for PE in the timing model is to remove this effect a priori during TOA-generation by using frequency-resolved templates. We will thus use this approach as a benchmark test for our PE models. In that respect, wideband timing employs template portraits, where the pulse shape is a continu-ous function of frequency and phase, and all frequencies are fitted simultaneously to produce a single wideband TOA (see Sec. 1.1). However, since our observations are in the narrowband regime, we used discrete 2D tem-plates, where the pulse shape varies with frequency but only in discrete steps, with one pulse profile shape per frequency sub-band. In this approach, each frequency channel in the frequency-resolved observation is cross-correlated with the corresponding frequency slice of the discrete 2D template, resulting in as many TOAs per observation as frequency channels in it. Starting from a dataset of time- and frequency-resolved observations, we generate a 2D template by: 1. Selecting a subset of the highest-S/N observations. 2. Aligning them in phase (e.g., using pam ). 3. Appending the observations in time (using 

psradd ) and then time-averaging them to obtain a high-S/N frequency-resolved pulse profile. 4. Frequency-averaging the resulting observation to the desired number of frequency channels, and 5. Applying a pulse-smoothing algorithm to the pro-file in each channel (using psrsmooth ). Each observation is then cross-correlated against this 2D template using pat . For a complete description of the 2D TOA generation process, see Melfor et al. (2026). We then compared the DM time series recovered from performing a timing fit using either: 1) TOAs created using a 1D template and a timing model that includes an FD or LIFD term (a posteriori fitting), or 2) TOAs generated using a 2D template and a timing model that does not include a PE term (since that effect was al-ready accounted for during the TOA generation). We perform this comparison using: a) real NG15 observa-tions of PSR B1937+21 for its large PE, and b) simu-lated observations that include pulse PE and dispersion. We present the recovered DM time series in Fig. 11. We observe that, for both real and simulated data, the DM time series recovered using 2D templates and LIFD models have significantly smaller errors than the DM time series obtained using FD models. Most notably, for the simulated observations, where we can control the injected dispersion, both the LIFD and the 2D template methods recover significantly better approximations to the real (injected) DM time series than the FD model.

CONCLUSIONS 5.1. Discussion of Results 

In this work, we tackled the mis-estimations in the FD models of pulse profile evolution with frequency, which lead to predictions of PE-induced timing delays that are not only inconsistent with the observed dispersion-corrected pulse profiles (see Fig. 1), but also change sig-nificantly across NANOGrav datasets (see Fig. 2). We attributed this discrepancy to FD’s log-polynomial co-efficients being covariant with one another and with pa-rameters describing other chromatic effects, most no-tably dispersion. These covariances produce an ill-conditioned design matrix for the timing fit and, conse-quently, statistically correlated and poorly constrained fitted parameters that can shift spectral power across chromatic effects and bias the fitted timing parameters. To mitigate these covariances, we introduced two novel PE formulations, using monomial (IFD) and Leg-endre (LIFD) bases, and tested them on the NG15 TOA measurements of 11 MSPs and on simulated data. LIFD consistently predicted PE delay curves that more closely resemble the observed pulse profiles (see Fig. 4) and are more stable and better constrained across datasets (see Fig. 7) than those obtained using FD models. The fitted IFD models generally predicted delay curves with am-plitudes intermediate between FD and LIFD, but larger variances than the LIFD curves. We also tested these models on simulated data, where we can control the injected DM and PE. Once again, we found FD and IFD models are susceptible to misat-tributing spectral power and fitting significant disper-sive/PE timing delays when none was injected. LIFD models provided the results most consistent with the injected chromatic delays, and their performance was comparable to that obtained using discrete frequency-resolved templates to account for PE a priori during TOA generation. These results support the idea that the choice of the basis used to describe PE determines the ability of a tim-ing model to separate and constrain the contributions of the different chromatic effects: 

• FD’s log-polynomial basis elements are not only covariant with one another, but also do not align with the directions of power-law chromatic effects in vector space, including dispersion. • IFD models better approximate the geometry of the problem by using basis elements that align with the power-law vectors and, in turn, constrain power leakage from other chromatic effects to spe-cific IFD terms. However, the fitted parameters are covariant with one another, and thus suscepti-ble to numerical instability and uncertainty prop-agation, resulting in poorly constrained fitted val-ues and larger variances. 

• LIFD models encode the PE delay into an orthog-onal basis where the fitted polynomial coefficients are independent, and errors in one direction of vector space do not propagate into other basis di-rections. This approach constrains spectral power leakage while minimizing parameter covariances, leading to more realistic PE. We also found that, over NANOGrav’s observing bandwidths, the fitted total chromatic delay is inde-pendent of the PE model (see Fig. 5). This suggests that an accurate phenomenological description of the global chromatic behavior in timing observations, as required by high-precision timing experiments (Agazie et al. 2023c), can be achieved even if the individual chromatic terms are biased. Because TOAs provide the sum of all chromatic terms at each observed frequency and epoch, and not how that sum is separated between dispersion and PE, this allows for degenerate solutions reproducing the same total chromatic delay. Accurate measurements of PE are extremely impor-tant for precision pulsar timing and for understand-ing noise in the timing data. In particular, GWs are not radio-frequency dependent, so PE-induced TOA bi-ases do not prevent potential GW detections, but they could induce additional red noise processes in the data (Shapiro-Albert et al. 2021). Moreover, advanced noise modeling techniques, such as those discussed in Arzou-manian et al. (2020), will benefit greatly from disentan-gling PE from other chromatic effects when precision down to ∼ 100 ns is needed. 5.2. Limitations 

Among the limitations of these methods, we recall that LIFD models do not completely isolate PE from other chromatic effects. In general, any algebraic basis used to describe PE will contain linear combinations of terms that scale as dispersion-like ν−2 and scattering-like ν−4.These components are therefore intrinsically covariant with propagation effects, and no choice of basis can fully disentangle them. Instead, the basis determines how this degeneracy spreads among the PE parameters and how covariant they are with each other. In the LIFD models, the projection of an excess dispersive-ν−2 signal is con-fined to the LIFD 0, LIFD 1, and LIFD 2 terms, and all other fitted parameters are decoupled from dispersion (see Sec. 2.3.1). This is already an advantage over FD models, where all FD k parameters are covariant with dispersion, leading to a more numerically unstable rep-resentation of the same underlying degeneracy. Another limitation is that we only analyzed the degen-eracy of PE with dispersion because it is often the most prominent propagation effect in pulsar observations. Other chromatic effects, such as scattering, refraction, and scintillation, act as noise processes and are not rep-22 resented by deterministic parameters in NANOGrav’s timing models. However, they will still introduce covari-ances at different levels in each of our PE formulations. For example, assuming that scattering delays scale as 

ν−4, they will be covariant with the IFD 4 parameter and with all LIFD k parameters of order k ≤ 4. Building on this point, we also assumed the weak-scattering regime, under which refraction-induced ge-ometric delays scale as trefrac ∝ ν−4, and a Gaus-sian medium, under which scattering delays scale as 

τscat ∝ ν−4. However, most MSPs showing diffractive scintillation are in the strong scattering regime (e.g., Cordes et al. 2016), where trefrac ∝ ν−2β+4 , with β the spectral index of the electron-density power spectrum (Shannon & Cordes 2017). For a Kolmogorov medium (β = 11 /3), this becomes trefrac ∝ ν−10 /3 ≈ ν−3.33 (shal-lower than ν−4) and τscat ∝ ν−4.4 (steeper than ν−4). We then expect this leakage, which was confined to the IFD 4λ4 term, to become degenerate with PE across sev-eral IFD terms, hindering the IFD model’s ability to iso-late chromatic effects. Nonetheless, LIFD models, which use an orthogonal, well-conditioned polynomial basis, should still have lower coefficient covariance and bet-ter mitigate the effects of mis-specified exponent leakage than FD or IFD models. Finally, we treated the LIFD polynomial basis as or-thogonal and its coefficients as uncorrelated. Strictly speaking, however, Legendre polynomials are only or-thogonal under the inner product defined by Eq. 11 with a flat weighting function w(x) = 1. In pulsar tim-ing, TOAs at different frequencies are included in the fit with weight proportional to their inverse variance, i.e., 

w(λ) ∝ 1/σ 2(λ). For typical TOA uncertainties, where 

σ(λ) varies only weakly with frequency, this weighting remains close to uniform, and the LIFD basis is approxi-mately orthogonal in practice. Some covariance between LIFD parameters is still expected, but it remains signif-icantly smaller than in non-orthogonal bases. 5.3. Future Work 

With the advent of wideband radio receivers, such as the ultra-wideband receiver (UWBR) at the Green Bank Telescope (Ransom et al. 2026) and the ultra-wideband low (UWL) receiver at the Parkes radio telescope (Hobbs et al. 2020), accounting for PE across broad frequency ranges has become increasingly important (e.g., Has-sall et al. 2012). For these receivers, pulse portraits have emerged as the preferred PE-modeling scheme (see Sec. 1.1). However, wideband timing residuals still show evidence of unmodeled frequency dependence, which is typically corrected by adding a-posteriori PE parame-ters to the timing model (e.g., Curylo et al. 2023; Alam et al. 2021b). This residual frequency dependence sug-gests that PE models capable of minimizing parameter covariances, such as LIFD, may still play a relevant role in high-precision timing experiments in the era of wide-band pulsar timing. In our analysis, we assumed that pulse profiles show no secular evolution, so any PE-induced timing delay is independent of the observing epoch. While this is gener-ally a good practical approximation (Lam et al. 2016a), some pulsars exhibit temporal variations in pulse shape, sometimes with long-term, quasi-periodic, and corre-lated behavior (e.g., Stairs et al. 2000; Keith et al. 2025). These variations may be due to ISM effects (Brook et al. 2018a), magnetospheric state changes (Lyne et al. 2010), geodetic precession (Perera et al. 2010), or long-term evolution in component separation (Lyne et al. 2013), among others. This suggests that the profile at a given frequency can also depend on time. Although these tem-poral changes are more pronounced in young pulsars than in MSPs, they can still introduce timing system-atics in profile evolution models (e.g., Pennucci et al. 2014). Therefore, future work could extend the current analysis to include epoch-dependent piecewise correc-tions to the IFD and LIFD parameters. The ability to effectively isolate PE will be key to test-ing and constraining theoretical models of pulsar emis-sion. An open question (Buschauer & Benford 1980) is whether pulsar emission is inherently narrowband (with different frequencies generated at distinct altitudes, giv-ing rise to RFM) or broadband (with a single emis-sion region producing radiation across a wide frequency range). The frequency dependence of pulse microstruc-ture may differ markedly between the two cases (Cordes 1978), suggesting that constraining how profile morphol-ogy evolves with frequency (in particular, whether com-ponents shift in position or only in width) can provide a direct empirical test of these scenarios. These differences in pulse morphology should be more easily observed at lower frequencies, where PE is more pronounced. However, that is also where ISM prop-agation effects, such as dispersion and scattering, are most severe. Scattering broadens and shifts the pro-file, while residual dispersive smearing contributes addi-tional broadening, thus mimicking PE effects expected from RFM. Therefore, these effects must be separated before PE can be used to test theoretical models. In particular, low-frequency pulsar observations from the LOFAR array (van Haarlem et al. 2013) have al-ready been proven useful for analyzing pulse profiles (Kondratiev et al. 2016) and constraining ISM-induced chromatic effects (Iraci et al. 2025). Future studies could leverage this data, combining LIFD models and tech-23 niques such as cyclic spectroscopy (Dolch et al. 2021; Turner 2026), or alternative methods of quantifying time-variable scattering (Main et al. 2020) to break the covariance between the different chromatic effects.
