LeoNet: A Machine Learning Method for Binary Pulsar Classification 

Zhaocheng Gong ,1 Jack White ,1 Zeyu Yang ,1 Jayanta Roy ,2 Karel Adamek ,1 and Wesley Armour 1

> 1Oxford e-Research Centre Department of Engineering Science University of Oxford, Oxford, UK
> 2National Centre for Radio Astrophysics (NCRA), Pune 411007, Maharashtra, India

ABSTRACT Binary pulsars provide valuable laboratories for testing theories of gravity, but orbital Doppler shifts complicate their detection. Fourier-domain acceleration and jerk searches address this challenge via matched filtering, but at substantial computational cost. We present LeoNet, a convolutional neural network that uses ten learnable filters to extract features of signals affected by Doppler shifts. The resulting ten-channel feature map provides a compact, lower-dimensional alternative to an explicitly sampled acceleration–jerk response grid and is analysed by a convolutional classifier to identify can-didate signals. For simulated observations lasting 500 s, LeoNet achieves a mean relative reduction in false negative rate of 55.5% across five sampling intervals compared with the evaluated PRESTO acceleration-search configuration. TensorRT-optimised LeoNet processes each 500 s observation in 3.44–4.37 ms in FP32 on an NVIDIA H100 PCIe GPU across eight sampling intervals, including pre-processing, inference, and postprocessing. At a sampling interval of 128 μs, its mean processing time is 3.54 ms, compared with 1.767 s for PRESTO FDAS on an AMD EPYC 9825 CPU with search-frequency limits of 96–1000 Hz, corresponding to an approximately 499 × speedup in the measured processing time. These results suggest that LeoNet has the potential to improve detection perfor-mance, while its millisecond-scale processing time supports its use as a candidate-identification stage in real-time binary pulsar search pipelines.

1. INTRODUCTION Binary pulsar systems are of extraordinary significance, providing ideal laboratories for testing theories of gravity. Nevertheless, the discovery of new, unseen binary systems is highly challenging. A common approach to searching for isolated pulsars is to apply a fast Fourier transform (FFT) to a dedispersed time series and examine the resulting power spectrum. The periodic pulse train concentrates signal power into narrow peaks at the pulsar’s spin frequency and its harmonics, enabling candidate signals to be distinguished from noise. The inferred rotation period can then be used to fold the time series, aligning and combining successive pulses to reveal weak pulse profiles. In binary pulsar systems, however, orbital motion can cause the observed spin frequency to vary during an observation. The resulting frequency drift spreads signal power across neighbouring Fourier bins, weakening the spectral peaks and reducing sensitivity in searches that assume a constant spin frequency. To address this issue, Ransom et al. (2002) proposed the Fourier Domain Acceleration Search (FDAS). This method employs multiple matched filters to convolve with the signal spectrum in the frequency domain, effectively mitigating the effects of Doppler shifts. However, FDAS is computationally expensive because it requires performing a large

2number of convolutions during the search process. Since the true acceleration of a pulsar is unknown prior to the acceleration search, it is necessary to explore a broad range of potential acceleration values. This results in the convolution of the input data with many trial templates, which leads to significant computational cost, which in turn limits the method’s feasibility for real-time use. To improve sensitivity and enable the detection of pulsars in binary systems whose orbital motions cannot be accurately approximated by constant acceleration, the Fourier Domain ”Jerk” Search (FDJS) Andersen & Ransom (2018) method has been introduced. Compared to FDAS, FDJS allows for a longer observation time, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting fainter pulsars. However, FDJS requires templates spanning both acceleration and jerk, increasing the template count from n for FDAS to nm , where m is the number of jerk trials. If m scales proportionally to n, the template count grows as O(n2), substantially increasing the computational cost. With the advancement of machine learning, various AI methods have been implemented to detect pulsar signalsWang et al. (2019)Zeng et al. (2020)Yin et al. (2022)Liu et al. (2024). Solitary pulsars have distinct features that neural networks can learn, simplifying the detection process. Unfortunately, due to Doppler shifts, binary pulsars lack such obvious features, significantly increasing the difficulty of using neural networks for their detection. However, current neural network models, such as CCNN Zeng et al. (2020), AR Net Yin et al. (2022), and others Wang et al. (2019); Liu et al. (2024), are unable to correct the Doppler shift of the binary pulsar signal as effectively as FDAS, limiting their ability to detect binary pulsar signals. In this paper, we present LeoNet, a neural network architecture that uses a compact set of learnable Fourier-domain filters as an alternative to the larger matched-filter bank used by FDAS. This approach reduces the dimensionality of the search representation, aiming to capture relevant features of signals affected by Doppler shifts with fewer filters and lower computational cost. We compare LeoNet with FDAS in terms of search runtime and detection performance to assess its suitability for real-time binary pulsar searches.

METHODS 2.1. Synthetic Data Generation 

In order to build a valid model for binary pulsar detection, a large amount of data is required. Currently, only a limited number of binary pulsars have been observed. As such, constructing the training set exclusively from real binary pulsar data yields a very small dataset, which is not enough for training a model effectively. Models trained on such imbalanced datasets are not well-suited for pulsar signal search. To create a balanced training dataset, we need to generate synthetic data. We use the fake SIGPROC program Lorimer (2011) to simulate the signal of binary pulsar systems. It is important to highlight that this will produce data with a fixed pulse profile (square pulses) in both the time and frequency domains. The amplitude of the square wave is determined by snrpeak . The parameters used to generate our synthetic training dataset, along with sampling distribution used for each parameter, are shown in Table 1 The distributions used have been selected based on the known distributions observed in binary pulsar systems. To ensure stable training, we set a minimum snrpeak of 0.04. Including too many samples below this threshold causes the network to waste iterations fitting noise rather than learning pulsar features, which both slows convergence and reduces generalisation. For data without pulsar signals, we set the value of SNR equal to 0 to obtain pure noise samples. This project specifically targets millisecond pulsars. When generating our simulated dataset, we restrict spin fre-quencies to the range 100–1000 Hz, since every known binary pulsar spins below 1000 Hz Manchester et al. (2005). However, the upper bound is further constrained by the Nyquist frequency corresponding to the chosen sampling time, 

fNyq = 1 /(2 ∗ tsamp ). FDAS matches the Fourier spectrum against templates that assume constant acceleration during an observation. When the acceleration varies appreciably, the signal’s phase evolution departs from this approximation, causing tem-plate mismatch and reducing the recovered matched-filter response. This motivates the use of learned features that are not restricted to a prescribed family of constant-acceleration responses. LeoNet uses ten convolutional templates learned from the training data to represent Fourier-domain structures associated with both acceleration and jerk. Their response magnitudes form a two-dimensional feature map across frequency positions and template channels, providing a compact, lower-dimensional alternative to an explicitly sampled acceleration–jerk response grid for candidate classification. The template layer provides this representation to a subsequent classification network, which combines information across channels and neighbouring frequency positions to identify candidate signals. Figure 1 illustrates this approach using a simulated binary pulsar in an eccentric orbit. The signal is generated and injected using SPECTRALib White (2026) into a Gaussian-noise filterbank produced using SIGPROC. The injected and noise-only filterbanks are dedispersed using PRESTO, providing identical input time series for the two methods. The 500 s observation spans periastron, during which the orbital acceleration and jerk vary. The FDAS response is distributed across trial frequencies and drift parameters, while the signal injection produces changes across several of LeoNet’s template channels. These changes are displayed relative to the same noise-only control to make their structure easier to distinguish from the background. For this simulated observation, LeoNet detects the injected signal, whereas the PRESTO FDAS search with zmax = 200, harmonic summing up to 16 harmonics, and search-frequency limits of 96–1000 Hz does not return a corresponding candidate. This selected example illustrates the potential of learned templates combined with a classification network to identify signals whose phase evolution is poorly represented by the constant-acceleration approximation. 4

The response differences in Figure 1 are used only for visualisation. The original template-response magnitudes form the input to the classification network, whose implementation is described in Section 2.5. 2.4. Training Dataset Construction 

To prepare the dataset for LeoNet, we first perform de-dispersion on the raw filterbank files to obtain the time series data. The de-dispersed time series is then Fourier transformed, and the spectrum is normalised by dividing by the total number of samples. The number of points in the Fourier spectrum can be extremely large. For instance, with a sampling time of 128 μsand an observation length of 500 s, the positive frequency spectrum contains 1 ,953 ,126 points. The large input size imposes substantial computational and memory requirements, making direct processing of the full spectrum by a neural network computationally demanding. To address this, we divide the spectrum into smaller blocks. 5Choosing the block size is critical: if the blocks are too long, computation becomes inefficient, whereas blocks that are too short may not capture the full spectral features of binary pulsars, reducing detection performance. In this work, we set the block length to 4000 points as a practical compromise between computational efficiency and spectral coverage. At an observation length of 500 s, the Fourier bin width is ∆ f = 1 /T obs = 0 .002 Hz, so a 4000-point block spans 8 Hz in frequency. We verified on test chirp signals that this length is sufficient to preserve the characteristic structure of binary pulsars. The total number of blocks, therefore, depends on both the observation time and the sampling time. Since the maximum frequency of interest is given by min(1000 Hz , f Nyq ), where fNyq = 1 /(2 tsamp ) is the Nyquist frequency, the number of blocks varies for different sampling times. When the observation time Tobs is shorter than 500 s, the Fourier bin size increases accordingly. To ensure consistency across the dataset, we zero-pad shorter observations to 500 s before applying the Fourier transform. In addition, each block is extended with 100 overlapping points from its neighbouring regions. This overlap-padding ensures that convolution with templates, which have a length of 201 points, does not lose signal power at the block boundaries. The choice of 201-point templates corresponds to a maximum drift parameter of zmax = 200, since Fourier-domain “jerk” searches have shown that most binary pulsars are detected with |z| < 200 (and |w| < 600) Andersen & Ransom (2018). During training, since the fundamental spin frequency of each synthetic pulsar is known, only the block containing the corresponding frequency is extracted to construct the training and validation sets. In contrast, during inference, the pulsar frequency is unknown, so all blocks are processed by the neural network to ensure that signals can be detected regardless of their location in the spectrum. 2.5. LeoNet Architecture 

LeoNet consists of two main stages: a template layer that extracts features from the complex Fourier spectrum, and a classification network that analyses the resulting template responses to identify candidate signals. The architecture is shown in Figure 2. The model receives a complex-valued Fourier block containing 4200 points, represented by separate real and imag-inary components, together with a six-dimensional conditioning vector describing the observing configuration. This vector contains a five-way one-hot encoding of the sampling-time category and the normalised observation duration, 

eTobs = ( Tobs − 100 s) /(400 s). The template layer contains ten learnable templates, with the template count selected through a Neural Architecture Search (NAS) procedure. Each template is implemented using paired real-valued convolutional kernels of length 201. The convolutions operate along the frequency dimension with unit stride and zero-padding of 100 points on each side, preserving the input length. Their outputs are combined to form complex responses, whose magnitudes produce a 10 × 4200 frequency–template feature map. This map is supplied to the classification network as a single-channel input, with template index and frequency position forming its two spatial dimensions. The classification network contains five two-dimensional convolutional layers: two with 5 × 5 kernels, followed by three with 3 × 3 kernels. All convolutions use unit stride and padding that preserves the spatial dimensions before pooling. The first four layers produce eight feature channels, and the final layer produces one. Each convolution is followed by batch normalisation, a ReLU activation, and max pooling with a kernel size and stride of (1 , 2), with the FiLM operation inserted before the first ReLU as described below. Pooling progressively reduces the resolution along the frequency axis while preserving the template dimension. A feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) operation is applied after the first batch normalisation layer and before the ReLU activation. An MLP maps the conditioning vector through hidden layers of widths 32 and 16, followed by two linear heads that generate eight channel-wise scaling and shifting parameters, γ and β. These parameters modulate the intermediate features according to X′ = X ⊙ (1 + γ) + β, with γ and β broadcast across the template and frequency dimensions. This provides the classification network with observing-configuration information during feature processing. The final CNN feature map is flattened into a 1310-dimensional vector and concatenated with a 16-dimensional embedding of the conditioning vector. This separate conditioning branch consists of a linear layer, a ReLU activation, and dropout. The resulting 1326-dimensional vector is passed through a fully connected layer with 512 hidden units, followed by ReLU activation, dropout, and a final linear layer producing a single logit. A sigmoid function converts the logit into a classification score between zero and one for the input frequency block. 6

2.6. Training Strategy 

We adopt stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with Nesterov momentum and a warm-up plus cosine annealing learning rate schedule to ensure smooth convergence. Weight decay and dropout regularisation are applied to prevent overfitting and improve generalisation. We train LeoNet using binary cross-entropy with logits, implemented as BCEWithLogitsLoss . Although the training dataset is balanced, we set pos weight to 2.0, doubling the contribution of positive samples to the loss to place greater emphasis on avoiding missed pulsar signals. Detection thresholds are calibrated separately using noise samples to limit false positives during inference. The optimiser and loss function are combined with a dynamic threshold calibration strategy. At the end of each epoch, the validation set is used to estimate both a global and configuration-specific detection threshold by computing the 99.99999th percentile of the model’s scores on noise samples, where each configuration corresponds to a unique combination of tsamp and Tobs . The calibrated threshold map is applied during evaluation to maintain consistent false positive control across different observing configurations while preserving sensitivity to genuine binary pulsar signals. During training, the model performance is monitored using the F1 score, false positive rate (FPR), and false negative rate (FNR). The model weights are saved after each epoch, and the final model used for inference corresponds to the epoch with the lowest validation loss. EVALUATION 3.1. FDAS 

In pulsar signal searches using the FDAS, harmonic summing is applied to improve detection sensitivity for low-duty-cycle pulsars. Pulsar signals with narrow pulse profiles distribute their energy across multiple harmonics in the frequency domain. To recover these signals, FDAS coherently sums the power of several harmonics, thereby enhancing the overall SNR and improving the likelihood of detection. In PRESTO’s accelsearch , the number of harmonics to be summed is specified by the -numharm parameter. In-creasing this value can improve sensitivity for pulsars with strong harmonic structures, but also increases computational cost. In practical applications, PRESTO’s candidate sifting procedure uses a default significance threshold of σ = 6 to reduce false positives. 8For the evaluation in this work, FDAS is executed using the acceleration routine in PRESTO with a maximum acceleration search parameter of −zmax = 200 and harmonic summing set to −numharm = 16. This configuration is adopted to evaluate the performance of FDAS, whilst retaining a realistic computational budget that could be incorporated into a real-time pipeline, and to provide a consistent baseline for comparison with LeoNet. 3.2. For fmax = 1000 Hz, each observation therefore contains 113 evaluated blocks. LeoNet independently evaluates each block, and a binary pulsar is considered detected if any block’s output proba-bility exceeds the defined threshold. This procedure ensures that the model can locate pulsar candidates regardless of where the harmonic appears within the spectrum. Before inference, a threshold calibration step is performed to ensure reliable and consistent detection performance. For each combination of sampling time and observation time, over 200,000 pure noise blocks are evaluated using the trained network. For each observing configuration, we record the maximum pre-sigmoid logit obtained from the calibration noise samples and add a safety margin to provide an additional buffer. An excessively large margin would raise the detection threshold and could increase the number of missed signals. We therefore use a small, fixed margin of 0.1 in logit space before applying the sigmoid function to obtain the final detection threshold. The resulting threshold exceeds all outputs in the calibration noise set, but does not guarantee a zero false positive rate (FPR) on unseen data. LeoNet searches for individual harmonic components without applying harmonic summing during evaluation. Conse-quently, it does not explicitly combine signal power across harmonics, which may limit its sensitivity to low-duty-cycle pulsars when individual harmonics are too weak to exceed the detection threshold. To quantitatively assess LeoNet’s performance, several standard classification metrics are employed, including recall, precision, F1 score and FNR, defined as: Recall = TP TP + FN , Precision = TP TP + FP ,F 1 = 2 · Precision · Recall Precision + Recall , FNR = FN TP + FN ,

Here, TP, TN, FP, and FN represent true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative samples, respectively. Precision measures the proportion of correctly identified binary pulsar signals among all detections and is crucial for large-scale pulsar surveys dominated by noise. Recall quantifies the model’s ability to identify actual pulsars, and accuracy reflects the overall correctness of the predictions. The F1 score, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, provides a balanced measure of performance, especially when both false negatives and class imbalance must be carefully managed. This evaluation framework allows LeoNet to be consistently assessed under varying sampling and observation times. For application to new telescopes or backends, the detection thresholds should be recalibrated using representative noise data, and sensitivity and false positive rates should be assessed on independent test observations. 3.3. DISCUSSION 4.1. Sensitivity Comparison of FDAS and LeoNet 

Figure 4 compares the mean FNR of FDAS and LeoNet over observation durations of 200–500 s for five sampling intervals. At Tobs = 200 s, FDAS performs comparably to LeoNet at tsamp = 128 μs and achieves a lower mean FNR at 256 μs. These results are consistent with the constant-acceleration approximation used by FDAS. For the simulated orbital periods of 1–10 h, a 200 s observation gives Tobs /P orb ≤ 0.056, satisfying the commonly used criterion 

Tobs ≲ Porb /10 Andersen & Ransom (2018). Over such a short portion of the orbit, the line-of-sight acceleration can be approximated as constant, allowing FDAS templates to match the resulting frequency drift. For longer observations, changes in line-of-sight acceleration can reduce the accuracy of this approximation, while the longer integration allows more signal to accumulate. The measured FDAS trends vary with the sampling interval: its mean FNR increases with observation duration at 128 and 256 μs, decreases initially and then increases at 512 μs, and decreases at 1024 and 1310 μs. LeoNet’s mean FNR decreases across all five configurations, although the additional improvement between 400 and 500 s is small at 256 and 1310 μs. At every tested duration from 300 to 500 s, LeoNet achieves a lower mean FNR than FDAS across all five sampling intervals. Tables 3 and 4 quantify the comparison at Tobs = 500 s. LeoNet achieves lower FNR and higher recall and F1 scores at every sampling interval under the adopted detection thresholds. Averaging the relative changes calculated separately for the five sampling intervals gives a 55.5% reduction in FNR and a 26.2% increase in F1 score. The relative FNR reduction ranges from 26.5% at 1310 μs to 87.4% at 128 μs. Both methods show higher FNRs at coarser sampling intervals. We next examine the properties of signals detected by FDAS and those detected only by LeoNet. Figure 5 separates the detection outcomes by duty cycle at Tobs = 500 s for all five sampling intervals. Each bar distinguishes signals detected by both methods, detected only by FDAS, detected only by LeoNet, and missed by both. The fraction detected only by LeoNet exceeds the fraction detected only by FDAS in every displayed duty-cycle bin. Thus, LeoNet achieves a higher detection fraction within each bin, although some signals remain detectable only by FDAS. At tsamp = 128 μs, LeoNet recovers nearly all signals with duty cycles above approximately 15%, while its missed detections are concentrated towards lower duty cycles. At 256 and 512 μs, the FDAS-only detections are also most apparent in the lowest duty-cycle bins. At 1024 and 1310 μs, a larger fraction of signals is missed by both methods, including at higher duty cycles. LeoNet continues to recover more signals in each bin, but small FDAS-only fractions persist over a broader duty-cycle range. 10

Robustness to RFI-contaminated data 

We evaluated LeoNet and PRESTO on MARS-mitigated observations at five sampling intervals using the procedure described in Section 3.3. Each dataset initially contained 1,000 observations, and the first Tobs = 500 s of each observation were searched. At tsamp = 128 μs, four observations with severe RFI contamination were excluded because their time series were unusable for pulsar searches after RFI mitigation. At tsamp = 256 and 512 μs, respectively, 17 and 19 processed outputs were excluded because they contained non-finite or extreme values, or fell outside the expected output distribution. All 1,000 observations were retained at both tsamp = 1024 μs and tsamp = 1310 μs. Both methods were evaluated on the same retained observations at each sampling interval. Table 6 compares LeoNet and PRESTO on the retained MARS-mitigated observations across all five sampling intervals. 14 At tsamp = 128 μs, LeoNet recovered 911 target signals after variance matching, compared with 762 for PRESTO, yielding recalls of 91.47% and 76.51%, respectively. LeoNet therefore achieved 14.96 percentage points higher recall. It also produced fewer FP-contaminated files: 44 (4.42%) compared with 89 (8.94%) for PRESTO, a reduction of 4.52 percentage points. LeoNet achieved higher recall than PRESTO at the remaining sampling intervals, with improvements of 11.50, 11.21, 12.00, and 10.80 percentage points at tsamp = 256, 512, 1024, and 1310 μs, respectively. However, LeoNet also produced a higher FP-file fraction in these configurations. Thus, although LeoNet recovered more target signals at every tested sampling interval, its lower candidate contamination relative to PRESTO was limited to the 128 μs configuration. The frequency coverage varied with sampling interval. Because LeoNet processes complete 8-Hz blocks, its input blocks covered 96–1000 Hz at tsamp = 128 and 256 μs, 96–976 Hz at 512 μs, 96–488 Hz at 1024 μs, and 96–376 Hz at 1310 μs. For false-positive statistics, candidates were counted only when their reported frequencies lay within the target evaluation range, from 100 Hz to fmax = min(1000 Hz , f Nyq ), where fNyq is the Nyquist frequency. Some residual LeoNet false positives may arise from harmonics of RFI below this evaluation range. For example, an RFI signal with a fundamental frequency of 60 Hz may produce harmonics at 120 and 180 Hz. Although the fundamental is outside the evaluation range, these harmonics are inside it and may be identified by LeoNet as pulsar candidates. Our comb-aware birdie zapping requires at least three detectable peaks belonging to the same harmonic series. It may therefore leave such interference unflagged when only one or two peaks satisfy the detection criterion. At coarser sampling intervals, the lower Nyquist frequency can further reduce the number of observable harmonics available to identify a comb. PRESTO may report the same interference at a different frequency. Its harmonic summing combines power from multiple harmonics and may report a candidate at the underlying fundamental, such as 60 Hz in this example. That candidate falls below our evaluation range and is therefore excluded from the false-positive count. This can explain why an RFI signal produces a counted false positive for LeoNet but not for PRESTO. The FP-file fractions in the table nevertheless reflect all counted false-positive candidates across the evaluated observations, so these individual cases do not imply that PRESTO always produces fewer FP-contaminated files. 4.4. Timing 

The inference efficiency of LeoNet can be improved using specialised optimisations. We evaluate three execution back-ends: vanilla PyTorch, TorchScript followed by torch.jit.optimize for inference (JIT-OFI), and Torch-TensorRT using its TorchScript frontend. JIT-OFI applies inference-oriented graph optimisations, while Torch-TensorRT com-piles supported operations into GPU-optimised TensorRT engines. We assess performance using the measured processing time per observation. The benchmarks use 500 s dedispersed time series with sampling intervals of 49.152, 64, 81.92, 128, 256, 512, 1024, and 1310 μs. For each sampling interval, the same input observation is used for LeoNet and PRESTO. LeoNet is evaluated on three NVIDIA GPUs: the RTX 3090, A100 PCIe 40 GB, and H100 PCIe. During inference, the batch size is set to 128. The LeoNet timings include all processing stages from the dedispersed time series to the detection records: FFT computation and normalisation, frequency-block extraction, FiLM conditioning, batch assembly, network inference, sigmoid evaluation, thresholding, and detection-record construction. The input time series is resident in GPU memory before timing begins. Transfers between CPU and GPU memory are excluded in both directions. This measurement targets integration into a GPU-based processing pipeline, in which upstream operations such as dedispersion have already produced data which would be resident in GPU memory. Disk reads, model loading and compilation, and result-file writing are excluded. For comparison, the Fourier Domain Acceleration Search is performed using PRESTO’s accelsearch on an AMD EPYC 9825 processor, with -zmax 200 and harmonic summing up to 16 harmonics. To select the number of CPU threads, we performed a thread sweep over 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 96, 144, 192, and 288 threads ( -ncpus ). Using 16 threads gave the lowest mean processing time for all eight sampling intervals, so all PRESTO timings reported below use 16 threads. To restrict the nominal search band to that considered by LeoNet, PRESTO is run with -flo 96 -fhi 1000 . These parameters specify the search-frequency limits for the highest harmonic in PRESTO’s harmonic-summing convention. For sampling intervals whose Nyquist frequency is below 1000 Hz, the available frequency coverage is correspondingly reduced. The input and output files used by PRESTO are placed in a memory-backed filesystem (tmpfs ) to reduce storage-related overhead. 15

CONCLUSION We have presented LeoNet, a neural network architecture for the efficient detection of binary pulsar signals. Its template layer uses a compact set of learnable Fourier-domain filters to extract features of signals affected by Doppler shifts, providing an alternative to the larger matched-filter bank used by FDAS and FDJS. On synthetic data without RFI at Tobs = 500 s, LeoNet achieved a mean relative reduction in FNR of approximately 55.5% compared with FDAS across the five evaluated sampling intervals. At tsamp = 128 μs, LeoNet also reduced the FNR by 35.5% relative to the GPU implementation of Pulscan operating at σ = 7. Evaluation on synthetic RFI-contaminated observations further demonstrated the applicability of LeoNet after MARS mitigation and the additional preprocessing described in this work. LeoNet achieved higher recall than PRESTO across all five sampling intervals, with improvements of 10.80–14.96 percentage points. Its FP-file fraction was lower than that of PRESTO at tsamp = 128 μs, but higher at the remaining sampling intervals. Residual RFI therefore remains a source of candidate contamination despite the improvement in target recovery. Inference optimisation further reduced the processing time of LeoNet. Across the three NVIDIA GPU types and eight sampling intervals tested, TensorRT achieved a geometric-mean speedup of 2 .12 × relative to vanilla PyTorch in the FP32 configuration. For a 500 s observation sampled at 128 μs, LeoNet required a mean processing time of 3.54 ms on the NVIDIA H100 PCIe, compared with 1.767 s for PRESTO FDAS using 16 threads on an AMD EPYC 9825 CPU with search-frequency limits configured to 96–1000 Hz. Under these timing conditions, the results demonstrate the low per-observation processing cost of LeoNet and support its potential for integration into GPU-based real-time binary pulsar search pipelines. LeoNet remains limited in recovering some short-duty-cycle signals and rejecting residual periodic RFI. Future work will investigate incorporating harmonic information, improving residual RFI rejection, extending the training data to cover a wider range of pulsar parameters, and validating the approach on real observations from different telescopes and backends.
