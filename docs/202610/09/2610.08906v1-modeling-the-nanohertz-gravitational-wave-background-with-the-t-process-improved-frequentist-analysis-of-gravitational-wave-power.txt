ABSTRACT 

Pulsar timing array (PTA) collaborations have recently reported evidence for a gravitational wave background (GWB) signal in the nanohertz band, which most likely originates from the incoherent superposition of GWs emitted by a population of supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs). Since the number of binaries emitting in each frequency bin is finite, and since individual loud sources can dominate the GWB, the power spectral density (PSD) of the background is expected to deviate significantly from the smooth power law that describes an idealized population. PTA searches usually describe the PSD either with a power law or with a free spectrum model, which fits the PSD in each frequency bin as a free parameter. In this paper, we explore the t-process as a third model; it assumes a power law as the underlying spectrum but leaves each frequency bin free to deviate from it. We compare the performance of these three models on simulated datasets, injecting 100 realizations of a realistic SMBHB population, to evaluate how well each model recovers a complex PSD. Using Bayesian and frequentist tools, we find that the t-process model gives the most precise PSD results, with uncertainties 43% and 26% narrower than those obtained with the power law and the free spectrum, respectively, while the improvement in accuracy is smaller. Moreover, using the t-process model, we compute isotropic and anisotropic signal-to-noise ratios with median gains of about 46% over the power law and 65% over the free spectrum in the first nine frequency bins. The t-process also returns lower anisotropy p-values and among the frequency bins with p < 0.01, it allows to localize the loudest binary within the angular resolution of the sky maps in 14 .3% of the cases, compared to the 10% for the power law and the 6 .2% for the free spectrum.

1. Introduction 

In recent years, evidence at di ff erent significance levels for a gravitational-wave background (GWB) in the nanohertz (nHz) regime has been reported by multiple pulsar timing array (PTA) collaborations, including the North American Nanohertz Observa-tory for Gravitational Waves (NANOGrav; Agazie et al. 2023a), the European and Indian collaborations (EPTA and InPTA; EPTA Collaboration et al. 2023), the Parkes PTA (PPTA; Reardon et al. 2023), the Chinese PTA (CPTA; Xu et al. 2023) and the MeerKAT PTA (MPTA; Miles et al. 2025). Moreover, a comparison of di ff er-ent datasets shows that the results are mutually consistent (Agazie et al. 2024). In PTA experiments, the stochastic GWB, expected to be the dominant signal in the nHz regime, appears in the times of arrival (ToAs) of rapidly spinning millisecond pulsars as low-frequency red noise that is common to all pulsars of the array. The signal itself is correlated between pulsars as a function of their angular separation on the sky, following the Hellings and Downs (HD) function, as predicted by Hellings & Downs (1983). Although the most likely origin of this signal is the incoherent superposition of the GWs emitted by a population of supermas-sive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) forming after galaxy mergers (Rajagopal & Romani 1995; Ja ff e & Backer 2003; Wyithe & Loeb 2003; Sesana et al. 2008; Rosado et al. 2015; Bécsy et al. 2022), the observed GWB can also be produced by cosmic strings (Kibble 1976; Damour & Vilenkin 2000), inflationary scenarios

Determining which of these mech-anisms produces the observed signal is therefore a major focus of the field. Among the properties that can help identify the origin of the signal are its spectral shape and its power distribution in the sky. Considering an idealized population of circular SMBHBs whose evolution is driven by GW emission only, the characteristic strain is expected to follow a power law (PL) as described in Phinney (2001). However, for a realistic population, the number of binaries emitting in each frequency bin is finite and, especially at high frequencies, the spectrum can deviate significantly from this PL approximation. Exceptionally loud binaries can add power in the specific bins in which they emit and even become individually resolvable (Sesana et al. 2009; Ravi et al. 2012; Kelley et al. 2017; Bécsy et al. 2023; Ferranti et al. 2025; Bécsy et al. 2025). In addition, the coupling between the binaries and their stellar or gaseous environment, as well as the eccentricity of their orbits, tend to suppress power at low frequencies and flatten the spectrum (Sesana 2013; Ravi et al. 2014; Sampson et al. 2015; Fastidio et al. 2024; Valtolina et al. 2024). Detecting these deviations from a PL would be a strong hint in favor of the SMBHB interpretation. The discrete nature of the source population can also make the GWB anisotropic. A background produced by a finite number of SMBHBs is not uniformly distributed in the sky, and the loudest arXiv:2610.08906v1 [astro-ph.GA] 6 Oct 2026 2binaries produce hot spots on the GW power maps (Mingarelli et al. 2013; Taylor et al. 2015, 2020). Several methods borrowed from the analysis of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) have been adapted to PTA data to describe these features (Ölmez et al. 2012; Gair et al. 2014; Mingarelli et al. 2017), and searches for anisotropy have been carried out on real datasets with so far inconclusive results (Taylor & Gair 2013; Agazie et al. 2023c; Grunthal et al. 2025). The sensitivity of current and future arrays to anisotropy has also been studied in detail (Pol et al. 2022; Depta et al. 2025), since detecting it and resolving an individual binary remain among the main goals of the field (Agazie et al. 2023b; EPTA Collaboration et al. 2024; Miles et al. 2025). Inferring this information from the data requires a model that adequately describes the GWB power spectrum. The two standard models used in the literature are the PL and the free spectrum (FS). The former cannot detect any deviation from its own shape, since it is described by its amplitude and spectral index, and it has an inherent relation of power between di ff erent frequency bins. The latter fits the power in each frequency bin as a free parameter and therefore can model deviations from the PL. How-ever, it analyzes each bin in isolation, ignoring by construction any possible correlation with other bins. The putative SMBHB origin of the signal suggests an alternative description, falling in between these two. Since di ff erent binaries emit at di ff erent frequencies, the power in each bin should be free to deviate from a continuous analytic function. At the same time, the fact that each individual SMBHB belongs to a collective cosmic popula-tion suggests some underlying regularity in the overall functional form of the signal. A model that can better accommodate the nature of a GWB sourced by a cosmic population of SMBHBs is the t-process (TP). This model uses a PL as the underlying description of the spectrum but introduces a per-bin parametric deviation from it, o ff ering a compromise between continuity and flexibility. The TP was originally introduced to describe an excess of power in the red noise of a single pulsar (Aggarwal et al. 2019; Arzoumanian et al. 2020) and was later applied to the common GWB signal (Agazie et al. 2023a). Sardesai et al. (2024) studied the TP systematically, comparing it with the PL and the FS on simulated datasets, and showed that the TP recovers the injected power spectral density (PSD) even when it deviates from a PL. The TP is therefore a useful model to describe the GWB produced by a realistic SMBHB population. That study models the com-mon signal as a common uncorrelated red-noise (CURN) process and does not fit the HD correlations between pulsars. In this article, we test the TP on a GWB signal produced by a realistic population of SMBHBs, whose discrete nature produces both deviations from a PL and sky anisotropies. We infer the signal PSD using both a Bayesian estimate of the CURN and the frequentist per-frequency optimal statistic (PFOS) (Gersbach et al. 2025), considering pair covariance and noise marginalization. The PFOS allows us not only to estimate the power and shape of the HD-correlated common signal, but also to build maps of the GW power across the sky in individual frequency bins through frequentist anisotropic methods (Pol et al. 2022). This is useful to localize the loudest continuous gravitational waves (CGWs) and measure the anisotropic and isotropic signal-to-noise ratios (S /Ns) (Gersbach et al. 2026; Konstandin et al. 2026). The TP was also suggested by Gersbach et al. (2026) as a model to be used in the Bayesian CURN search, since the choice of the spectral model assumed in the initial spectral estimate af-fects the result of the PFOS. In this work, we test this suggestion by fitting the data with the three models: TP, PL and FS. We show that the TP model allows us to recover, in the PFOS analysis, a more precise estimate, with also modest improvements in ac-curacy, of the injected power of a realistic SMBHB population than the PL or the FS. Moreover, it results in higher isotropic and anisotropic S /Ns. In particular, among the frequency bins with significant anisotropy, the TP gives the largest fraction of bins in which the loudest binary is localized. The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. 2 we present the simulated PTA, the injected SMBHB populations and the three models used to characterize the GWB spectrum. In Sec. 3 we describe the Bayesian and fre-quentist tools used to recover the GWB signal and to build the sky maps. In Sec. 4 we present the results of the analysis and the statistics comparing the three models over 100 realistic SMBHB populations. In Sec. 5 we summarize our findings and present our conclusions. 

2. Simulated PTA dataset 

The main goal of this project is to test the ability of the TP to model the GWB spectrum and compare its performance with that of the PL and FS. We therefore require a dataset with a known injected signal to measure how well each model is able to recover it. In this section, we describe the simulated PTA dataset and the realistic SMBHB populations that we injected. We then define the three models used in our analysis to describe the PSD of the GWB. 

> 2.1. Pulsar noise and realistic GWB injection

Our simulated PTA consists of 100 pulsars randomly distributed across the sky, at a distance of 1 kpc, and observed with a ca-dence of 14 days for 30 years. We construct the array using the timing model of a real millisecond pulsar as a template. We simu-late ToAs with the libstempo Python wrapper (Vallisneri 2020) for the Tempo2 pulsar timing package (Hobbs et al. 2006; Ed-wards et al. 2006), generating ToAs at the two observing radio frequencies of 1400 and 2200 MHz. To simulate and analyze a large number of realizations within a reasonable computational time, we inject only white noise (WN) into the PTA, which we model as a stationary Gaussian process. Other stochastic noise processes, such as red noise (RN) and noise linked to variations of the dispersion measure (DM, see e.g. van Haasteren et al. 2009; Lentati et al. 2013), are not injected and will be included in future studies. WN has the property of having the same power at all frequencies and is described by two main parameters: an error scaling factor (EFAC, EF ), which multiplies the uncertainty of the ToA, and an error added in quadrature (EQUAD, EQ), which describes additional contributions such as instrumental e ff ects or pulse profile jitter (Verbiest & Shaifullah 2018, and references therein). The TP is therefore described by the two parameters of the underlying PL model plus an extra αi parameter for each fre-quency bin. To illustrate the behavior of the αi parameters, a value log αi = 0 means that the PSD in that bin agrees with the underlying PL, while a value significantly di ff erent from zero quantifies the deviation from it: if log αi > 0, the PSD measured is higher than the PL measurement, while if log αi < 0, the mea-sured PSD is lower. The name of the model comes from the prior used on the Fourier coe ffi cients. In standard RN models these coe ffi cients have a Gaussian prior whose variance is set by the PL PSD; this defines a Gaussian process. In the TP, the variance is not fixed by the PL alone but is rescaled by αi, which is itself a random variable with an inverse gamma prior. If we marginalize the Gaussian over αi, we obtain a Student’s t-distribution for the coe ffi cients. The TP can therefore be viewed as a process in which marginalizing over the inverse-gamma variance scale gives Student’s t-distributed Fourier coe ffi cients. The TP was not initially applied to model the GWB. It was introduced to model the RN of a single pulsar. In particular, Aggarwal et al. (2019) searched the NANOGrav 11-year dataset for possible continuous GWs from individual SMBHBs and found that the then observed increase in the strain upper limit at fgw =

15 nHz was generated by the pulsar PSR J0613 −0200. A dropout analysis found a much larger Bayes factor for that pulsar than for all the others. Thus, the authors concluded that the increase in the upper limit was produced by an unmodeled feature in PSR J0613 −0200 and not by a GW signal. Arzoumanian et al. (2020) analyzed the same pulsar while searching for a continuous GW from the candidate SMBHB 3C 66B. The excess of power in PSR J0613 −0200 RN made the sampling of the CGW frequency unreliable. Because the pulsar could not simply be removed from the array without losing sensitivity to the target search, its RN was described with a more flexible model, the TP spectrum, in which the Gaussian process prior was indeed generalized to a Student’s t-distribution. The purpose was therefore to correctly model the excess of power in one frequency bin of that pulsar. This is possible because the heavy tail of the inverse gamma prior (Fig. 2) allows the αi of a single bin to assume large values. A few years later, Agazie et al. (2023a) applied this "fuzzy" model to search for the GWB in the NANOGrav 15-year dataset. Here the model was used to test how robust the recovered spectral index was against any deviations from a pure PL model. Finally, Sardesai et al. To summarize the history, the TP was initially applied to describe an excess of power present in a single pulsar’s RN in a specific frequency bin on top of a smooth PL process. Then it was used to model the GWB, since we expect a similar signature in multiple frequency bins when the signal is sourced by a realistic SMBHB population.

In this section we describe the Bayesian and frequentist tech-niques used to recover the GWB signal and to build sky maps of the GW power at di ff erent frequency bins.

> 3.2. Frequentist GWB power reconstruction

In this work, we paid particular attention to two aspects of the PFOS procedure that are not present in the original formulation of the optimal statistic. First, in our analysis we consider the covariance between pulsar pairs, thus we assume a non-diagonal covariance matrix C j. The "standard" OS works in the weak-signal regime, in which the GWB has a negligible contribution to the total noise of each pulsar, and in that limit the covariance of the pairwise estimators can be taken as diagonal. This assumption is not valid once the GWB becomes the dominant red noise con-tribution in the lowest frequency bins. The o ff -diagonal elements grow with the strength of the signal, and dropping them returns an estimator whose variance is too small (Gersbach et al. 2025). Since in our analysis the injected background is loud in the lowest frequency bins, we replace the diagonal approximation with the full pulsar-pair covariance matrix defined as:

Following the noise-marginalized ap-proach of Vigeland et al. (2018), we instead evaluate the statistic many times over draws from the posterior of the common sig-nal, obtaining a distribution of the recovered PSD described in Eq. (18) rather than a single number. In this work we use 500 random draws, taken from the posterior obtained with the varia-tional inference described in Sect. 3.1. Although it is called noise marginalization, in this work it is performed over the CURN pa-rameters, since in the OS method the CURN is treated as a noise process. Noise marginalization alone, however, is not enough. Each draw returns an estimate of the power together with its own uncertainty σ ˆS ( f j),0, and keeping only the value of ˆ S ( f j) while discarding the uncertainties makes the final distribution narrower than it really is. We therefore also apply the uncertainty sampling proposed by Gersbach et al. (2025): every noise-marginalized estimate is replaced by a set of random draws from a Gaussian centered on that estimate ˆ S ( f j), with a standard deviation equal to its uncertainty σ ˆS ( f j),0. The resulting distribution accounts for both the spread of the CURN chain and the uncertainty of the es-timator itself. Thus, throughout this work, we use the PFOS with the pulsar-pair covariance (PC), the noise marginalization and the uncertainty sampling (NM). For brevity, we retain the name PFOS throughout, but here it denotes the PFOS evaluated with pulsar-pair covariance, noise marginalization, and uncertainty sampling. We therefore run the whole procedure three times for each pop-ulation, providing to the PFOS the CURN chains obtained by modeling the GWB with the PL, the FS, and the TP model, so that each spectral model produces its own reconstruction of the correlated power.

4. Results 

In this section we present the results of the analysis. We first show the PSD recovered using the Bayesian approach, and then the GWB power computed by applying the PFOS analysis. We repeat the same procedure on 100 di ff erent populations, and we compare results using TP, FS, and PL models. 

> 4.1. Bayesian and frequentist results

As a first step, we analyze each simulated dataset modeling the GWB as a TP, FS, and PL process, in all three cases as a CURN signal, computing the posterior distributions of the GWB pa-rameters through the Bayesian analysis described in Sect. 3.1. Fig. 3 shows the spectra recovered in one SMBHB realization, together with the power that was injected in each frequency bin, as estimated from Eq. (4). At low frequencies, where the GWB dominates, the TP and FS results are consistent with each other and with the injected PSD. At high frequencies, where WN be-gins to dominate, the FS results become uninformative, whereas the TP remains constrained and consistent with the injected PSD because it is tied to the underlying PL shape. The PL model is not able to follow the injected PSD in each bin, since it is constrained to a PL shape that cannot capture any deviations from it. These results are not completely new; Sardesai et al. (2024) analyzed simulated datasets based on the NANOGrav 15 yr array and found that the FS recovers the injected PSD at low frequency but loses information at the high frequencies where the WN dom-inates, while the TP keeps following the injected spectrum. We therefore do not repeat the characterization of the Bayesian poste-riors and we do not build any statistic at this stage of the analysis. We instead use these CURN chains only as input for the frequen-tist analysis, in which we compare the HD power recovered with

Two models are commonly used in PTA searches: the PL and FS models. The former cannot follow these deviations, while the latter can, but constrains each frequency bin using only the information available in that bin; high-frequency bins can therefore become uninformative where WN dominates. The aim of this work was to assess whether a third model, the TP, provides a better description of the GWB. The TP keeps a PL as the underlying description of the spectrum and allows each frequency bin to deviate from it through a multi-plicative factor with an inverse-gamma prior. The model was first introduced to describe an excess of power in the RN of a single pulsar (Aggarwal et al. 2019; Arzoumanian et al. 2020) and was later applied to the common signal (Agazie et al. 2023a; Sardesai et al. 2024). To compare the three models we built a simulated PTA of 100 pulsars observed for 30 years with a cadence of 14 days, in which the WN is the only injected noise process. We then injected 100 realizations of a realistic SMBHB population in the time domain. Each of the 100 datasets was then analyzed three times, modeling the GWB as a PL, as a FS and as a TP. The analysis consists of three main steps. First, we estimated the CURN posterior distri-butions of each spectral model with variational inference. Second, we used these chains as the input of the PFOS, with the pulsar-pair covariance, noise marginalization, and uncertainty sampling, obtaining the HD correlated power in each frequency bin. The Bayesian analysis confirmed what found by Sardesai et al. (2024). At low frequency the TP and the FS agree with each other and with the injected PSD, while at high frequency the FS posteriors become uninformative, and the TP keeps following the injected spectrum because it is tied to an underlying PL. The PL, by construction, cannot reproduce any deviation from its own shape. The novel results of our work concern what happens when these chains are provided to the frequentist PFOS analysis. We found that the per-frequency PSD reconstructed by the PFOS is more precise and accurate when the CURN process is modeled with the TP. In the 9 lowest frequency bins, where the GWB power is constrained by the array, the uncertainties of the PFOS results obtained from the TP chains are 43% narrower than those obtained from the PL, and 26% narrower than those obtained from the FS. The results are also closer to the injected PSD than the FS results, with a 7 .4% median gain, while relative to the PL the two reconstructions are at a comparable distance from the injected value, with a gain of 2 .1% for the TP. We then computed the detection statistics, measuring how much of that power can be attributed to an HD correlated back-ground, and how much of it is not uniformly distributed in the sky. Summed in quadrature over the first nine bins, the TP gives a larger value in all 100 populations, with a median gain of about 46% over the PL and 65% over the FS, for both the anisotropic and isotropic S /N. Above the tenth bin, where the WN domi-nates, this gain decreases to about 1 .5–2%. Although systematic, this small gain in noise dominated bins does not indicate a real improvement of the TP over the other models. Finally, we computed the anisotropy p-values, calibrating the anisotropic S /N against the isotropic null hypothesis. The TP gives a smaller median p-value than the FS, with a median gain of 22%. Against the PL the di ff erence is much smaller, with a median gain of 4%, and we therefore consider these results as a marginal di ff erence. Selecting the bins in which the anisotropy p-values are p < 0.01, the loudest injected CGW lies within 18 deg of the maximum of the sky map in 14 .3% of the cases using the TP model, against 10% with the PL and 6 .2% with the FS. This is not a statement about how well the sky maps are recovered; indeed di ff erent sky maps built with the three models agree with each other. What changes is the p-value that each model assigns to a similar sky map. The TP yields more selected bins in which the loudest binary satisfies the adopted localization criterion. These results show that the TP is a valid alternative to the PL and FS for modeling the GWB produced by a realistic SMBHB population. It follows the deviations from a PL that such a popu-lation imprints on the spectrum, and, when it is used in the PFOS analysis, it gives the most precise reconstruction of the correlated power and the largest isotropic and anisotropic S /N of the three models. As the sensitivity of future PTAs improves and the shape of the GWB PSD has to be characterized more accurately, we therefore suggest that the TP be considered alongside the PL and FS as a flexible model for characterizing the GWB spectrum. Our analysis has several limitations that motivate future work. First, we injected only WN into the datasets. Including intrinsic RN and DM variations is therefore an important next step to test whether the TP retains its advantages over the PL and FS models. Moreover, our simulated pulsars are evenly sampled, without any gaps and with almost the same time span, conditions in which the PFOS performs better (Gersbach et al. 2025, 2026). In future work, we will therefore simulate more realistic pulsars, with unevenly sampled data, gaps, and di ff erent time spans. Another limitation is that the injected SMBHB population used in this work consists of circular binaries without any en-vironmental e ff ects, so the only deviations from a PL that we have in our datasets are due to the discreteness of the population and to the individually loud sources. Therefore, the comparison presented here assesses how well each model describes the dis-creteness of a population, and not how well it follows a flatter PSD at low frequency. Repeating the three-model comparison 12 with eccentric binaries and environmental e ff ects would also test the models at low frequencies. Furthermore, we did not correct for the leakage of power between nearby frequency bins due to the finite observation time and the fact that the binaries do not emit exactly at the bin centers (Crisostomi et al. 2025). As discussed above, the FS fits the power in each bin as a free parameter, while the TP keeps all the bins tied to a common PL, thus sharing information across bins. Whether this makes the TP less a ff ected by leakage than the FS remains an open question that can be addressed with simulations designed specifically to quantify this e ff ect. A further limitation is that the PFOS analyzes each frequency bin independently, as mentioned in Section 3.2. Modeling the CURN with the TP reduces the impact of this assumption, but it is still present in the PFOS analysis. Removing it would require a PFOS that analyzes all frequency bins simultaneously, includ-ing the covariances between them (Gersbach et al. 2026). Once these limitations are addressed, the next step will be to apply the pipeline to real PTA datasets.
