ABSTRACT 

We report the discovery of three candidate spider millisecond pulsars via multi-wavelength follow-up of unassociated Fermi Large Area Telescope 𝛾 -ray sources. All have optical spectroscopy from the SOAR 4.1-m telescope and X-ray data from Swift. Two optical/X-ray sources are exceptionally strong redback candidates, sitting close to the centres of the localization ellipses of the 𝛾 -ray sources 4FGL J1020.4–5314 and 4FGL J1408.7–3747. We show that both optical sources are spectroscopic binaries with orbital periods (0.15–0.3 d) and mass functions (0.6–0 .9𝑀 ⊙ ) typical for redbacks. 4FGL J1020.4–5314 shows bright, very broad (full width at half maximum 2100 km s −1) H 𝛼 emission in all spectra obtained over two years. Remarkably, the H 𝛼 radial velocities are offset in phase from the absorption-line velocities of the secondary, and match the signal expected for an emission source near the primary. This demonstrates, for the first time for any spider, that the H 𝛼 emission can be associated with an intrabinary shock that wraps around the pulsar, opening up the possibility of using optical emission lines as kinematic tracers of the intrabinary shock in some redbacks. A third candidate spider is less secure in its association with the 𝛾 -ray source 4FGL J1429.8–0739, but has unusual properties, including a 30.5-hour orbital period, a potential subgiant secondary, and a high X-ray luminosity, making it worthy of further investigation. 

Key words: pulsars: general – binaries: spectroscopic – stars: neutron – gamma-rays: stars – X-rays: binaries 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Millisecond pulsars are thought to have been recycled to short periods through accretion from a companion (Bhattacharya & van den Heuvel 1991). Typical millisecond pulsars in the field are fully recycled and have low-mass white dwarf companions (Tauris & van den Heuvel 2006). However, the discovery via the Fermi Large Area Telescope (LAT) that most millisecond pulsars are efficient emitters of GeV 

𝛾 -rays (Abdo et al. 2013) has allowed for targeted radio, optical, and X-ray searches of Fermi-LAT 𝛾 -ray sources for new pulsars. These efforts have demonstrated that “spider" millisecond pulsars with close main sequence-like companions are common (Ray et al. 2012; Roberts 2013), making up ≳ 15–20% of the fully recycled millisecond pulsar population with known companion types (Swihart et al. 2022) in the Galactic field. Their previous rarity was entirely a selection effect, as spiders are often eclipsed in radio observations due to ionized gas associated with the secondary, as originally predicted by Tavani (1991). The main classes of spiders are “redbacks" with ≳ 0.1𝑀 ⊙ sec-ondaries and “black widows" with lower-mass companions (Roberts

All spiders are X-ray-luminous due to intrabinary shocks between the pulsar and the companion (Roberts et al. 2014). These millisecond pulsars are not simply a curiosity: ≳ 80% of pulsars with spins < 2 msec are spiders (Swihart et al. 2022), includ-ing the current record holder (a globular cluster redback; Hessels et al. 2006). The primaries in spiders are also more massive than typical neutron stars (e.g., Strader et al. 2019), including several sys-tems with claimed neutron star masses > 2.3𝑀 ⊙ , such as the black widow PSR J0952-0607 (Romani et al. 2026) and the redback PSR J2215+5135 (Linares et al. 2018; Kandel & Romani 2020). While im-portant systematics can affect these mass measurements (e.g., Clark et al. 2023), it is plausible or even likely that the most massive and fastest spinning neutron stars reside in spiders. These extreme spin and mass properties are likely due to their evolutionary histories: spiders are thought to descend from progenitor binaries with short initial orbital periods and lengthy accretion (Chen et al. 2013; Ben-venuto et al. 2014; Bellm et al. 2016; Ginzburg & Quataert 2021; Misra et al. 2025; Sautron et al. 2026).

As of mid-2026 1 there are about 90 field spiders confirmed with detection of their radio pulsations (Koljonen & Linares 2025), along with many yet unconfirmed but convincing candidates. A large frac-tion of these were discovered through radio, X-ray, and/or opti-cal follow-up of previously unassociated Fermi-LAT 𝛾 -ray sources, which continues to be an effective method of finding new spiders. Optical follow-up is especially valuable for redbacks and huntsman systems, which have more luminous companions that allow optical spectroscopy to help determine the physical parameters of the binary. As part of our ongoing programme on the SOAR 4.1-m telescope to find new millisecond pulsar binaries associated with Fermi 𝛾 -ray sources, here we report the discovery of three new spectroscopic binaries: two nearby redbacks with compelling evidence for being spiders (4FGL J1020.4–5314 and 4FGL J1408.7–3747) and an un-usual more distant candidate spider with an uncertain classification (4FGL J1429.8–0739).

3 RESULTS 

Here we discuss the initial results from the optical spectroscopy and photometry of these binaries. Given the large systematic uncertainties associated with masses and inclinations derived from light curve fitting in the absence of pulsar constraints, we defer such detailed modelling until after the expected pulsars have been confirmed and timed. 

3.1 4FGL J1020.4–5314 

The spectra (Figure 1) show a combination of metal absorption features and strong double-peaked H 𝛼 emission, with higher or-der Balmer lines and a few He i lines also occasionally visible in emission. We fit absorption-line radial velocities using RVSpecFit (Ko-posov et al. 2011; Koposov 2019), which performs full spectral fit-ting over a grid of PHOENIX (Allard 2016) templates convolved to the observed resolution. Regions around emission lines or telluric features were excluded from the fits, hence, in practice the fits only include metal lines. We obtained absorption line velocities for 28 spectra (Table A2), with a median uncertainty of 25 km s −1.Using TheJoker (Price-Whelan et al. 2017), we initially fit a circular Keplerian model to the absorption line radial velocities. This fit was poor with large residuals, but an eccentric fit (Figure 2a) produced substantially better results. The best-fit parameters of the eccentric fit were orbital period 𝑃 = 0.1508431 (2) d, eccentricity 

𝑒 = 0.111 ± 0.015, secondary semi-amplitude 𝐾 2 = 381 ± 6 km s −1,and systemic velocity 𝛾 = 91 ±5 km s −1; this fit has 𝜒 2/d.o.f. = 34/22 with an r.m.s. of 28 km s −1. 3.1.1 Emission Lines 

All the spectra show double-peaked emission, most readily seen in H𝛼 but in a few spectra also He 1 5875 Å just blueward of NaD (Figure 1). The morphology varies little among the spectra. We measured the emission velocity by fitting a single Gaussian to the H 𝛼 profile in each spectrum. Despite the double-peaked nature of the H 𝛼 line, a single Gaussian fit is a robust route to determining the line center and width. The median FWHM of H 𝛼 is large, about 2100 km s −1;if a double Gaussian fit is used instead, the median peak separation is about 1200 km s −1.The H 𝛼 radial velocities from the single Gaussian fits are compared to the previously measured absorption-line velocities in Figure 3. The H 𝛼 velocities are anti-correlated with the radial velocities of the secondary, offset in phase by half an orbit and of lower amplitude (a formal fit gives 88 km s −1). This is what would be expected for an emission location near the primary rather than the secondary. The classic interpretation of such a signature would be an accretion disc. 4 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 4.1 The candidate redbacks 4FGL J1020.4–5314 and 4FGL J1408.7–3747 in context 

First, we briefly review why we believe these two sources are al-most certain to be binary millisecond pulsars with low-mass H-rich secondaries. They cannot be active binaries: if the primaries were main sequence stars of the masses implied by the radial velocities of the secondaries, the primaries would dominate the optical light; instead they are not seen. The X-ray luminosities are also at least two orders of magnitude above the coronal saturation limit (Vilhu & Walter 1987). Hence the primaries must be compact objects. The lack of a blue continuum in either source, or emission lines for 4FGL J1408.7–3747, excludes accreting white dwarfs. In principle a non-accreting white dwarf with an M dwarf companion could have a mass function and X-ray luminosity similar to 4FGL J1408.7–3747. However, known systems in this class (e.g., AR Sco; Marsh et al. 2016) have a secondary effective temperature close to that expected for a main sequence star of the secondary’s stellar mass, unlike the large mismatch seen for 4FGL J1408.7–3747 (Section 3.2). By contrast, the multi-wavelength properties of these two sources are as observed for spiders: they are the only detected X-ray sources located within the 68% localization ellipse for curved non-variable Fermi-LAT sources and hence the most likely counterparts to these 𝛾 -ray sources. The large amplitude, time-variable optical heating seen in 4FGL J1020.4–5314 is not shown by any class of compact binary other than spiders. 4FGL J1408.7–3747 has a nightside 𝑇 eff = 5725 K (typical of a G dwarf) but a size of only 0 .32 ±0.04 𝑅 ⊙ (typical of an M dwarf). Such discrepancies between the 𝑇 eff observed and the value predicted for a normal main sequence star of the observed radius are common for redbacks (e.g., Breton et al. 2013; Li et al. 2014), likely due to the stripped and/or heated nature of the donor stars (e.g., Chen et al. 2013). Finally, for both of the candidates, the mass functions of the primary are consistent with other redbacks (Strader et al. 2019), implying neutron star-like masses for the invisible primaries. For these reasons we believe it is nearly certain that these two binaries are indeed spiders. Both 4FGL J1020.4–5314 and 4FGL J1408.7–3747 have 0.5–10 X-ray luminosities 𝐿 𝑋 ∼ 2 × 10 31 erg s −1, on the lower end but well within the range previously found for redbacks (Urquhart et al. 2020; Koljonen & Linares 2023). Their X-ray (0.5–10 keV) to 𝛾 -ray (0.1-100 GeV) flux ratios are 0.025 and 0.055, respectively, which are values typical for redbacks (Miller et al. 2020). 4.2 Interpreting 4FGL J1429.8–0739 

The case of 4FGL J1429.8–0739 is rather different than the other two redbacks considered here. The first issue is that the potential X-ray/optical binary counterpart is outside the formal 95% positional uncertainty ellipse of the 𝛾 -ray source. This is not necessarily a fatal objection, since there is no candidate X-ray counterpart within the 95% ellipse, and more broadly, published Fermi-LAT 95% error ellipses have been shown to contain fewer than 95% of true source locations when additional data became available (e.g., Ballet et al. 2026).

Even though the alternative potential X-ray counterpart discussed above (a background AGN; see Section 2.2) is slightly closer to the centre, the 𝛾 -ray properties of 4FGL J1429.8–0739 in the 16-year Catalog are more similar to a pulsar than an AGN: the best-fit model is a LogParabola with significant curvature and a peak energy of 1 .4±

In addition, 4FGL J1429.8–0739 shows no evidence for 𝛾 -ray variability, as expected for a pulsar but unlike typical Fermi-LAT AGN (Abdollahi et al. 2020). Another possibility is that the single catalogued 𝛾 -ray source is actually a blend of two fainter 𝛾 -ray sources that are too close to be distinguished in existing data, but which if properly split could be separately associated with these two different plausible counterparts to the current single catalogued 𝛾 -ray source. The second potential issue is the unusual inferred high-energy properties of the binary. At its poorly-measured X-ray flux with an assumed distance of 8 kpc, it would be the most X-ray-luminous non-accreting spider known at 𝐿 𝑋 ∼ 4 × 10 33 (𝑑 /8 kpc )2 erg s −1.The only source potentially in the same 𝐿 𝑋 neighbourhood is the huntsman PSR J1417–4402, which has 𝐿 𝑋 = 2.1 × 10 33 erg s −1

(converting the X-ray flux measurement from Swihart et al. 2018 to 0.5–10 keV, and using the Gaia DR3 parallax distance of 4.2 kpc). This physical comparison is perhaps encouraging if 4FGL J1429.8– 0739 does indeed have an evolved secondary like PSR J1417–4402, potentially placing it in the huntsman category. This secondary could have a strong wind that allows a larger fraction of the pulsar wind to be radiated at a shock. As mentioned above, the 1.3 d period of 4FGL J1429.8–0739 is also close to that of the spider PSR J1740–5340A in the globular cluster NGC 6397, which may have an evolved secondary (Mucciarelli et al. 2013), though this system has only 𝐿 𝑋 ∼ 10 31 erg s−1 (Bogdanov et al. 2010). While the ideal confirmation of the nature of 4FGL J1429.8–0739 would come from the detection of a pulsar, this may be challenging, as the pulsar would be both distant and likely to be frequently eclipsed. Instead, an X-ray light curve that could show the expected orbital modulation of a spider might be the most promising route to further explore the nature of this binary. 

4.3 Emission Lines as Kinematic Tracers of Intrabinary Shocks in Spiders 

Optical emission lines are common in spiders, with nearly half of systems with optical spectroscopy at least sometimes showing them, typically traced by H 𝛼 (Swihart et al. 2022). However, these emission lines are typically variable and often disappear. Binaries with persistent strong emission lines are rare. The redback PSR J1048+2339 (Strader et al. 2019; Simpson et al. 2025) appears to nearly always show broad H 𝛼 , and H 𝛼 emission is likewise ubiquitous in the huntsman system 1FGL J1417.7-4407 (Strader et al. 2015; Swihart et al. 2018) and perhaps in the 1.3d orbital period spider PSR J1740–5340A in the globular cluster NGC 6397 (Sabbi et al. 2003). In this context, finding two new candidate spiders that show emis-sion lines in literally all spectra obtained over a long timespan is remarkable and useful. The stable profiles allow for reliable kine-matics of the emission lines to be measured at all orbital phases. For 4FGL J1020.4–5314, this has allowed us to demonstrate, for the first time for any spider, that the H 𝛼 emission is associated with a shock located near the pulsar. There is already evidence from modelling X-ray light curves of redbacks that the shock often wraps around the pulsar (e.g., Wadiasingh et al. 2017; Kandel et al. 2019), but of course kinematic measurements from these X-ray data are not available. Joint modelling of a future X-ray light curve for 4FGL J1020.4–5314 with optical emission line data should allow an im-proved understanding of the details of the intrabinary shock. The case of 4FGL J1429.8–0739, where the H 𝛼 -emitting gas is located close to the companion, appears more typical among spiders. Similar conclusions were reached for the black widow PSR J1311– 3430 (Romani et al. 2015) and the huntsman 1FGL J1417.7–4407 (Swihart et al. 2018), though it was not clear whether the emitting material was associated directly with a wind from the secondary or from the shock itself. Many candidate or confirmed spiders lack extensive spectroscopy, even for systems that have shown strong emission lines in the limited data available (e.g., 4FGL J0935.3+0901; Wang et al. 2020). The observational requirements to obtain emission line velocities are less stringent than for absorption lines. Hence there are likely more spiders for which observational constraints are possible on the location of the optical line-emitting gas. 

4.4 Concluding Remarks 

We have presented the discovery and initial characterization of three candidate spider millisecond pulsar binaries found through optical spectroscopic follow-up of Fermi-LAT 𝛾 -ray sources. Two of these, 4FGL J1020.4–5314 ( 𝑃 = 3.6 hr) and 4FGL J1408.7–3747 ( 𝑃 = 7.3hr), are nearby redbacks. The former is a heating-dominated sys-tem with significant and variable irradiation, while the latter shows ellipsoidal variations with no evidence for heating. The third bi-nary, potentially associated with 4FGL J1429.8–0739, would have the longest orbital period of any known redback (30.5 hr), and may in-stead harbor an evolved subgiant secondary. The orbital ephemerides presented here should aid targeted radio searches for the pulsars in all three systems. The clear association of the H 𝛼 emission lines in 4FGL J1020.4– 5314 with an intrabinary shock located near the pulsar is in contrast to a few previous spiders, where the optical emission lines were instead located close to the companion. This suggests that modelling these emission lines in conjunction with other data (such as X-ray light curves) could be a powerful tool for understanding the dynamics of shocks in spiders.
