# Stationarity and angular momentum conservation in pulsar spin noise 

## Reginald Christian Bernardo, ★

Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics (Albert Einstein Institute), Leibniz Universität Hannover, Callinstrasse 38, D-30167 Hannover, Germany 

Accepted XXX. Received YYY; in original form ZZZ 

ABSTRACT 

We present an analysis of pulsar spin noise based on physically-motivated two-component models of spin wandering. We focus on two models, distinguished by their total angular momentum dynamics: a singular, nonstationary model with a diffusive total angular momentum, and a minimal, stationary model anchored on a conserved total angular momentum. We develop scalable Gaussian process methods and, using mock data, show that the two models are fully testable and distinguishable with full-state observations, i.e., simultaneous independent data on the crust and the superfluid rotational states. This paves a path to testing stationarity in pulsar spin noise, potentially achievable with joint continuous gravitational wave observations and radio timing of pulsars. However, robust inferences and predictions are harder to achieve, and depend on the priors and data quality, when only one component is observationally accessible. 

Key words: pulsars: general – methods: data analysis – methods: statistical 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Pulsars are highly magnetized, stably rotating neutron stars that send beams of electromagnetic radiation at regular intervals (Hewish et al. 1968; Pilkington et al. 1968; Wallace et al. 1977; Taylor & Manch-ester 1977). For this reason, they are regarded as one of the Uni-verse’s most precise natural clocks (Lorimer 2001; Kaspi & Kramer 2016; Bassa et al. 2017; Clark et al. 2018; Nieder et al. 2020; Clark et al. 2025; Bagchi et al. 2025). This exquisite timing precision has been used to set constraints on fundamental physics through time delays caused by fields such as strong gravity (Manchester 2015; Kramer et al. 2021; Freire & Wex 2024), dark matter (Khmelnitsky & Rubakov 2014; Porayko & Postnov 2014; Porayko et al. 2018; Smarra et al. 2023), and gravitational waves (Hulse & Taylor 1975; Taylor & Weisberg 1982; Hulse 1994; Damour 2015). Pulsar timing arrays have made the best out of this to search for a gravitational wave background signal via spatially-correlated time delays (Agazie et al. 2023; Antoniadis et al. 2023; Reardon et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023). The precision achievable with pulsar timing has a limit that re-mains incompletely understood. Once a deterministic timing model – accounting for the pulsar’s spin period, period derivative, orbital dynamics, and other deterministic effects – is subtracted, glitches, timing noise, mode switching, and nulling are among the phenom-ena that set the ceiling on a pulsar’s utility for testing fundamental physics through time delays. Physical pictures exist for some of these. Glitches are reasonably well described by a two-step pinning and un-pinning process of superfluid vortices entrained in the neutron star’s crust (Baym et al. 1969; Andersson et al. 2012; Chamel 2013); timing noise is commonly depicted as a red stochastic process in the neu-tron star’s spin or angular momentum, i.e., spin wandering (Groth

However, a unified physical picture connecting these phenomena, and in particular tying timing noise to a falsifiable physical origin, remains lacking. We start with glitches and timing noise, both of which have been given phenomenological accounts within a single body-averaged two-component dynamical model of a neutron star’s rotational de-grees of freedom. A glitch is preceded by the build-up of a crust-core lag and an angular momentum reservoir that is released in a sudden spin-up of the crust (Montoli et al. 2020; Andersson et al. 2012; Chamel 2013). Timing noise, on the other hand, is associated with stochastic torques that continuously act on the components of the neutron star. However, the two-component models considered so far to explain radio timing noise correspond to a nonstationary Gaus-sian process (Meyers et al. 2021a,b; Antonelli et al. 2023; Bernardo 2026), whose nonstationarity can be traced to a diffusive total angu-lar momentum mode excited by the stochastic torques. A stationary process can instead be motivated by imposing that the total angular momentum is conserved, or that stochastic torques compel only in-ternal angular momentum exchanges (Antonelli et al. 2023, 2025). Conservation of total angular momentum therefore sits at the heart of the question of whether pulsar spin noise is stationary, and hints at a potentially falsifiable prediction of pulsar physics: testing stationarity in spin noise amounts to testing whether the total angular momentum of a neutron star is conserved. This work sets out to develop methods geared toward testing stationarity in pulsar spin noise. However, there is an observational caveat. Radio pulsar timing measures only the crust; in principle, we take this to be the part of the star that is strongly electromagnetically-coupled. The detected pulses, and our understanding of their emission, are tied to the crust’s rotational state. Theory offers insights into the star’s interior dynam-ics (Prix 1999; Prix et al. 2002; Prix 2004; Andersson et al. 2006;

Melatos & Link 2014; Haskell & Melatos 2015; Haskell & Sedrakian 2018), and continuous gravitational waves may complement radio timing by probing the star’s interior rotation (Jaranowski et al. 1998; Prix 2009; Owen et al. 2009; Haskell & Schwenzer 2021; Ming et al. 2022; Dergachev & Papa 2025, 2026; McGloughlin et al. 2026), completing the picture beyond the radio band. This raises a question that this work takes up directly: if only a single axis of a multidi-mensional rotational dynamics is observed, what can we robustly say about the underlying model, or about the interior state it predicts? Previous studies have focused on parameter identifiability. Elec-tromagnetic timing data alone can only recover a subset of the model parameters, and gravitational wave data on the superfluid rotation is needed to break the remaining degeneracies (Meyers et al. 2021a,b). The model’s power spectral density has been used to extract ana-lytic timing-noise-strength scalings (Antonelli et al. 2023), and fu-ture joint electromagnetic-gravitational wave correlations have been proposed to observationally distinguish internal and magnetospheric noise (Antonelli et al. 2025). Robust tests of these physical models must also confront the prac-tical realities of pulsar timing data (Kelly et al. 2014; Ambikasaran et al. 2015; van Haasteren & Vallisneri 2015; Goncharov et al. 2020): decades-long, unevenly sampled time series with heteroscedastic measurement uncertainties, frequently analyzed jointly across many pulsars in a pulsar timing array. A direct Gaussian process likelihood evaluation scales as O( 𝑁 3) in the number of observations, which becomes prohibitive as data spans lengthen and as more pulsars, or additional rotational-state observables, enter a joint analysis. This motivates the development of scalable, physically-motivated Gaus-sian process methods (Singh et al. 2018; Foreman-Mackey et al. 2017) that retain the O( 𝑁 ) efficiency of phenomenological kernels while remaining anchored to the dynamics of spin wandering. We first revisit the two-component model of spin wandering (Sec-tion 2) and its analytical solutions (Section 3), which together pro-vide the foundation for constraining stationarity in spin noise from timing observations. Building on this foundation, we develop scal-able, physically-motivated Gaussian process methods for pulsar spin noise using well-established diffusion theory and the corresponding analytical solutions (Section 4). We then test the method on mock angular velocity residuals generated from both the nonstationary and the stationary spin noise models (Sections 5–6). We conclude with a discussion of future work (Section 7). We include four appendices with additional details. Appendix A motivates the two-component model starting from a two-fluid description of a neutron star; Appendix B briefly reviews the fluctuation-dissipation theorem; Appendix C illustrates the fast Bayesian method of Section 4 on 2D Brownian motion; and Ap-pendix D presents the full-state semiseparable likelihood for the two-component model.

4 FAST BAYESIAN INFERENCE METHODS 

The regularized two-component model (24) with drift matrix (31) constitutes a bivariate OU process with strictly positive eigenvalues. This enables application of the fast Bayesian inference method in Singh et al. (2018) for multivariate OU processes in the idealized scenario that both Ωc and Ωs are observed and that the data is sampled evenly. The sufficient statistics must be computed from the stochastic residuals 𝛀 S = 𝛀 − 𝛀 D = [𝛿 Ωc, 𝛿 Ωs]⊤; where 𝛿 Ωc and 𝛿 Ωs are the stochastic spin wandering components of the crust and superfluid angular velocities, respectively. When only the crust angular velocity is observed, we rely on analytic time-domain solution derived in Bernardo (2026) to construct a scalable Gaussian process method.

7 CONCLUSIONS 

Two-component models of pulsar spin wandering, like their use in explaining glitches, are a natural observational starting point for associating radio timing noise with underlying physical processes 

MNRAS 000 , 1–21 (2026) 16 Reginald Christian Bernardo 

in neutron stars. However, these models are completely determined by the rotational states of both the crust and the superfluid. For the observing span, cadence, measurement noise, and model-prior pairs considered here, crust-only evidence does not reliably identify the generating model and remains sensitive to prior volume. Observing only a single component does not provide sufficient information to reliably constrain the model or to make robust predictions, whether of future configurations or of a radio-hidden state (Meyers et al. 2021a; Antonelli et al. 2023). This work has provided an explicit, quantitative example of this limitation. We worked with two spin noise or spin wandering models, distin-guished by the dynamics of their total angular momentum: a singular, nonstationary model (1–5) and a minimal, stationary one (19-21). The former produces nonstationary spin noise owing to a diffusive total angular momentum mode (Meyers et al. 2021a), while the latter produces stationary spin noise anchored on a conserved total angular momentum (Antonelli et al. 2023). Both models were written as a shifted 2D OU process, drawing on their analogy with 2D Brownian motion to develop fast and scalable Gaussian process methods for in-ference and prediction (Section 4). With simulated mock data based on both models, we have shown that the two are fully constrainable and reliably distinguishable when both components are observed, but that robust inferences and predictions cannot be obtained when only a single component is observationally accessible (Sections 5-6). This is analogous to constraining a 2D random walk that is observed only along a single axis. Beyond these physical conclusions, this work is fundamentally a methods contribution. The Gaussian process models constructed here are physical, derived directly from the two-component equations of motion rather than from a generic phenomenological kernel or power spectral density, which is what makes them testable and falsifiable and lets the stationarity of spin noise be read directly as a statement about the conservation of a neutron star’s total angular momentum. They are also scalable, with cost growing linearly rather than cubically with the number of observations, a necessity for the long and ever-growing datasets of pulsar timing. In this sense, the paper pushes the modelling of timing noise in a more physical direction. In radio pulsar timing, the observed pulsar phase and its associated timing noise are tied to the crust’s rotational state. The explanation of glitches by a two-component model provides strong evidence for a neutron superfluid, but glitches constrain only part of the two-component parameter space and are typically treated as a separate phenomenon from timing noise. The results of this work indicate that spin wandering in the two-component model may only be robustly constrained with simultaneous, independent observations of the ro-tational states of the crust and the superfluid, or with an observation of the full state space of the neutron star. Continuous gravitational wave observations tied to the neutron star core, and possibly to the interior superfluid motion, are a promising avenue to complement ra-dio timing and access this state space. The methods developed here are directly applicable to such joint analyses, and we intend to apply them to real pulsar timing data, expanding the methods developed here at the level of angular velocities, to phases, in a different work.
