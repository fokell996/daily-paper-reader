ABSTRACT Pulsar timing array (PTA) experiments have seen evidence for a nanohertz-frequency gravitational wave background (GWB) through coordinated radio timing observations of millisecond pulsars. The significance of this evidence is expected to grow and the GWB’s progenitor(s) uncovered with continued observations, especially as the next gen-eration of radio telescopes comes online. We present a framework for simulating timing observations of a set of pulsars with a set of telescopes to predict the per-pulsar opti-mal instrument, observing frequency, and integration time. We apply this methodology to the NANOGrav source list of 85 pulsars observed with the Green Bank Telescope, Very Large Array, Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME), and the Deep Synoptic Array (DSA), a radio dish array under construction in Nevada. We discuss the dominant noise contributions to the timing precision of these pulsars and show that most are not dominated by intrinsic pulse phase jitter noise. We also de-termine which pulsars should be timed with DSA and CHIME or only DSA and show that an ab initio DSA PTA is more sensitive than one which uses current instruments. Finally, we solve for the optimal integration time per pulsar, subject to a fixed time budget, that maximizes the GWB signal-to-noise. We show that there is little benefit to optimizing over integration time for these pulsars observed with DSA. Through Monte Carlo realizations of the PTA, we show that the GWB sensitivity improves by ∼ 5% for time-optimized observations compared to the equal-time-per-source scenario with an early-science, fixed source list. INTRODUCTION Millisecond pulsars (MSPs) are neutron stars which have been spun up to tremendous rotation rates that are stable over cosmological timescales by accreting matter from a binary star companion. MSPs send radio pulses toward Earth with such regularity that they can act as precise, naturally occurring clocks. Nanohertz-frequency gravitational waves (GWs) passing through the Galaxy will cause the pulsar-Earth light travel time to oscillate on periods of years. Pulsar timing array (PTA) experiments measure these oscillations by searching for correlations in high-precision timing obser-vations of a set of MSPs carried out over decades. One possible source of nHz GWs is a stochastic gravitational wave background (GWB). The prevailing theory is that such a background could be generated by a cosmological population of supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs) in the cen-ters of merging galaxies (S. Detweiler 1979). Therefore, nHz GWs could act as a probe into the evolutionary history of galaxies and populations of SMBHBs (A. H. Jaffe & D. C. Backer 2003; A. Sesana et al. 2004). Regional PTAs have recently seen evidence for the GWB (D. J. Reardon et al. 2023; EPTA Collaboration and InPTA Collaboration et al. 2023; G. Agazie et al. 2023, hereafter NG15gwb), but determining its source requires constraining the properties of the GWB spectrum through continued observations. Eventually we expect to be able to resolve signals from individual SMBHBs amidst the background, potentially providing opportunities to study the mechanisms that power active galactic nuclei (S. Burke-Spolaor et al. 2019). The North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves (NANOGrav) times 78 MSPs and has amassed 20 years of data using the Arecibo Observatory, Green Bank Telescope (GBT), Very Large Array (VLA), and Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME). NANOGrav regularly releases datasets of its timing measurements, the latest being the 15-yr data set (G. Agazie et al. 2023a, hereafter NG15) consisting of 68 pulsars. For the last five years NANOGrav has conducted dual-band observations with the GBT and VLA, where 59 pulsars are timed with the GBT only, 15 are timed with the GBT and VLA, and one is timed with only the VLA. In addition, daily cadenced, low-frequency (400–800 MHz) observations are carried out by CHIME for 54 MSPs. GBT and VLA observations have a monthly cadence, with observing time split equally among the pulsars. While an equal-time allocation is convenient from a scheduling perspective, it has not previously been determined whether that is the optimal strategy for this set of pulsars. NANOGrav is expected to transition most of its pulsar timing to the Deep Synoptic Array (DSA), a dish array under construction in Eastern Nevada, by the late 2020s (G. Hallinan et al. 2019). DSA is expected to have a significant increase in sensitivity and continuous bandwidth over current instruments. It is critical to strategically plan how this instrument will be used now, while there is still some flexibility in the design of the instrument and observing program. There have been many efforts to optimize PTA observations for sensitivity to GWs, from the optimal observing frequency per pulsar that minimizes timing noise (M. T. Lam et al. 2018) to the optimal integration time per pulsar that maximizes the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio for the GWB (K. J. Lee et al. 2012; H. Middleton et al. 2025) and optimal integration time that maximizes single-SMBHB,

Additionally, much work has been done to derive scaling laws that predict PTA time-to-detection of the GWB and its S/N (X. Siemens et al. 2013; S. J. Vigeland & X. Siemens 2016). These works have shown that PTA sensitivity to GWs is a function of radio telescope capabilities, the number of MSPs observed, the cadence with which they are observed, the total time baseline of observations, and uncertainties in their pulse times of arrival (TOA). Therefore, the time required to achieve PTA science objectives will be determined, in part, by how consistently new MSPs can be discovered and added to the PTA and how effectively next-generation telescopes like DSA, next-generation VLA (ngVLA) and Square Kilometer Array (SKA) are able to time these MSPs. As new MSPs continue to be discovered and PTA experiment source lists continue to expand, it becomes crucial to make the most of telescope resources in order to maximize scientific output. We present a novel framework and software package, PTAOptimizer, for simulating and predicting many sources of noise in pulsar timing data with radio telescopes and predicting a simulated PTA’s sensitivity to the GWB. We use this framework to optimize the NANOGrav PTA for the simultaneous optimal instrument, observing frequency range, and integration time per pulsar that maximizes GWB sensitivity. In Section 2 we discuss the infinite-frequency TOA uncertainty figure-of-merit we use to evaluate the performance of a pulsar-receiver pair. In Section 3 we discuss the GWB S/N objective function, which quantifies a PTA’s sensitivity to the GWB. In Section 4 we describe our models of the NANOGrav PTA and the telescopes/receivers with which we simulate observations. In Section 5 we discuss the optimal instrument choice per pulsar and present our predictions for the improvement of a DSA PTA over current instruments. In Section 6 we discuss the number of jitter-dominated pulsars and the efficacy of subarrying and strategic beam placement. In Section 7 we discuss the geometry of the objective function with respect to integration time and present our results for the optimal distribution of integration time for a PTA using DSA. Finally, we summarize our conclusions and discuss potential future work in Section 8.

THE INFINITE-FREQUENCY TOA UNCERTAINTY Pulsar timing requires one to sum many thousands of pulses measured across time and observing frequency in order to accumulate enough S/N to measure a TOA. “Folding” the pulses at the correct rotational phase requires a timing model that can account for every rotation of the pulsar. This timing model must account for all of the effects along the line-of-sight to the pulsar that would result in measuring an apparent change in the rotational frequency of the pulsar, from spin-down due to magnetic dipole radiation, to parallactic motions, to binary orbital accelerations. The model must also correct for dispersive delays as the pulses propagate through the interstellar medium (ISM), which are radio frequency-dependent and spread out the pulse fluence in phase. The timing model is fit to align the pulse phase over many rotations of the pulsar, producing an average “pulse profile.” This profile is then cross-correlated with a “template” profile (often a smoothed profile from previously measured noisy data or a sum of Gaussian components) to compute the phase offset from the start of the observation from which one can measure the TOA at the center of the observation. Subtracting the TOAs predicted from the timing model from the measured TOAs produces residuals that can then be cross-correlated between pulsars in the PTA in order to search for low-frequency noise due to the GWB. The ISM causes broadening of the pulse profile, frequency-dependent dispersive delays in the TOA, and fluctuations in the observed flux, effects which all depend on observing frequency, ν. While TOAs Optimal Pulsar Timing Array Strategies with the Deep Synoptic Array 5are typically measured at multiple frequencies to quantify and model these effects, one can compute a single TOA unperturbed by these effects referenced to an “infinite frequency,” t∞. The uncertainty on t∞ depends on the sensitivity and configuration of the telescope-receiver system, the intrinsic properties of the pulsar, and the properties of the ISM along the line-of-sight. The optimal instrument and observing frequencies are chosen by the smallest σTOA .A few caveats are necessary for simulations of multi-band observations. We treat the receiver combinations as a single, simultaneously-observed band in our computation of σTOA , meaning G(ν), 

Trcvr (ν), and ∆ ν cover the entire frequency range from the bottom of the lowest band to the top of the highest band. For non-adjacent bands (CHIME and GBT L-band, GBT L-band and 820 MHz, GBT L-band and VLA S-band) we insert a single frequency channel between the bands with very low G (∼ 10 −5 K Jy −1) to make the template-fitting noise covariances, Sν1ν2 ∝ G−2, and therefore the achromatic noise covariances Cν1ν2 that include this channel approximately infinte 3. σdDM , σδt C ,and σDM( ν) are all weighted by C−1, so the contribution from this gap is effectively ignored in σTOA .However, σJ, σDISS , and σtel don’t depend on Sν1ν2 . They will see a larger effective bandwidth and will be slightly overestimated from nonexistent frequency-frequency correlations when they are con-sidered as individual noise components, but the effect on σTOA is suppressed by the achromatic noise covariances. Whenever two bands overlap (e.g. CHIME and DSA), we use the noise characteristics of the more sensitive instrument (DSA) in the overlapping bandwidth. Additionally, jitter noise is not correlated between non-simultaneous observations, so σJ will be slightly overestimated in all considered multi-instrument combinations. These effects can be addressed in the future by masking the rows and columns of all of the noise covariance matrices for frequencies in the gap between bands and between nonsimultaneous frequencies in the jitter covariance matrix. The Deep Synoptic Array is a 1650-dish array under construction in Eastern Nevada, expected to make its first observations in the late 2020s. We assume that pulsar observations will be conducted down to a minimum elevation limit of 10 ◦, yielding a Dec. range of −41 ◦ < Dec . < 90 ◦. DSA is expected to cover 0.7-2 GHz simultaneously. The simultaneous bandwidth offers at least a factor of √2 improvement in template-fitting uncertainty by covering the full band with the same amount of observing time as current dual-band configurations, assuming a fixed observing time. The telescope was originally designed with 2000 dishes, but recently underwent a redesign that reduced the number of dishes but increased their diameter from 5 m to 6.15 m (M. McLaughlin et al. 2026). Assuming the same 70% aperture efficiency (G. Hallinan et al. 2019) and Trcvr (ν) (Figure 1c, J. Flygare 2024) as the original design, we account for the overall change in the collecting area by increasing the gain from 10 to 12 .5 K Jy −1. The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment telescope is a transit instrument composed of four cylindrical reflectors located at the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory (DRAO) at a latitude of 49 .32 ◦ with a declination range of −20 ◦ < δ < 90 ◦ (C. Ng 2018). The telescope’s F-Engine covers 400-800 MHz, but anywhere from 15%-68% of the band is masked on a given day due to RFI ( CHIME/Pulsar Collaboration et al. 2021). Because CHIME is a transit telescope, the (beam-center) gain depends strongly on declination, which we model as (E. Fonseca, internal memo) 

G0(δ) = √0.7 × 1.2 cos( δ − 49 .32 ◦) K Jy −1. (34) where 1 .2 K Jy −1 is the nominal zenith gain (B. C. Andersen et al. 2023) and the √0.7 prefactor accounts for an assumed 70% usable bandwidth due to RFI. The cylinders of CHIME point N-S and so the field-of-view of the telescope in this direction is 120 ◦. In the E-W direction however, the field-of-view depends strongly (linearly) on frequency: 2.5 ◦ at 400 MHz and 1.3 ◦ at 800 MHz. An order of magnitude estimate for the maximum tint /day for a source at δ = 0 at 400 MHz is 10 minutes (C. Ng 2018; CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2021). For patches of sky of equal area but at different declinations, it takes more time to cover the same area by an amount of 1 / cos( δ). JITTER-DOMINATED PULSARS When designing a pulsar timing program, it is often useful to ask whether a pulsar is dominated by jitter. Some authors (e.g. J. M. Cordes & R. M. Shannon 2010; P. Gitika et al. 2025) define jitter-dominated as a pulsar which has σS/N < σ J and found a significant fraction of MSPs are jitter-dominated in timing observations. We find it more useful to instead define jitter-dominated in the context of subarraying with dish interferometers and the tradeoff between integration time and collecting area. If multiple pulsars are jitter-dominated and at a sufficiently high elevation at the same time, they will be timed to higher precision by apportioning a fraction of the dishes of an interferometer to each pulsar and increasing the integration time by the same factor, x. We investigate which pulsars are dominated by jitter noise by iteratively halving the DSA collecting area, doubling the integration time (starting from 1.75 hrs), and computing σTOA at each iteration until none of the pulsars improve. While DSA is not currently expected to be capable of subarraying, we still find this method potentially instructive for other dish arrays with finite time resources and a useful way to evaluate a PTA noise budget’s dependence on tint and telescope sensitivity. We define a pulsar to be highly jitter-dominated if the optimal integration factor that minimizes σTOA is x∗ ≥ 8, mildly jitter-dominated for x∗ = 2 , 4, and not jitter-dominated if x∗ = 1. 2. Most pulsars in our source list will not be highly jitter-dominated with DSA, in the sense that their timing precision does not improve significantly with reduced instrumental sensitivity and increased integration time. The benefits of subarraying or strategic beam placement are highly limited due to the scarcity and distribution of highly jitter-dominated pulsars on the sky. This could change if new discoveries and/or techniques like cyclic spectroscopy, which can enable the removal of time-variable scattering delays, increase the number density of jitter-dominated pulsars accessible to DSA. 3. In the DSA era, the GWB S/N will be dominated by red noise from the GWB itself. This limits the improvement in the GWB S/N achievable by optimizing observing frequency and integration time over the PTA subject to a fixed time budget to only a few percent. The opportunity cost of an early science DSA observing program with optimal per-pulsar integration time over equal-time allocation is low. 4. The overall GWB S/N is sensitive to the pulsar noise characteristics but the relative improvement from optimization is not. Monte Carlo simulations reinforced the conclusion that even if the pulsar noise characteristics are considerably different than what we’ve assumed, the improvement in GWB S/N is a small, constant offset. 5. In the intermediate GWB signal limit, the most important attributes for determining whether a pulsar will contribute GWB sensitivity are intrinsic pulsar red noise and, to a lesser degree, the amount of multi-path scattering by the ISM. We give the caveat that our results are highly dependent on our assumed pulsar noise models. We sought to address this through our Monte Carlo analysis, but some of the predictive distributions are inconsistent with current measurements, especially for red noise. Using custom noise modeling to mitigate unmodeled chromatic noise, N. Agarwal et al. (2026) found that only six pulsars in the NG15 dataset have significant red noise measurements when accounting for a common red noise process. As subsequent NANOGrav datasets will have increased timespans and tighter constraints on spatially-correlated red noise from the GWB, constraints on intrinsic pulsar red noise will improve and so too will the accuracy of our predictions. There are many other opportunities for improving the robustness of our pulsar models. Future optimal strategies analyses will use newer Galactic electron density models (e.g. S. K. Ocker & J. M. Cordes 2026). M. T. Lam et al. (2019) reported that a majority of NANOGrav pulsars have observing frequency-dependent, single-pulse jitter noise. Since some of the frequency-dependent models did not cover the full DSA band, we assumed their constant-in-frequency model for all pulsars rather than extrapolate. It will be relatively simple to include a frequency-dependent jitter model into our optimization framework in the future. Our pulsar noise model accounts for stochastic, frequency-dependent variations in pulse profile shapes due to DISS, but some pulsars also exhibit frequency-evolution of their profile shape intrinsic to their emission (M. Kramer et al. 1999; T. T. Pennucci 2019). It is difficult to disentangle intrinsic profile evolution from the impulse response of the ISM, so we have assumed that Weff and Uscale are constant with observing frequency. We are currently undertaking an analysis of MSP profile shapes and may be able to use 

Weff (ν) and Uscale (ν) in future analyses. Both frequency-dependent jitter and profile shape evolution will re-weight the pulsar achromatic noise covariances in the computation of σTOA .We have only scratched the surface of possible GW sources and signals to which we can optimize our PTA. In our analysis we have assumed a fixed GWB amplitude and spectral index, but we could explore whether our optimal tint results hold for different GWB spectral indices or sample AGWB from the NG15 GWB amplitude posterior. Additionally, in the post-GWB detection era, the sensitivity to the GWB becomes less of a target for optimization than our ability to characterize its spectrum. In the future, we aim to instead minimize an objective that is more akin to the measurement uncertainty on the GWB amplitude and spectral index. Such an objective will depend on the integration time per pulsar in a more nuanced way as the integration time also affects our ability to characterize the chromatic noise in our pulsars. J. S. Hazboun et al. (2019) also presented a statistic for the S/N of a PTA to a single, deterministic GW source. We could perform the same decomposition (as in Appendix B) of this statistic into its quadtratic form and write an objective function for CW sensitivity across the whole sky or in targeted directions where we suspect strong CW sources to exist without having to modify the rest of the simulation machinery. GW spectrum parameter estimation is even more important for CWs, so we could modify the Fisher matrix approach we took in T. Liu et al. (2023) to minimize amplitude and spectral index measurement uncertainties for CWs with DSA Optimal Pulsar Timing Array Strategies with the Deep Synoptic Array 35 to include the GWB and intrinsic pulsar red noise. When pulsar and GWB red noise dominate, the cadence does not appreciably affect the GWB sensitivity (J. M. Cordes & R. M. Shannon 2012). On the other hand, the cadence increases the high-(GW) frequency sensitivity and the detector volume to CW sources (J. G. Baier et al. 2025), thus we would seek to optimize over tint and ∆ t simultaneously and explore the geometry of this 2 Npsr -dimensional objective function. Finally, it is an open question as to how many total MSPs can or should be included in a PTA in the DSA era. We plan to explore this through optimization of synthesized populations of MSPs and simulations of next-generation surveys in a future paper.
