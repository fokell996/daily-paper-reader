ABSTRACT 

Whether spin noise of a neutron star is stationary is, in two-component models, a question of whether stellar angular momentum is conserved internally or exchanged externally through independent stochastic torques on the crust and superfluid. The question is posed in the two components’ angular velocities, but radio pulsar timing observes only the crust, through its rotational phase after a deterministic timing model is fitted. We develop a Gaussian process method for testing spin noise stationarity directly with such phase data. Starting from the exact analytical phase means and covariances of a singular, nonstationary and a minimal, stationary two-component model, we show that the crust phase covariances are semiseparable and construct a likelihood that scales linearly with the number of observations, with the timing model marginalized analytically. We analyze simulated phase data from both models using full-state and crust-only observations, and after differentiation into local spin frequencies. Integration, timing model removal and differentiation neither create nor destroy the distinction between stationary and nonstationary spin noise, but determine how much survives in a finite, noisy data set. With both components observed, the models are distinguished decisively and the generating parameters are recovered. With the crust alone, both models describe the observed phase equally well over much of parameter space, and the Bayes factors are of order unity and prior-limited. The minimal model nonetheless ties the hidden superfluid phase to the observed crust phase through a single factor set by the superfluid inertia fraction, so an independent estimate of this fraction, as from glitches, turns it into a falsifiable prediction that a continuous gravitational wave observation of the interior could test.

1 INTRODUCTION 

Radio pulsars (Hewish et al. 1968; Pilkington et al. 1968) are ex-ceptionally stable rotators, and the precision with which their pulses can be timed has made them laboratories for fundamental physics (Lorimer 2001; Manchester 2015; Verbiest et al. 2024). Timing of pulsars in relativistic binaries provides strong-field tests of general relativity (Hulse & Taylor 1975; Kramer et al. 2021), and the timing of arrays of millisecond pulsars has recently yielded support for a nanohertz gravitational wave background (Agazie et al. 2023; Anto-niadis et al. 2023; Reardon et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023; Miles et al. 2025; Yu & Allen 2025). This precision is nonetheless bounded by timing noise, a slow, red wandering of the rotational phase relative to a deterministic spin-down model that was recognized early in the Crab pulsar (Groth 1975) and that is present in most pulsars with long timing baselines (Hobbs et al. 2010). Timing noise limits the low-frequency sensitivity of pulsar timing experiments (Shannon & Cordes 2010; Lasky et al. 2015), but it is also a signal in its own right, since it carries information on the rotational dynamics of the neutron star (Melatos & Link 2014; Goncharov et al. 2020). A physical picture of timing noise is provided by two-component models of the neutron star, in which a crust that is electromagnetically coupled to the observed pulses exchanges angular momentum with an interior superfluid through mutual friction, and in which stochas-

The same two-component picture underlies the standard interpretation of glitches (Baym et al. 1969; Groth 1975; Andersson et al. 2012; Chamel 2013; Haskell & Melatos 2015). The coupling time scale has recently been constrained from timing noise, first for PSR J1359-6038 (O’Neill et al. 2024) and subsequently for a sample of UTMOST pulsars (Jankowski et al. 2019; Lower et al. 2020; Dunn et al. 2025; Dong et al. 2026). Within this picture a phys-ical question arises that the present work is concerned with. If the stochastic torques on the two components are independent, the total angular momentum of the star performs a random walk and the spin noise is nonstationary (Meyers et al. 2021a). If instead the stochastic torques are internal, equal and opposite between the components, the total angular momentum is conserved and the spin noise of each component is stationary (Antonelli et al. 2023). The stationarity of pulsar spin noise is therefore a statement about whether angular mo-mentum is conserved inside the neutron star, and it can in principle be tested by comparing two models, referred to below as singular and minimal, that realize the two alternatives. This work follows up Bernardo (2026), which obtained exact time-domain solutions for the singular two-component spin-noise model (Meyers et al. 2021a) and established the stochastic framework used here. The present work extends the analytical treatment to the full phase covariance of both the singular and minimal models and develops the corresponding inference problem at the observa-tional level. The present work is concerned with the same question at the observational level. Radio timing does not measure angular velocities. It measures pulse times of arrival, which are converted into rotational phases and then into timing residuals by fitting and subtracting a deterministic timing model that accounts for the spin frequency, the spin-down and other deterministic effects (Hobbs et al. 2006; Edwards et al. 2006; Manchester 2015). Only the crust par-ticipates in this, since the pulses are locked to the crust. The data that are available to test the physics are therefore crust-only phase residuals from which the lowest-order trends have been removed, and a test of spin noise stationarity must be carried out on these data, or on spin-frequency time series derived from them by differentiation (O’Neill et al. 2024). Each step between the physical state and the analyzed data has consequences that must be understood before the physics can be read off. Integration of a stationary angular velocity produces a phase that is not stationary, so that the nonstationarity of the phase is not by itself evidence for a nonstationary spin. The fitting and removal of the timing model suppresses the lowest frequencies, where the difference between a random walk and a stationary process is largest, and is itself known to leave nonstationary residuals (Lee et al. 2012; van Haasteren & Levin 2013; Allen et al. 2026). Differentiating the phases back into spin frequencies averages the state over finite windows and thins the sampling. None of these steps is the object of this work, but each of them could bias a test of stationarity that is carried out at the observational level, and each must therefore be treated exactly. We do so by deriving the phase-domain likelihood of the two models building on the analytical solutions of Bernardo (2026), with the timing model marginalized analytically, and by comparing inference in phase with inference in derived spin frequencies on the same simulated data. The result is a scalable, exact Gaussian process method for the phases, and an assessment of what radio timing alone can and cannot establish about the stationarity of spin noise. We first present the exact phase means and covariances of the singular and minimal models (Section 2). We then derive the phase likelihood with linear scaling in the number of observations, the analytic marginalization of the timing model, and the evidence and superfluid-prediction machinery used throughout (Section 3). The method is tested on simulated phase data generated from the singu-lar and the minimal model, with full-state and crust-only observa-tions (Sections 4–5). We then ask whether the same conclusions are reached when the phases are first processed into local spin frequen-cies (Section 6), and we discuss what pulsar timing can and cannot do to test the stationarity of spin noise and the internal conservation of angular momentum (Section 7). We conclude in Section 8. We include four appendices with additional details. Appendix A derives the finite-start kernel orientation and its multi-output sym-metry; Appendix B presents the semiseparable factorization and the forward and backward solves that give the likelihood its linear cost; Appendix C documents the analytic and numerical checks used to validate the implementation; and Appendix D records the evidence settings and reproducibility metadata for the cached ensembles.

Simulated phase data 

Figure 1 shows simulated noisy singular and minimal phase paths before and after projection. The simulation makes direct use of the analytical solutions of Section 2. 4 SINGULAR SIMULATIONS 

Consider the singular model as the data-generating process. This section provides a controlled test of the phase-domain inference and reconstruction when the rotational state is fully observed (full-state), and of the systematic effects that arise when only the crust is ob-served (crust-only). The noisy singular realization shown in Figure 1, with the model and noise parameters of Table 1 and the cadence of Section 3, is analyzed with the singular model in two observation settings: full state, in which both the crust and superfluid phases are observed, and crust only, in which the superfluid is hidden and must be predicted. In both settings the quadratic timing model of each observed component is marginalized analytically, so that the infer-ence concerns the spin-wandering parameters (𝜏, 𝑟, 𝑄 c, 𝑄 s) alone. The same fitted objects are used for the reconstructions, parameter posteriors and evidence calculations. Figure 2 shows the phase reconstructions and the corresponding full-state and crust-only posterior constraints. With full-state obser-vations, the smoothed crust and superfluid phases follow the latent phases within bands that are comparable to the measurement uncer-tainties; the conditioning of Section 3 then acts as a smoother for both components. With crust-only observations, the crust phase is recon-structed equally well, since the crust data are the same and the crust covariance is well described by either model. The superfluid predic-tion, however, is of a different nature. Its conditional mean stays close to zero and its band is wide, spanning several times the excursions of the latent superfluid phase. This is the conditional covariance of (51) at work. The cross covariance 𝐾 sc transfers information from the crust to the superfluid only through the exchange mode, which is subdominant in the crust phase for the parameters considered here, so that most of the superfluid variance remains unexplained by the crust data. The width of the band is therefore a faithful statement of what the crust alone can say about the superfluid in this model. The parameter posteriors reflect the same hierarchy. With full-state observations, the two diffusion coefficients are determined to within a factor of about two, with the superfluid coefficient 𝑄 s pinned by the superfluid channel. The relaxation time and the ratio 𝑟 remain broad even in this setting, with posteriors that lean toward shorter 

𝜏 and smaller 𝑟 than the injected values; for a window of about four relaxation times and a diffusive mode that dominates the low-frequency phase, the exchange mode that carries 𝜏 and 𝑟 leaves a comparatively weak imprint. With crust-only observations, the crust diffusion coefficient 𝑄 c is constrained about as well as with full-state data, because it sets the amplitude of the crust phase directly. In contrast, the superfluid coefficient becomes prior-dominated. Its marginal is nearly flat over the prior range, and its two-dimensional marginals with 𝜏 and 𝑟 show that the crust phase constrains only the combination 𝜏 2s 𝑄 s that enters the transformed intensities 𝑄 ± and 𝑄 × .The relaxation time and 𝑟 are likewise prior-dominated. The injected parameters lie inside the 95 percent intervals in both settings, so the crust-only posteriors are wide rather than biased; what is lost with the superfluid is the ability to attribute the observed crust wander to a specific partition of the stochastic torques between the components. The same realization is also fitted with the minimal model, and Figure 3 presents the log Bayes factor of equation (47) over indepen-dent realizations of the singular sampled mock data, with indepen-dent measurement-noise draws, using the estimators of Section 3. With full-state observations the Bayes factor is overwhelming. The median ln 𝐵 𝑠,𝑚 is of order several hundred and essentially all realiza-tions favour the singular model. The minimal model has no diffusive common mode and cannot reproduce the joint crust-superfluid co-variance of a singular realization, so that the full-state data rule it out decisively. With crust-only observations the picture changes qualitatively. The median ln 𝐵 𝑠,𝑚 is of order unity, corresponding to posterior odds of only about three to one in favour of the singular model, even though the sign of the Bayes factor selects the singular model in about 96 percent of the realizations. The consistency of the sign across realizations indicates that the crust phase does carry a signature of the diffusive mode, but the magnitude of the Bayes factor indicates that this signature is weak in any single realization of the length and cadence considered here. The models differ in their predictions for the hidden superfluid, which the crust data do not probe. This is the counterpart, from the oppo-site direction, of the singular experiment. Singular data leave a weak but consistent common-mode imprint in the crust, whereas minimal data leave nothing in the crust that the singular model cannot also produce. The prior study is correspondingly revealing. Reducing the sin-gular prior half-widths by one decade shifts the median crust-only ln 𝐵 𝑠,𝑚 to positive values, so that the majority of realizations favour the singular model, whereas enlarging them by one decade shifts the median to negative values and the majority to the minimal model; the nominal prior sits in between with no preference. The sign of the crust-only comparison is therefore set by the prior volume assigned to the singular model rather than by the data, precisely because the data do not constrain the coordinates that distinguish the two physical models. Taken together with the singular experiment (Section 4), this establishes the central result of the simulations. Full-state observa-tions recover the parameters of the generating process, reconstruct both phases and distinguish the stationary and nonstationary spin noise models reliably, whereas crust-only phase observations recon-struct the crust and, at most, leave a weak and prior-limited preference when the generating process is singular. 

6 PHASE OR ANGULAR VELOCITY 

The physical distinction between the two models is a statement about angular velocities. The minimal spin noise is stationary and the sin-gular spin noise is not. Radio timing, however, delivers phases, or equivalently timing residuals. There are then two routes to a test of the models. The phase can be analyzed directly, as with Dong et al. (2026) using the Kalman filter or with the likelihood of Section 3, or it can first be processed into a time series of local spin-frequency estimates, as was done for PSR J1359-6038 by O’Neill et al. (2024). The question we address in this section is whether the features that distinguish the models, stationarity or its absence, survive the chain of operations that separates the physical state from the analyzed data, and whether the two routes lead to the same conclusions. We do so by processing the same simulated phase data along both routes and comparing the results. It is useful to regard each step of the chain as a linear operator acting on the two-component state, since every such operator maps a Gaussian process onto another Gaussian process with a covariance that follows exactly. If 𝑶 is linear and 𝑲 is the covariance of its input, the output covariance is 𝑶𝑲𝑶 T. a differentiation operator, followed by the removal of a linear trend. We discuss the operators in turn. 

Selection of the crust 

Hiding the superfluid maps the multi-output covariance of Section 2 onto its crust block 𝐾 cc . This is an observability loss of physical origin and it is the same along both routes. It removes the parameter combinations that appear only in the cross and superfluid blocks. In the singular model, 𝑺 removes the partition of the stochastic torques between the components, which is why 𝑄 s and 𝑟 become prior-dominated in Section 4. In the minimal model, 𝑺 isolates the superfluid moment of inertia fraction 𝑥 s, which is absent from 𝐾 cc 

altogether. Sections 4-5 have shown that this operation alone reduces a decisive model comparison to a weak or absent one.

Radio timing data are phases, and the physical question is posed in angular velocity. The phase route analyses the data as they are delivered, with a likelihood that accounts exactly for the timing model and for the integration-induced nonstationarity, and it retains the short-lag information that a window-averaged angular velocity series compresses away. The angular velocity route places the analysis in the variables in which the physics is stated, at the cost of an intermediate processing step whose averaging and cadence compression must be kept in mind when interpreting the posteriors. Neither route recovers the information lost with a hidden superfluid state. 

7 WHAT PULSAR TIMING CAN DO? 

The question that motivates this work is whether radio pulsar timing can tell us whether the spin noise of a neutron star is stationary, and thereby whether the angular momentum of the star is conserved inter-nally or exchanged with the outside through independent stochastic torques on its components. The preceding sections allow a conserva-tive answer to be given, in three parts, on what timing can do on its own, on what it cannot, and on what becomes possible with external information. Radio timing on its own can be analyzed exactly for this purpose. The likelihood of Section 3 is written for the phases that timing delivers and it scales linearly with the number of observations. The simulations show that this likelihood reconstructs the crust phase and constrains the parameters that set the amplitude of the crust spin noise, the crust diffusion coefficient in the singular model and the torque strength 𝐴 min /𝜏 in the minimal model, from the crust alone. Timing also responds to the physics we are after. When the generating process has a diffusive total angular momentum, the crust phase carries an imprint of it that the singular model picks up, and the sign of the Bayes factor points to the singular model in most realizations. This is what one would hope for from a method that is to be applied to real pulsars, and it holds regardless of whether the phases are analyzed directly or first differentiated into spin frequencies. Radio timing on its own cannot, for observations of the span and quality simulated here, establish either alternative with confidence. The crust-only Bayes factors are of order unity for a singular gener-ating process, corresponding to odds of a few to one, and consistent with zero for a minimal generating process, and their magnitudes shift with the prior volume assigned to the singular model. The rea-son is that both models describe the observed crust phase equally well over a substantial part of their parameter space. The crust phase of the minimal model is fixed by two combinations of its param-eters, and the singular model reproduces it whenever its diffusive mode is weak enough to hide below the measurement noise. The parameters that distinguish the two pictures physically, the partition of the stochastic torques between the components in the singular model and the superfluid inertia fraction in the minimal one, do not enter the crust phase covariance and are returned as their priors. The crust-only comparison therefore reduces to a question of which model has the smaller effective prior volume near the observed crust covariance, which is a statement about the priors as much as about the star. A crust-only preference of the magnitude found here should accordingly be reported together with its prior dependence and not be read as a detection of a diffusive mode or of angular momentum conservation. External information changes the problem, and this is where the minimal model becomes falsifiable. Because its crust and superfluid phases are tied by the conservation law (32), the minimal model predicts the superfluid phase from the crust phase up to the single factor −𝑥 c/𝑥 s (Section 3). An independent estimate of the superfluid inertia fraction, such as that inferred from the angular momentum reservoir required by glitches in the same pulsar (Andersson et al. 2012; Haskell & Melatos 2015; Montoli et al. 2020), fixes this factor and turns the minimal model into a definite prediction for a quantity that timing does not observe, with a well-defined uncertainty inher-ited from the crust reconstruction. Any independent measurement of the superfluid rotation, however sparse, then tests the prediction directly, and continuous gravitational waves from the interior of the star are the natural candidate for such a measurement (Jaranowski et al. 1998; Haskell & Schwenzer 2021; Ming et al. 2022; Haskell & Schwenzer 2021; Haskell et al. 2022; Dergachev & Papa 2025; An-

tonelli et al. 2025). The singular model makes no comparably rigid prediction, since its superfluid phase depends on the partition of the stochastic torques, which the crust does not constrain. Nonetheless, an independent measurement of the superfluid moment of inertia fraction or the superfluid phase at a few epochs can be used to con-strain the parameter space. Whether a glitch-derived inertia fraction refers to the same effective superfluid component that participates in spin wandering is a question of neutron-star physics that the present framework does not settle, and we treat such an identification as a hypothesis to be tested rather than as an input. Two further directions follow from these considerations. A pulsar with both a long timing baseline and a glitch-derived inertia fraction is the immediate application. Beyond individual pulsars, the station-arity test could be carried out across a population. Isolated pulsars are the natural sample, since a binary companion provides an ex-ternal channel through which angular momentum may be exchanged stochastically with the pulsar, and a preference for the singular model in isolated pulsars would be harder to attribute to external torques. Millisecond pulsars whose timing noise is loud compared with the gravitational wave background are of particular interest, since they combine long, dense baselines with a spin noise that can be studied on its own. Such a study would ask whether the crust-only Bayes factors of a population lean consistently in one direction. 

8 CONCLUSIONS 

We have developed a scalable Gaussian process method for pulsar spin noise that works directly with the phases, or timing residu-als, delivered by radio pulsar timing. The method is built on the exact analytical solutions of the singular, nonstationary and the min-imal, stationary two-component models of a neutron star (Bernardo 2026). The crust phase covariances of the two models turn out to be semiseparable, of rank three and two respectively, which allows the likelihood to be evaluated with a cost that grows linearly with the number of observations, and the deterministic timing model is marginalized analytically within the same framework. The result is an exact likelihood for the observable of radio timing that is anchored to the dynamics of the two components. The physical question is whether the spin noise of a neutron star is stationary, which within two-component models is the question of whether the total angular momentum of the star is conserved in-ternally. It is posed in the angular velocities, or spin frequencies, whereas timing observes the crust phase. We have therefore had to deal with several operations that stand between the physics and the data. Integration turns a stationary angular velocity into a nonsta-tionary phase, so that a wandering phase is not by itself evidence for a random walk in the spin (Antonelli et al. 2023; Bernardo 2026). The least-squares removal of the timing model suppresses the lowest frequencies and is itself a source of nonstationarity in the residuals (Lee et al. 2012; van Haasteren & Levin 2013; Allen et al. 2026). Differentiation of the phases into local spin frequencies averages the state over finite windows and thins the sampling. We have treated each of these operations exactly, and we have found that none of them changes the qualitative answer to the physical question. The stationarity or nonstationarity of the spin noise is neither created nor destroyed on the way from the state to the data. What the operations change is how much of the distinction survives in a finite, noisy data set, and here the phase route retains the short-lag information that a window-averaged spin-frequency series compresses away, which matters most for the stationary model, whose relaxation time and amplitude are otherwise degenerate over observation spans of a few relaxation times. The simulations give the same central result as their angular veloc-ity counterparts. When both the crust and the superfluid are observed, the parameters of the generating process are recovered, both phases are reconstructed, and the stationary and nonstationary models are told apart decisively. When only the crust is observed, as in radio timing, the crust phase is reconstructed and the amplitude of its spin noise is measured, but the two models describe the observed crust equally well over much of their parameter space. A singular generat-ing process leaves a weak preference in its favour, with posterior odds of a few to one that shift with the prior volume of the singular model. A minimal generating process leaves no preference. Radio timing alone, for observations of the span and quality simulated here, does not establish either alternative with confidence, and the crust-only Bayes factors should be read as prior-limited. The minimal model nevertheless makes a prediction that timing alone does not. Because its stochastic torques conserve angular mo-mentum, it ties the superfluid phase to the crust phase up to a single factor set by the superfluid inertia fraction. An external estimate of this fraction, as inferred from glitches in the same pulsar, turns the minimal model into a falsifiable prediction for the hidden rotational state, which a continuous gravitational wave observation of the inte-rior could test.

APPENDIX D: EVIDENCE SETTINGS AND REPRODUCIBILITY 

Full-state ensembles use bounded Laplace evidence from the exact dense multi-output phase Gaussian process. The radio-only head-line uses stepping-stone evidence with normalized bounded priors. The cached high-statistics products contain 1000 full-state realiza-tions, 500 stepping-stone radio realizations and 200 singular-prior sensitivity realizations for each generator and domain. Their meta-data record deterministic seeds, bounds, configuration fingerprints, software context and acceptance diagnostics. The high-statistics pro-duction ladder used 80 retained steps, 40 burn-in steps, thinning by four and 1024 prior samples; smaller real-data applications may use longer chains. Laplace radio evidences are retained only as local-geometry diagnostics.
