ABSTRACT Magnetar sources are the leading theory for Fast Radio Burst (FRB) progenitors. If magnetars are indeed the dominant sources, we may thus expect any metallicity preference for magnetar formation to be reflected in the FRB site in host galaxies. Here, we leverage Integral Field Unit (IFU) spectroscopy to spatially-resolve gas-phase metallicity distribution in five FRB host galaxies, FRB 20240210A, FRB 20240312D, FRB 20250227A, FRB 20211127I, and FRB 20250316A, at z < 0.05, including the FRB site, with the O3N2 index. We observed the first four sources with the newly commissioned Large Lenslet Array Magellan Spectrograph (LLAMAS) IFU on the Magellan Telescopes and used archival Keck/KCWI data for FRB 20250316A. We find ISM metallicity variations of at least 0.3 dex within individual hosts for the majority of the sample, with median values spanning 12 + log(O /H) ≈ 8.5–8.7. At the burst sites, four have metallicities of 12 + log(O /H) ≈ 8.5–8.9, while the fifth has a metallic-ity below 8.5. These findings reveal substantial metallicity variations both among FRB host galaxies and within the local environments of the bursts. Consequently, our study shows that longslit-based global estimates of host galaxy metallicity do not always represent the true burst-site distributions. We therefore advocate for IFU follow-up of larger host samples to ultimately provide FRB-site relevant observational constraints on metallicity preferences for FRB occurrence.

INTRODUCTION Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond-duration ex-tragalactic radio transients whose progenitors remain observationally elusive, and the properties of their host galaxies have become one of the primary tools for con-straining candidate formation channels. Systematic studies of FRB hosts have largely focused on global host properties estimated from integrated photometry or spectroscopy. These have inferred that the population is diverse: FRBs mostly occur in actively star-forming galaxies, broadly consistent with a picture in which most progenitors form promptly after core collapse of mas-sive stars (e.g. K. E. Heintz et al. 2020; S. Bhandari et al. 2022; A. C. Gordon et al. 2023; K. Sharma et al. 2024). K. Sharma et al. (2024) additional reported a preference for massive hosts or a deficit of low-mass hosts (log( M∗/M ⊙) ≲ 9; total sample size NFRB = 20) at z ≤ 0.2 relative to the cosmic star-formation rate. Adopting a population-level mass–metallicity relation, they translated this deficit into an inferred suppression of FRB occurrence at 12 + log(O /H) < 8.08 +0 .61 

> −0.47

. Sub-sequently, S. Yamasaki et al. (2026) directly estimated oxygen abundances with the O3N2 index (M. Pettini & B. E. J. Pagel 2004) for a larger volume-limited sample of FRB hosts ( NF RB = 27; 0 .01 ≤ z ≤ 0.48) with emis-sion lines but found no evidence for a metallicity lower bound. Additionally, S. Yamasaki et al. (2026); A. M. Moroianu et al. (2026) and A. R. Muller et al. (2026) find hosts with low metallicity (12 + log(O /H) ∼ 8.0). Most recently, Y. Dong et al. (2026) examine a larger sam-ple of FRB hosts including five new dwarf hosts and the host sample of K. Sharma et al. (2024) and find evidence for metallicity preferences for FRBs. Specifically, they report that hosts of non-repeating FRBs occur prefer-entially at 12 + log(O /H) ≳ 8.35 while repeaters prefer 12 + log(O /H) ≲ 8.35. Additionally, FRBs associated with persistent radio sources (PRSs) prefer dwarfs with 12 + log(O /H) ≲ 8.15. On the theory front, a magnetar origin is the lead-ing source model for FRB progenitors (C. D. Bochenek et al. 2020; CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020; B. Zhang 2023), and any metallicity preference for magne-tar formation should be reflected in FRB occurrence in varying galaxy types. K. Sharma et al. (2024) have theo-rized that isolated massive stars can preferentially evolve into neutron stars, as opposed to black holes, in a solar or higher metallicity environment due to more efficient stellar winds (A. Heger et al. 2002). They also speculate

that in binary systems, higher metallicities can more reliably trigger faster Roche-lobe overflow (J. Klencki et al. 2020), which can lead to massive-star mergers that could lead to more frequent magnetars (A. J. Frost et al. 2024). In contrast, Y. Dong et al. (2026) pro-pose an alternative mechanism, specifically for repeating FRBs, wherein low-metallicity environments can pro-duce faster-rotating stars and subsequently faster mag-netars from lower mass loss in low-metallicity environ-ments (S. E. Woosley & J. S. Bloom 2006). Supporting evidence comes from A. Schootemeijer et al. (2022), who find a high fraction of extreme OBe stars in metal-poor dwarfs. Thus, the exact mechanism that drives metal-licity preference is debated. Regardless of the exact mechanism, granting that a metallicity-sensitive channel exists for magnetar forma-tion, an FRB is a local event which is only sensitive to its immediate neighborhood, and not necessarily the host galaxy’s global properties, i.e., the host metallic-ity inferred from integrated light might not be repre-sentative of the local value at the progenitor forma-tion site. Metallicity gradients spanning ∼ 0.3 dex are well-established in stacks of Sloan Digital Sky Sur-vey (SDSS) galaxies, with individual galaxies exhibiting even broader spatial variations (e.g. D. Zaritsky et al. 1994; S. D. Ryder 1995; F. Belfiore et al. 2017; J. Lian et al. 2018). More directly, T. Pessi et al. (2023) find that galaxies hosting core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe), a magnetar-formation channel, can show different metal-licity values at the transient site than the global host value. Thus, there is a clear need to characterize FRB hosts at the burst site, ideally with integral field unit (IFU) observations. Though the FRB literature does not lack IFU ob-servations nor inferences of host properties from them, (e.g., S. Simha et al. 2020; J. S. Chittidi et al. 2021; I. S. Khrykin et al. 2024; L. Bernales-Cortes et al. 2025; J. N. Jahns-Schindler et al. 2026), only two studies by S. P. Tendulkar et al. (2021) and CHIME/FRB Col-laboration et al. (2025) characterize local metallicity for FRB20180916B and FRB 20250316A respectively. Both studies are comparable with respect to FRB localization (∼ 50 mas) and offset from the nearest star-forming region ( ∼ 200 pc) to the FRB. They find that the local gas-phase metallicities are 8.4 and 8.65, respec-tively. Interestingly, these hosts also have global metal-licity estimates from longslit observations. B. Marcote et al. (2020) and A. C. Gordon et al. (2023) estimate 12+log(O /H) = 8 .8–8.9 for FRB20180916B using Gem-ini and Keck longslit observations, 0.4–0.5 dex different from the local value. Y. Dong et al. 2. SAMPLE DEFINITION AND IFU OBSERVATIONS Table 1 lists the sources in our sample consisting of five apparently non-repeating FRBs with ≲ 1′′ localizations. METHODS 3.1. Data reduction and cube construction 

The data were reduced using our GitHub fork of the LLAMAS-Pyjamas pipeline 19 (Hughes et al. 2026; in prep). The fork incorporates features from the rs-dev 

branch 20 of the parent repository but adds code for post-processing. The base pipeline performs bias and dark subtraction, flat fielding, wavelength calibration, sky subtraction, and cube construction for individual exposures. A detailed description of the post-processing steps is provided in Appendix A. Briefly, these include flux calibration, co-addition of cubes, and astrometric correction. We adapted code from the PypeIt package (J. X. Prochaska et al. 2020) to flux-calibrate the cubes using the standard star observations taken on the nights when the sources were observed. Then, for each FRB host, we reproject and co-add cubes from the individ-ual exposures after registering them to a common WCS. Finally, we extract a narrow-band image (10 ˚ A band-width) centered on the galaxy H α emission and align this to archival imaging from the Legacy Survey DR10 (A. Dey et al. 2019) or Pan-STARRS DR2 (H. A. Flewelling et al. 2020), which, in turn, are calibrated to Gaia Col-laboration et al. (2018). The results are flux-calibrated, coadded, and astrometrically calibrated cubes. Figure 1 illustrates the H α emission from our final cubes as con-tours overlaid on false-color images of the hosts. For the remainder of the analysis, we use the final cubes from the green channel alone, which includes the H β, [O iii ]λλ 4959 , 5007, H α, and [N ii ]λλ 6548 , 6583 emission lines. We note that we detect [O ii ]λ3726 , 3729 emission in the blue channel for all sources but refrain from using it in our analysis below, as we observe signif-icant artifacts from coadding the blue cubes with gaps from the two missing detectors.

We therefore apply a thorough masking procedure (described further below) to filter out these artifacts and isolate the galaxy light. 3.2. Emission-line maps 

We obtain line emission maps for Hβ,[O iii ] λλ 4959 , 5007, Hα, and [N ii ] λλ 6548 , 6584. We fit each spaxel of every coadded science cube’s spectrum independently, producing the spatially resolved (effec-tive PSF FWHM ≲ 2′′ ) flux maps shown in Figure 2. At each spaxel, two wavelength slabs corresponding to emission line groups – H β+[O iii ] (restframe wavelength range 4760-5110 ˚ A) and the H α+[N ii ](restframe wave-length range 6490-6620 ˚ A) – were fit independently with a cubic polynomial continuum plus a sum of Gaussian line profiles, with all lines in a given complex sharing one common redshift and line width as they are spec-trally unresolved. For H β, we also fit a Gaussian with a negative amplitude to model the Balmer absorption from the stellar components of the galaxy spectrum. We tied the flux ratio of line doublets, whose relative strength is fixed by atomic transition probabilities (the two [O iii ] and two [N ii ] lines), to their theoretical flux ratio rather than fit them independently. We propa-gated formal flux uncertainties for every line directly from each fit’s covariance matrix. To reject spurious emission from reduction or fitting artifacts, we restrict fits to the galaxy footprint, de-fined by H α at S /N ≥ 10 (i.e., spaxels not clearing this threshold are not considered for any other emis-sion line fit). For FRB 20211127I only, we additionally require H β at S /N ≥ 5 – a second, independent line whose own footprint suppresses a coherent banding ar-tifact otherwise present in that target’s green-channel cube. Within that footprint, a spaxel’s flux for any in-dividual line is retained only where that line itself inde-pendently clears S /N ≥ 3. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS To summarize our study, our work utilizes data from one of the earliest set of observations with the LLAMAS IFU, and we note the instrument is very well-suited for resolving the < 2 kpc environment of z < 0.05 galaxies. The 35 ′′ ×35 ′′ field of view is well matched to the typical sizes of such galaxies, while the sensitivity allows us to detect H α emission at the 10 −18 erg s −1 cm −2 arcsec −2

level in ∼ 1 hour of integration. We have observed five nearby FRB hosts with IFUs and estimated the gas-phase metallicity at the burst sites. We have compared these values against the distribution from the full host galaxy to determine if FRB sites have representative metallicities of their global host environments. We note different metallicity estimates at FRB sites compared to global host values from our IFU observa-tions. At least one FRB in our sample, FRB 20211127I, shows significantly different values compared to the me-dian value of the host. All four of our hosts show a spread of ∼ 0.1–0 .4 dex in metallicity across the galaxy, which is larger than the typical statistical errors on 12+log(O /H) from SED fitting or longslit-based strong-line diagnostics. The metallicities we estimate at four FRB positions cover a range of values (12+log(O /H) = 8 .5–8.9), and at the fifth site we place an upper limit of 12 + log(O /H) <

8.6. Our sample consists of non-repeaters and most re-cently, Y. Dong et al. (2026) find evidence that such FRBs may preferentially occur in hosts with global 

metallicity estimates of 12 + log(O /H) ≳ 8.35. We find our burst-site metallicity estimates are consistent with their work. However, the substantial metallicity varia-11 tions we measure indicate that longslit spectra can pro-duce biased estimates of metallicity depending on which regions the slit covers. This can result in a systematic er-ror comparable to the scatter in metallicity in the hosts. Thus we urge the FRB community to take this effect into account when interpreting global metallicity distri-butions for FRB hosts. We strongly advocate for IFU follow-up of larger host samples to ultimately provide FRB-site relevant observational constraints on metallic-ity preferences for FRB occurrence. In addition, we find that all five of the FRB sites we examined show robust H α detections within ∼ 2 kpc, establishing their association with recent star-formation activity. This can favor a magnetar origin for these bursts, assuming a prompt formation channel. How-ever, within the hosts, the FRB sites do not always cor-respond to the most actively star-forming regions, as also echoed in studies of the UV distribution of their host light (A. G. Mannings et al. 2021; J. S. Chittidi et al. 2021). Translating the observed burst-site Σ Hα

to a magnetar formation rate per projected area is not a trivial exercise (e.g., P. Beniamini et al. 2019). Thus we shall relegate to future work the estimation of the probability of a recent magnetar occurrence at the five burst sites. In general, if all magnetars are equally likely to pro-duce observable FRBs, and since most magnetars are expected to form from core-collapse supernovae (CC-SNe) (Q. Xi et al. 2025), one may expect a wide range of burst-site values as observed by R.-C. Hu & B. Zhang (2026) (12 + log(O /H)=8.1–8.7). The onset of the CHIME/FRB Outriggers full array (CHIME/FRB Col-laboration et al. 2025), DSA-2000 (G. Hallinan et al. 2019), BURSTT (H.-H. Lin et al. 2022) and the FAST Core Array (P. Jiang et al. 2024) will enable a larger sample of nearby FRB hosts, including repeaters, in which IFU studies will be crucial to further test this. ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS S. POST-PROCESSING OF REDUCED SCIENCE EXPOSURES After basic extraction and wavelength calibration of the individual fiber spectra into row-stacked-spectra (RSS) format for each of LLAMAS’s three spectral channels (blue, green, and red), each science exposure was converted to a datacube using the instrument’s fiber-to-sky mapping. This section describes the three post-processing steps applied to those cubes before scientific analysis: absolute flux calibration ( §A.1), multi-exposure spatial registration and combination ( §A.2), and astrometric calibration onto an archival imaging reference frame ( §A.3). A.1. Flux Calibration 

Absolute flux calibration was derived from observations of the CALSPEC spectrophotometric standard star HD 14943 (R. C. Bohlin 2014; R. C. Bohlin et al. 2020), obtained on the same night as the science exposures. The standard’s spectrum was extracted from the RSS data using a circular aperture centered on the fiber-weighted centroid of its continuum light; the aperture radius was chosen large enough that a curve-of-growth analysis of the star’s white-light image showed it recovered all ( > 98%) of the total flux, so aperture losses do not contribute meaningfully to the calibration uncertainty. The extracted count spectrum was compared against the star’s tabulated CALSPEC flux standard to derive a sensitivity function independently for each of the three channels, following standard long-slit/fiber spectrophotometric calibration practice (J. B. Oke 1990; M. Hamuy et al. 1992) as implemented in the open-source spectroscopic reduction package PypeIt (J. X. Prochaska et al. 2020). Each exposure’s airmass and integration time were propagated into the calibration together with an atmospheric extinction curve appropriate for the observing site,placing the resulting sensitivity functions on an absolute physical scale (erg s −1 cm −2 ˚A−1) independent of the specific conditions of any individual exposure. The appropriate per-channel sensitivity function was then applied to every spaxel of each science exposure’s datacube prior to spatial combination ( §A.2). A.2. Cube Alignment and Combination 

Each target was observed as a sequence of several individual exposures, splitting the total integration to reject cosmic rays. Before combining, we spatially registered the per-exposure cubes onto a common grid. For each target, we solved this registration using the green-channel exposures by cross-correlating each exposure’s broadband (white-light) continuum image against that of a designated reference exposure to measure its spatial offset. The resulting per-exposure astrometric corrections were then applied uniformly to the corresponding blue- and red-channel cubes from the same exposure, ensuring that all three channels of a given exposure share one internally consistent spatial registration, even though the alignment itself is solved only once, on the channel best suited to it. Once registered onto the common grid, the flux-calibrated exposures for each channel were combined via inverse-variance weighting using the pipeline’s propagated per-spaxel variance, producing a single cosmic-ray-rejected, depth-optimized datacube per spectral channel for each target. The reference exposure’s astrometric grid and field footprint were adopted as those of the final combined cube. A.3. Astrometric Calibration and Registration to Archival Imaging 

Since the pipeline-derived WCS solution was inaccurate, the combined cube at this stage was not correctly aligned to existing imaging. We took the following steps to rectify this: 1. We visually examined each combined cube and reoriented it by flipping the horizontal axis and subsequently rotating clockwise or anti-clockwise (as required) by 90 ◦ into a standard north-up, east-left orientation. 2. With further visual examination of the continuum image shown in Figure 2 we adjusted the CRVAL keywords to ensure the continuum centroid roughly matched the galaxy position in the catalog of the Pan-STARRS or Legacy Survey reference image. 3. We further refined the offset by subsequently cross-correlating the H α emission-line map (the H β map for FRB 20211127I, whose H α map shows banding) against the same reference image within a search window of 13 radius 1.5 times the FWHM of the galaxy’s continuum emission, centered on the coarse solution. The line map is clumpier, allowing us to identify substructure in emission that could also be seen in the legacy imaging. The cross-correlation peak is located on the reference image’s pixel grid, so the residual astrometric uncertainty is of order half a reference pixel ( ≈0.15”, or ≈50% of a spaxel). This procedure ties each combined cube’s astrometric solution directly to the Gaia reference frame (L. Lindegren et al. 2018; Gaia Collaboration et al. 2018; Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023) through the Pan-STARRS or Legacy Survey reference images, providing the relative astrometric precision needed to localize FRBs with respect to resolved host-galaxy substructure such as individual star-forming regions.
