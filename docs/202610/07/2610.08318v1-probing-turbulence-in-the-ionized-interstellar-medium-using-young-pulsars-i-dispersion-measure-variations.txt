ABSTRACT 

Context. Temporal variations in the dispersion measure (DM) of pulsars probe fluctuations in the free-electron density of the ionized interstellar medium (IISM). While DM variations have been studied extensively in millisecond pulsars, young pulsars provide a complementary probe due to their lines of sight (LoSs) intersecting supernova remnants, pulsar wind nebulae, and other turbulent environments. However, systematic studies of DM variability in young pulsars remain limited. 

Aims. We characterize the temporal DM variations of a large population of young pulsars to constrain the turbulence of the IISM along their LoSs and to investigate the physical origins of the observed variability. 

Methods. We analyzed 18 years of timing observations of 364 young pulsars obtained with the Murriyang /Parkes radio telescope, combining legacy data from the 21 cm Multibeam receiver with recent observations from the ultra-wideband low-frequency (UWL) receiver. Epoch-wise DM measurements were derived from sub-banded times of arrival, and the variability was characterized using linear trends and DM structure functions, which were compared with expectations for a Kolmogorov turbulence. 

Results. Significant DM variations are detected in 89 pulsars in the UWL data and in 23 pulsars in the multibeam data, with 16 in the combined dataset showing significant trends. This represents one of the largest long-baseline studies of DM variability in pulsars. We find tentative evidence that the observed DM variations are predominantly consistent with Kolmogorov turbulence. PSRs J1105– 6107 and J1829–1751 show estimated DM variations driven by periodic profile variability. We also identify 21 bright, slow pulsars exhibiting short-term DM variations that exceed expectations from the IISM. 

Conclusions. Long-baseline timing of young pulsars is a powerful probe of IISM turbulence on large scales. The emergence of DM measurements limited by intrinsic pulse-to-pulse variability highlights a key limitation for IISM studies at next-generation facilities with high sensitivity and large fractional bandwidths.

1. Introduction 

The rotational stability of pulsars enables the high-precision tim-ing of their beamed radio emission, which is observed as peri-odic pulses when the beam intersects our line of sight (LoS) to the pulsar. Consequently, temporal changes in the free electron content along the LoS manifest themselves as variations in the measured DM. As the pulsar, Earth, and IISM all move relative to one an-other, the LoS sweeps through di ff erent regions of the electron density distribution, and the resulting DM variations encode in-formation about the structures encountered along the way (e.g., Rankin & Roberts 1971). These variations take several forms. Linear trends can arise when the pulsar’s transverse velocity dominates the relative motion, causing the LoS to drift steadily through a density gradient (e.g., Keith et al. 2013; Lam et al. 2016). Evidence of such variability dates back nearly five decades. Isaacman & Rankin (1977) discovered DM variations in the Crab pulsar (PSR B0531 +21), where the DM increased by ap-proximately 0.02 pc cm −3 yr −1 over a six-year period as the LoS evolved within its host supernova remnant (SNR; Lyne et al. 1988). Around the same time, the Vela pulsar (PSR J0835–4510) was found to have a decreasing DM of 0.04 pc cm −3 yr −1, which was attributed to the pulsar’s transverse motion sampling di ff er-ent parts of the Vela SNR (Hamilton et al. 1977, 1985). Annual modulations can be introduced by the Earth’s or-bit, which changes the solar elongation angle and, therefore, the solar wind contribution to the DM (e.g., You et al. 2007b; Tiburzi et al. 2019; Susarla et al. 2024). In addition, high-density plasma structures, such as those responsible for extreme scat-tering events, can act as diverging lenses and produce local-ized transient DM excursions (e.g., Coles et al. 2015; Kerr et al. 2018). Superimposed on these trends are stochastic fluctuations driven by turbulence and anisotropy in the IISM (see Eq. 3; e.g., Rickett et al. 2002; Brisken et al. 2010). These early detections hinted at a broader picture in which the stochastic component of DM variations directly probes the spatial power spectrum of electron density fluctuations. The connection between DM variability and the pulsar’s en-vironment has been exploited in numerous population studies of pulsars and their DM variations (e.g., Phillips & Wolszczan 1991; You et al. 2007a; Keith et al. 2013; Jones et al. 2017; Donner et al. 2020). Most of these studies have focused on mil-lisecond pulsars (MSPs), whose exceptional rotational stability makes them ideal for high-precision timing experiments. How-ever, the LoSs to MSPs primarily sample the large-scale, di ff use IISM far from active star-forming regions. Young pulsars occupy a fundamentally di ff erent regime. They are often still embedded in or near their birth SNRs and surrounding pulsar wind nebulae (PWNe), where the local electron density and turbulence can ex-ceed the ambient IISM values by orders of magnitude. Their DM variations therefore carry imprints of both the di ff use IISM and these dense, structured local environments, providing access to turbulence on spatial scales and in physical conditions that MSP studies cannot probe. Despite this potential, the DM variations of young pulsars remain poorly characterized on a population level. Petro ff et al. (2013) analyzed 168 young pulsars observed at 0.7, 1.4, and 3.1 GHz with a 40, 1024, and 256 MHz bandwidth over five years at the Murriyang /Parkes radio telescope but, because of the significant uncertainties in their DM estimates, found significant DM variations in only four objects. More recently, Keith et al. (2024) studied 597 pulsars over four years with the radio tele-scope MeerKAT and identified 87 pulsars with significant DM gradients. Such gradients can be a signature of a Kolmogorov-like energy cascade, in which the power-law nature of the elec-tron density fluctuations produces correlated, quasi-linear DM trends. These results raise a natural question: to what extent are the DM variations of young pulsars driven by Kolmogorov tur-bulence in the di ff use IISM, and to what extent by the dense, structured environments in which some of these pulsars reside? Answering this question requires both a large sample of young pulsars and a long observational baseline with su ffi cient band-width to measure DM variations combined with the precision needed to distinguish between these scenarios. In this paper, we present one of the largest and longest DM variation studies of young pulsars to date. We analyzed 364 young pulsars observed with the Murriyang /Parkes radio telescope over more than 18 years, combining data from the 21 cm Multibeam receiver (Staveley-Smith et al. 1996) with re-cent observations from the ultra-wideband low-frequency re-ceiver (UWL; Hobbs et al. 2020). The UWL provides continu-ous frequency coverage from 704 to 4032 MHz, and its 3.3 GHz instantaneous bandwidth reduces per-epoch DM uncertainties by an order of magnitude relative to the previous multibeam system. This combination of a long timing baseline with wideband pre-cision allowed us to detect subtle and long-term DM variations that were inaccessible to earlier studies and to characterize the turbulence spectrum along individual LoSs. This paper is structured as follows. Sect. 2 describes the observations and analysis methods, including the DM extrac-tion and structure function (SF) estimation. Sect. 3 presents the population-level results and detailed case studies of individual pulsars with notable LoSs. We conclude in Sect. 4.

We analyzed the multibeam and UWL datasets separately but followed the same prescription to create the template of the pulse profile used to measure the times of arrival (ToAs). To create the template for each pulsar, we time-averaged a significant num-ber of high signal-to-noise ratio (S /N) observations, which were chosen based on the median S /N from the archives, and used the

To determine the number of frequency channels to use, we weighted the channels used by the median S /N and the bandwidth of the archives. We then removed the outlier ToAs using a Huber-regression-based rejection scheme outlined in Tiburzi et al. (2019).

> DM slope as a first-order diagnostic for DM variations

As a first-order characterization of the DM variability in our sample, we performed a reduced chi-squared linear fit to each DM time series and quantified the significance of the trend using the fitted slope and its associated uncertainty. We use the abso-lute slope as a proxy for the amplitude of DM variations. Follow-ing this approach, we considered a DM slope to be significant if it exceeds the 5 σ level. As noted by Petro ff et al. (2013), a lin-ear fit is insensitive to variations occurring on timescales shorter than the data span, but it provides a robust and uniform statistic for identifying pulsars with significant DM variations across the full sample. 

> DM structure functions

A more complete characterization of the DM variability is pro-vided by the DM SF, which measures the average squared di ff er-ence in DM as a function of the time lag between measurements, 

DDM (τ) = D[DM( t + τ) − DM( t)] 2E , (4) where ⟨· · · ⟩ denotes an ensemble average and τ is the time lag that separates two DM measurements. The SF encodes the tur-bulence spectrum of the IISM along the LoS: in the intermediate lag regime, where the data span is shorter than the outer scale of the turbulence, the SF follows a power law whose slope is directly related to the spectral index β of the electron density wavenumber spectrum (see Eq. 3). For a Kolmogorov medium with β = 11 /3, the SF scales as τ5/3. At the smallest lags, the SF is dominated by the measurement noise floor, and at the largest lags, it saturates as the time lag approaches the outer scale of the turbulence. In full, the observed SF can be decomposed as (e.g., Lam et al. 2016; Jones et al. 2017) 

DDM (τ) = Dsys DM (τ) + Dpl DM (τ) + Dnoise DM (5) where the three terms correspond to systematic trends, a turbu-lent power-law component, and a white-noise floor, respectively. (β = 4) dependence above the measurement noise floor (e.g., Lam et al. 2016), while periodic modulations appear as dips at harmonics of the corresponding period, which can be caused by annual variations in the solar wind or the ionosphere (e.g., Tiburzi et al. 2019). > Structure function error estimation

As the time lag approaches the length of the dataset, the limited number of independent realizations in the data limits the statisti-cal reliability of the SF (Rickett et al. 2000). Many young pulsars in our sample lack phase-connected timing solutions, making standard noise modeling with a Bayesian timing pipeline di ffi -cult. We therefore adopted a two-step approach to estimating the SF uncertainties. First, we modeled the SF uncertainties that arise from the uncertainties on the estimated DM values. For each pulsar, we generated 10000 realizations of the DM time series by drawing each epoch independently from a Gaussian distribution centered on the estimated DM value with a standard deviation equal to its measurement uncertainty. We computed the SF for each realiza-tion at the sampled time lag and took our SF error bars to be the standard deviation of the resulting simulated SFs at each lag. Second, we modeled the SF uncertainties that arise from the assumption that the DM variations are described by a power-law process (see Eq. 3). We first performed a least-squares fit to the observed SF, modeling it as the sum of a power-law component and a white-noise floor, DDM = Cτβ−2 + 2σ2 (see Appendix B of Lam et al. 2016). We used this fit to calibrate the amplitude and spectral index for our simulated DM time series: for each pul-sar, we generated 10000 realizations drawn from this power-law spectrum and took the 68% confidence interval of the resulting SFs as the uncertainty at each time lag. Finally, we refit the observed SF to the same model with the simulation-derived uncertainty band using the same least-squares fit. Additionally, we performed a Bayesian fit to the same model using the nested sampling algorithm dynesty (Speagle 2020) as implemented in bilby (Ashton et al. 2019) and sampled the parameters with a log-uniform and uniform prior log 10 C ∈

[−12 , 4] and β ∈ [0 , 7]. We note that both these prior ranges are deliberately chosen to be wide to avoid biasing the fit and should be regarded as conservative bounds rather than physically moti-vated constraints. As discussed in Sect. 1, the observationally ex-pected range for β is much narrower than the prior range adopted here. Similarly, the broad prior range for log 10 C far exceeds the range of values for a power-law amplitude expected for our data. For log 10 σ, we used a truncated Gaussian prior centered around the value inferred from the direct fit with a standard deviation of 2 and truncated to the same range as log 10 C. This prior is cho-sen because the constraints on log 10 σ significantly impact those on β. The least-squares and Bayesian fits yield consistent results in the scintillation regime when β is constrained in the Bayesian fit. We find that the uncertainty estimates on the parameters from the least-squares fit, derived from the diagonal of the covariance matrix alone, are significantly lower than those derived from the parameter posteriors of the Bayesian fit. We attribute this to the fact that the least-squares method does not capture the full de-generacy between the parameters, for example, between the am-plitude and the spectral index, and the power-law amplitude and the white noise floor at the shortest lags. The Bayesian method samples the full parameter space and therefore captures these de-generacies, yielding more conservative uncertainties. Through-out the main text, we quote the uncertainties from the Bayesian fit. For completeness, both sets of uncertainties are listed in Ap-pendix A. We note that these simulations only model a stochastic power-law process and do not include a deterministic linear DM gradient. Although a power-law spectrum stochastically induces apparent linear trends over the finite span of our dataset, a gradi-ent can arise from the changing distance to the pulsar due to the

Therefore, the inferred spectral index β should be interpreted with caution, as it may in-clude contributions from the motion of the pulsar rather than the underlying turbulent spectrum alone. 

3. Results and discussion 

We detect significant DM variations at the ≥ 5σ level in 89 pul-sars in the UWL data, 86 of which are direct detections based on the significance of the DM gradient measured from the per-epoch DM estimates; the remaining 3 required additional mod-eling (Appendix B), as well as in 23 pulsars in the multibeam data. There are 16 pulsars that also show a significant DM slope detection in the combined dataset. The full results are tabulated in Appendix A. This represents a substantial improvement over the four detections reported by Petro ff et al. (2013) for the same telescope, which we attribute to two main factors: our data time span exceeds theirs by more than a factor of two, and the 3.3 GHz instantaneous bandwidth of the UWL reduces per-epoch DM un-certainties by approximately an order of magnitude relative to the 256 MHz multibeam system. Our measurements are consis-tent with theirs over the overlapping data span. 

3.1. Excluded pulsars 

Not all pulsars with significant DM gradients enter the count above. We excluded two pulsars whose apparent DM variations are driven by profile shape changes rather than IISM propaga-tion e ff ects. PSR J1428–5530 (B1424–55) shows pulse-width variations most prominent in the trailing edge of the profile (Lower et al. 2025), while PSR J1825–0935 (B1822–09) ex-hibits correlated changes in the interpulse and main pulse am-plitude with the same period as the spin frequency (Yan et al. 2019). PSR J1105–6107 and PSR J1829–1751 show periodic profile evolution but are included in the sample because the lin-ear component in their DM variations can be disentangled from the periodic variations since the periodic variations are approx-imately symmetric around the linear trend and therefore largely average out over time (see Sect. 3.4.3 for a full discussion). More broadly, intrinsic profile evolution across the UWL bandwidth is a potential source of bias in DM estimates as the pulse profile can vary significantly across the full bandwidth. To mitigate this eff ect, we used frequency-resolved templates of each pulsar (see Sect. 2.1), such that the template at each sub-band captures the profile shape at that observing frequency. A further 21 pulsars show ≥ 3σ DM slopes in the UWL data but are also excluded from the main sample because their epoch-to-epoch DM scatter significantly exceeds the level expected from IISM turbulence. We identified these pulsars based on two criteria: the epoch-to-epoch variations greatly exceed the DM uncertainties, and the a ff ected pulsars are all bright (S /N ≳ 500) with spin periods P ≳ 0.3 s. Both properties point to pulse jit-ter as the dominant noise source rather than genuine DM vari-ability driven by IISM turbulence. To still obtain a first-order estimate of the DM variations, we inflated the DM uncertain-ties with an additional white-noise term in quadrature (EQUAD). Using these uncertainties, we find three additional pulsars with a significant DM slope (see Appendix B for details). This brings the total number of pulsars with significant DM variations in the UWL data to 89 pulsars. The individual pulsars with the most notable LoS are discussed in Sect. 3.4. 

> 3.3. Scattering as a source of DM bias

A second chromatic e ff ect present in our data is pulse broad-ening due to multipath scattering in the IISM. A scattered pulse often exhibits an exponential tail with a characteristic timescale τs ∝ ν−α, where α = 4 for a thin scattering screen (Cronyn 1970; Lang 1971) and α = 4.4 for a Kolmogorov medium (Lee & Jokipii 1976; Rickett 1977). Because the scat-tering delay itself is frequency-dependent, it can bias sub-banded ToAs and thus contaminate per-epoch DM measurements (e.g., Geiger et al. 2025). 3.4. Individual pulsars with notable DM variations 

3.4.1. PSR J0835–4510 and PSR J0908–4913 

PSR J0835–4510 and PSR J0908–4913 o ff er a rare opportunity to test whether IISM turbulence driven by SNR environments is consistent with a Kolmogorov energy cascade. Both pulsars are embedded in, or closely associated with, local SNRs and PWNe, placing their LoSs in environments where the electron density and turbulence amplitude are expected to far exceed the di ff use IISM. PSR J0835–4510 (B0833–45; Large et al. 1968) has a spin period of 89 ms and a spin-down energy loss rate of 6 .9 ×

10 36 ergs s −1. The pulsar is located within the Vela SNR at a well-constrained distance of 290 pc (Caraveo et al. 2001) and drives the PWN Vela–X (Weiler & Panagia 1980). The Vela SNR clump contributes significantly to the electron column den-sity along this LoS. PSR J0908–4913 (B0906–49; D’Amico et al. 1988) has a spin period of 106 ms and a spin-down energy loss rate of 4.9 × 10 35 ergs s −1. Its distance is less certain: H i absorption measurements place an upper limit of 6.7 kpc (Koribalski et al. 1995), while DM model-based distance estimates from YMW16 and NE2025 (Ocker & Cordes 2026) yield a lower limit of 1.0 kpc. We adopted 3 kpc, which is consistent with the pul-sar’s pulsed gamma-ray emission (Abdo et al. 2013) and with the revised environment analysis of Johnston & Lower (2021). Gaensler et al. (1998) detected a clear PWN and bow shock around PSR J0908–4913; the implied average hydrogen den-sity along the LoS is nH > 2 cm −3 at 6.7 kpc, compared to 

nH ≈ 0.08 cm −3 at 3 kpc, the latter being consistent with typ-ical IISM values (Johnston & Lower 2021). Johnston & Lower (2021) also found a possible association between PSR J0908– 4913 and the SNR candidate G320.4–1.0; if confirmed, this would place the pulsar’s true age at ∼13 kyr, far below its char-acteristic age of 112 kyr. 

DM time series: The DM time series for both pulsars, spanning 18 years of combined multibeam and UWL data, are shown in the top panels of Fig. 4. Both pulsars show significant, time-correlated DM variations across both receiver systems. For PSR J0835–4510, we measure a decreasing DM slope of (−2.03 ±0.04) ×10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1 over the full 18-year baseline. The overlapping multibeam data from Petro ff et al. (2013), cov-ering February 2007 to October 2012, exhibit a nearly flat DM trend, consistent with the shallow slope they reported. Looking further back, Hamilton et al. (1985) found a much steeper de-crease of −0.040 cm −3 pc yr −1 from observations taken between 1970 and 1985, which they attributed to a magnetized filament moving out of the LoS within the Vela SNR. The flat structure seen in the Petro ff et al. (2013) data is consistent with this fila-ment having moved fully out of the LoS by the mid-2000s. The varying slopes across these three epochs illustrate how the ob-served DM gradient for Vela reflects transient local structures rather than a steady bulk flow, underlining the importance of long baselines to correctly characterize the turbulence. For PSR J0908–4913, we find an overall DM slope of (−1.57 ± 0.04) × 10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1 from the combined dataset. The UWL data alone show a slope an order of magnitude smaller in absolute value because the UWL time series cap-tures a decrease that flattens and reverses near the end of our data span. Petro ff et al. (2013) measured a decreasing slope of −0.030 ± 0.001 cm −3 pc yr −1, consistent with Keith et al. (2024). Rather than the dense PWN environment invoked by Petro ff et al. (2013) to explain the elevated DM variations, a sim-pler physical explanation is the association between PSR J0908– 4913 and SNR candidate G320.4–1.0: material from the SNR shell crossing our LoS would produce the observed filamentary DM variations. 

Structure functions: To characterize the turbulence spectrum along both LoSs, we computed the DM SFs for the combined datasets, which are shown in the bottom panels of Fig. 4. At time lags shorter than those sampled by the DM time series, we anchored the SF using measured DISS timescales: ∆τd ∼ 70 s at 8.4 GHz for PSR J0835–4510 (Johnston et al. 1998), and 

∆τd ∼ 59 s at 8.5 GHz for PSR J0908–4913 (Johnston & Lower 2021). 4 Fitting the SF with a power law and a constant noise floor, we find spectral indices of β = 3.8 ± 0.2 and β = 3.5 ± 0.1for PSR J0835–4510 and PSR J0908–4913, respectively. Both values are consistent with the Kolmogorov expectation of β =

11 /3 ≈ 3.67 within 5 σ, although the median β inferred for J0908–4913 is significantly shallower than expected from a Kol-mogorov turbulence. The measured SF and our Kolmogorov simulation bands lie systematically above the extrapolation from the DISS timescale at all lags, rather than coinciding with it. Because our simula-tions model only the stochastic density-fluctuation component and do not include a deterministic linear trend (see Sect. 2), this o ff set can be explained by the fact that we did not model the SF contribution from the changing Earth-pulsar distance. We lack measurements of the parallel motion for J0835–4510 and J0908–4913, so we cannot directly quantify the distance-change contribution and thereby distinguish a quasi-linear trend of the wavenumber spectrum from contamination by a system-atic trend. We further note that the scintillation timescale is itself expected to vary epoch-to-epoch as the LoS samples di ff erent IISM electron densities, so the DISS anchor point should be re-garded with caution.

> 3.4.3. PSR J1105–6107 and PSR J1829–1751

PSR J1105–6107 (Kaspi et al. 1997) has a spin period of 63 ms and a spin-down energy loss rate of 2 .5 × 10 36 ergs s −1. Upon its discovery, a possible association with the SNR G290.1– 0.8 was proposed. Based on its DM value, the distance to this pulsar is estimated to be ∼ 2.4 kpc using the YMW16 model. Compared to J1105–6107, PSR J1829–1751 (B1826– 17; Davies et al. 1973) is a slower (300 ms) and less energetic ( ˙ E = 7.6 × 10 33 ergs s −1) pulsar located at a DM-based distance between 4.4 kpc (NE2025) and 5.9 kpc (YMW16). The primary interest of these two pulsars is not in their local IISM environ-ments, but in whether their measured DM variations genuinely trace IISM turbulence or are instead contaminated by pulse pro-file changes driven by their magnetospheres. For both pulsars, we find a DM time series dominated by a linear trend with a superimposed quasi-periodic modu-lation. A Lomb-Scargle periodogram analysis shows a signifi-cant period of 0.347 yr −1 (∼ 1052 days) for PSR J1105–6107 and 0.329 yr −1 (∼ 1110 days) for PSR J1829–1751. The pe-riodicity in PSR J1829–1751 is not new: Hobbs et al. (2010) identified a 2.4–3.2 yr periodicity in its timing residuals, and Shaw et al. (2022), Ni¸ tu et al. (2022), and Lower et al. (2025) re-covered the same period in its spin-down rate. PSR J1105–6107 is also known to exhibit correlated profile and spin-down varia-tions (Brook et al. 2016; Lower et al. 2025), although these are not strictly periodic. Lyne et al. (2010) showed that such quasi-periodic modulations in young pulsars are driven by changes in the pulsar magnetosphere and are unrelated to their IISM envi-ronments. To test whether the same mechanism operates in PSR J1105– 6107, we compute the post-fit timing residuals of both pulsars using tempo 2. The DM values (linear trend subtracted) and the timing residuals are shown in Fig. 6. For PSR J1829–1751, we find a DM slope of dDM /dt = (−1.81 ±0.12) ×10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1

over the UWL span and a Spearman correlation coe ffi cient of 

ρ = −0.636 ( p-value 3 .18 × 10 −12 ) between the DM residu-als and post-fit timing residuals. For PSR J1105–6107, we find dDM /dt = (2 .55 ± 0.11) × 10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1 and ρ = 0.472 ( p-value 7 .07 × 10 −6). Both correlations are significant. In contrast, PSR J0908–4913, a pulsar known to show nonperiodic profile and spin-down variations (Brook et al. 2016; Lower et al. 2025), does not show significant correlation between the DM time se-ries and the timing residuals ( ρ = 0.064, p-value 0 .73) and does not exhibit any periodic structure in the DM variations. This strengthens the interpretation that the correlated behavior seen in PSR J1829–1751 and PSR J1105–6107 arises from periodic profile variability contaminating the DM measurements. We emphasize that the apparent periodic DM variations ob-served in PSRs J1105–6107 and J1829–1751 do not represent genuine changes in the electron density content along the LoS in the IISM. Instead, they arise because of the assumption of a static pulse template to model the frequency-dependent variations in the pulse profile that bias our sub-banded ToAs and, therefore, our DM measurements. The statistically significant correlations between the post-fit timing residuals and the DM variations sup-port this hypothesis, which shows that the profile changes likely originating from the pulsar’s magnetosphere introduce system-atic biases. Disentangling these e ff ects from other apparent peri-odic DM variations due to, for example, an intervening scatter-ing screen requires a more detailed study into the frequency- and time-dependent evolution of the pulse profile using wide-band receivers such as the UWL receiver.

> 3.4.5. PSR J1413–6141

PSR J1413–6141 (Kramer et al. 2003) has a spin period of 285 ms and a spin-down energy loss rate of 5 .6 × 10 35 ergs s −1.It sits in one of the most complex regions of our sample: within 1 ◦ on the sky, there are three additional pulsars of var-ious ages (e.g., Chambery et al. 2023), the SNR G312.4–0.4, and the gamma-ray source 3EG J1410–6147. Early work by Manchester et al. (2002) proposed an association between the SNR and PSR J1413–6141, but Doherty et al. (2003) subse-quently found PSR J1412–6145 to be a more likely link with the SNR. The gamma-ray emission is associated with a highly ener-getic pulsar J1410–6132 (O’Brien et al. 2008). PSR J1413–6141 itself does not have confirmed gamma-ray emission (Smith et al. 2019). Nevertheless, with a DM-based distance estimate be-tween 8.5 kpc and 10 kpc, its LoS would still traverse mate-rial associated with both the SNR and the gamma-ray region, so these structures may still influence the observed DM variations even without a physical association. PSR J1413–6141 shows one of the steepest DM gradients in our sample. From the UWL data (Fig. 8), we measure a slope of (23 .27 ± 0.61) × 10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1. The DM time series is strongly dominated by this linear trend, which is reflected in the spectral slope value of β = 4.0 ± 0.5 in the SF. The physical origin of this gradient is di ffi cult to isolate. If the dominant contribution is the pulsar’s transverse motion sweeping through an electron density gradient, the implied mean electron density along the LoS would be ne ≳ 1.1 × 10 4 cm −3,assuming the transverse velocity upper limit of 200 km s −1 from Doherty et al. (2003). This density is unphysically high for a typ-ical IISM environment, exceeding even dense H ii regions by an order of magnitude, which suggests that transverse motion alone cannot account for the observed slope. An additional con-tribution from the pulsar’s radial motion is likely: as the pulsar moves along the LoS, its distance to Earth changes gradually, al-tering the total integrated DM even in a uniform medium (e.g., Lam et al. 2016). The steep gradient therefore most probably re-flects a combination of radial motion and transverse sampling of dense structures along the LoS (such as the SNR G312.4– 0.4 material). Precise measurements of the proper motion of this pulsar, as well as distance estimates, could help disentangle the relative contributions to the observed DM variations.

3.4.7. PSR J1731–4744 

PSR J1731–4744 (B1727–47; Large et al. 1968) is a pulsar with a spin period of 830 ms and a spin-down energy loss rate of 1.1 × 10 34 ergs s −1. The DM-based distance estimate places the pulsar at approximately 2.2 kpc (NE2025) and 5.5 kpc (YMW16) from Earth. Shternin et al. (2019) show that the pul-sar’s proper motion is consistent with originating from the SNR G343.0–6.0 (RCW 114). If this association is correct, the pul-sar would instead lie at a much smaller distance of ∼ 0.6 kpc. This possible association is further supported by the results of Main et al. (2023) and Lower et al. (2021). The DM variations observed in PSR J1731–4744 are among the most extreme in our sample (see Fig. 10). Before Au-gust 2020, the data show a negative slope of ( −2.33 ± 0.14) ×

10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1. After this, the trend increases sharply with a positive slope of (12 .34 ± 0.54) × 10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1. Moreover, the rotation measure time series exhibits an identical correlation compared to the DM time series (Johnston et al. 2021, Melfor et al. in prep.), supporting the fact that the electron column density is changing in the LoS. The DM SF is not well described by a simple power law with a constant noise floor and therefore appears inconsistent with Kolmogorov turbulence over the sampled length scales. However, this does not necessarily imply that the underlying wavenumber spectrum deviates from a Kolmogorov form. Be-cause the observations sample only a finite range of spatial scales from a single realization of the turbulent medium, the measured SF could reflect a portion of the spectrum near a turnover or break. In this case, the apparent deviation from a power law would arise from the limited data span rather than from a fun-damentally di ff erent turbulence spectrum in the IISM. Alternatively, the observed DM variations could be influ-enced by the local environment of the associated SNR. This interpretation is supported by the results of Main et al. (2023), who measured a short scintillation timescale and clearly resolved scintles in frequency, indicating a scattering screen located close to the pulsar. While some pulsars embedded in or close to SNRs, such as PSR J0835–4510 and PSR J0908–4913, exhibit DM SFs consistent with Kolmogorov turbulence, others show more com-plex behavior. Distinguishing between these possibilities requires contin-ued long-term monitoring of the DM variations of PSR J1731– 4744. In addition, measurements of the DISS timescale and bandwidth over a longer baseline would provide complementary constraints on the scattering environment and help determine whether the observed variations are consistent with Kolmogorov turbulence or instead arise from localized plasma structures. 

3.4.8. PSR J1833–0827 

PSR J1833–0827 (B1830–08; Clifton & Lyne 1986) is a pul-sar with a spin period of 85 ms and a spin-down energy loss rate of 5 .8 × 10 35 ergs s −1. The pulsar lies close to the radio-bright shell SNR G23.3–03. A proper motion measurement by Hobbs et al. (2005) is consistent with the pulsar originating within this remnant. In addition, a relic PWN associated with the pulsar was detected by Esposito et al. (2011) and later confirmed by Kargaltsev et al. (2012) using XMM-Newton observations. Figure 11 shows the DM time series for PSR J1833–0827 derived from the multibeam and UWL datasets. We measure an overall decreasing slope of ( −3.24 ± 0.25) × 10 −2 cm −3 pc yr −1.Although Keith et al. (2024) reported a positive slope, their re-sult reflects the increase in DM observed between approximately 2020 and 2022. Earlier work by Petro ff et al. (2013) reported a strong decreasing slope of −0.13 ± 0.02 cm −3 pc yr −1, the largest DM gradient detected in their sample. Petro ff et al. (2013) suggested that these DM variations could originate from the X-ray PWN, with a dense filament crossing the LoS when the DM increases. However, in our data, the DM variations appear to show a quasi-periodic structure. Such behavior is di ffi cult to reconcile with a single transient fila-ment crossing the LoS and instead suggests a more persistent or structured origin for the observed variations in the time series. 4. Conclusions 

We have analyzed DM variations in a sample of 364 young, en-ergetic pulsars observed with the Murriyang /Parkes radio tele-scope over 18 years, combining legacy multibeam data with UWL observations. The substantially wider bandwidth and im-proved per-epoch DM precision of the UWL receiver, combined with the extended time baseline, have enabled the most sensitive characterization of DM variability in young pulsars to date. We detect significant ( ≥ 5σ) DM gradients in 23 and 89 pul-sars in the multibeam and UWL datasets, respectively, with 16 pulsars in the combined dataset showing significant variations. This represents a substantial improvement over the four detec-tions reported by Petro ff et al. (2013) from an overlapping sam-ple, which we attribute to our longer time baseline and the order-of-magnitude improvement in DM precision provided by the UWL receiver. The measured DM gradients are consistent with the power-law |dDM /dt| ∝ DM 0.81 relation, which is steeper than the relation of Hobbs et al. (2004), albeit within the error bars of their inferred slope. We attribute this to the fact that only 21 out of their 374 pulsars show a significant DM slope above 3 σ

(Donner et al. 2020). Moreover, the DM fluctuations have a de-pendence on the pulsar’s velocity, and the resulting spread in velocities contributes to a corresponding spread in the measured DM slopes. This makes it di ffi cult to clearly distinguish between the di ff erent scaling relations. The majority of our pulsars with detected DM variations are consistent with a Kolmogorov wavenumber spectrum ( β = 11 /3) within 5 σ: out of 38 pulsars in the UWL dataset with a con-strained β in the Bayesian fit, 31 are consistent with a Kol-mogorov spectral index. For the combined dataset, this is 5 out of 6 pulsars. However, due to the uncertainties on β, a substan-tial fraction of these are also consistent with β = 4, the value expected when the DM SF is dominated by a linear DM gradi-ent: this is the case for 28 of the 38 UWL pulsars and 5 of the 6 combined dataset pulsars. We note that we did not model the contribution to the SF that arises from the linear gradient itself, which introduces a bias in β inferred from the SF slope. Never-theless, the larger fraction of sources consistent with β = 11 /3than with β = 4 provides tentative evidence that the observed DM variations are predominantly consistent with Kolmogorov turbulence. We highlight several pulsars with notable DM vari-ations that show the influence of local environments on the ob-served turbulence: PSR J0835–4510 and PSR J0908–4913, both associated with SNRs and PWNe, show DM SFs that are consistent with Kol-mogorov turbulence within 3 σ. However, they have spectral co-effi cients of C2 

> n

≃ 10 0.4 m−20 /3 and C2 

> n

≃ 10 −2.4 m−20 /3, respec-tively, which are significantly higher than expected from the dif-fuse IISM. This demonstrates that turbulent energy injected by the SNR cascades to large scales in a manner that preserves the Kolmogorov spectral shape, with the SNR environment increas-ing only the amplitude of the turbulent fluctuations. Several other LoSs exhibit DM variations that cannot be rec-onciled with a broad picture of Kolmogorov turbulence in the IISM. PSR J1413–6141 shows an exceptionally high DM gra-dient, which implies an unphysically high electron density if at-tributed to the transverse motion of the pulsar alone. PSR J1731– 4744 and PSR J1833–0827 display a sign reversal in the slope, non-Kolmogorov SFs, and quasi-periodic behavior in the DM variations. PSR J1512–5759 shows that DM and scattering delays can be strongly correlated, which either indicates that the varia-tions originate from the same turbulent structures in the LoS or that our DM measurements are biased because of the leakage of the scattering delays into our estimates. On the other hand, PSR J1105–6107 and PSR J1829–1751 demonstrate that not all apparent DM variations are due to an electron-density gradient in the LoS and that frequency-dependent profile evolution can mimic IISM propagation e ff ects.

A concerning limitation encountered in this analysis is the contamination of wideband DM measurements by pulse jitter noise. We find this for 21 bright, long-period pulsars. The wide fractional bandwidth of the UWL, which reduces the formal DM uncertainty by an order of magnitude relative to the multibeam receiver, simultaneously increases sensitivity to intrinsic pulse-to-pulse profile variability. The jitter-induced epoch-to-epoch DM scatter exceeds the contribution expected from IISM turbu-lence, rendering the DM time series for these pulsars unreliable as a probe of the IISM. This issue will likely become even more pronounced with next-generation facilities such as the SKA. The increased sensitivity is expected to enable formal DM precisions as low as ∼ 10 −8 cm −3 pc (Tiburzi et al. 2025) and yield ex-tremely high-S /N measurements of many pulsars. In practice, this is impossible to achieve, as jitter noise, DISS, and profile evolution will remain a fundamental floor for these sources. The SKA will be a heavily oversubscribed facility, so it will be dif-ficult to obtain the required longer per-pulsar integration times for a large sample of pulsars such as ours. This will be espe-cially problematic for IISM studies because the most informative targets are young pulsars, which are located in highly turbulent IISM environments. At the same time, their periods are often longer than those of MSPs and, therefore, are more susceptible to jitter on short integration times. Without a robust strategy to mitigate jitter, the next generation of radio telescopes may o ff er limited advantages over current instruments such as the Mur-riyang /Parkes radio telescope for IISM studies of these sources. A dedicated strategy to separate jitter-dominated DM measure-ments from genuine IISM variability will be essential to fully exploit the sensitivity of next-generation instruments. Taken together, our results provide tentative evidence for the long-standing picture of a universal Kolmogorov power-law wavenumber spectrum for electron density fluctuations in the IISM (cf. Armstrong et al. 1995), although the uncertainties on 

β and our neglect of the linear-gradient contribution to the SF prevent us from coming to a definitive conclusion. Young pul-sars o ff er a uniquely sensitive probe of this spectrum through their proximity to active SNRs and star-forming regions, which increase the turbulence amplitude while preserving the Kol-mogorov spectral shape. The systematic deviations seen in a mi-nority of LoSs suggest structured local environments that require multiwavelength follow-up to disentangle.

Appendix B: Extraction of the DM slope of pulsars with DM misestimation from jitter 

For 21 slow and bright pulsars in our UWL sample, the measured DM time series exhibit significant variability that is not captured by the formal measurement uncertainties. This is primarily because of intrinsic pulse-to-pulse jitter. In these cases, the reported 1 σ

error bars by tempo 2 are underestimating the DM uncertainties, which can bias our linear fit. To mitigate this and still get a first-order estimate of the DM variations, we include an Extra QUADrature (EQUAD) additional white-noise term, which adds in quadrature to the DM uncertainties. The e ff ective DM uncertainties for each epoch are therefore
