(Dated: October 7, 2026) Future gravitational-wave observatories such as the Einstein Telescope will detect tens of thousands of binary neutron star mergers per year, making gravitational waves a dominant probe of the neutron-star equation of state in the coming decades. However, extracting this information requires hierarchical inference across a rapidly growing catalog of events, and existing methods must restart from scratch whenever a new event arrives, making them impractical at this scale. In this Letter, we introduce a hybrid sequential Monte Carlo algorithm that combines data and likelihood tempering to adaptively update the equation-of-state posterior in batches, reusing previous inference results instead of restarting from a wide prior. Accelerated by GPU hardware, our method infers the equation of state from a simulated month of around 1500 binary neutron star mergers in a few hours on a single GPU, and we project that a full year of detections could be processed in a few days on current hardware. This establishes sequential Monte Carlo as a practical route for real-time hierarchical inference with future gravitational-wave detectors. 

Introduction — Neutron stars (NSs) are unique laboratories to study cold, strongly interacting matter [1–4], and observing their properties can constrain the equation of state (EOS) of matter at supranuclear densities [5]. Besides radio timing [6, 7] and X-ray pulse profile modeling [8–10], gravitational waves (GWs) emitted by binary neutron star (BNS) mergers reveal the properties of this matter through an imprint left by the tidal deformations of the two NSs [11–13], as demonstrated for the first time by GW170817 [14–18]. The next generation of ground-based GW observatories, the Einstein Telescope (ET) [19, 20] and Cosmic Explorer [21–23], will provide a leap forward for this science case, detecting up to tens of thousands of BNS mergers per year [24, 25], several orders of magnitude more than the currently operational Advanced LIGO [26], Advanced Virgo [27] and KAGRA [28]. This unprecedented volume of detections strains the current data analysis methods in the GW community in two ways. First, analyzing all GW signals from separate mergers incurs a significant computational cost [29, 30]. Second (and the focus of this Letter) is the challenge presented by hierarchical infer-ence [31, 32], where the information from individual sources is combined to distill insights into properties of the popula-tion. This is a crucial step to understand the mass distributions of compact objects [33], study cosmology [34], test general relativity [35], and, as we focus on in this work, determine the NS EOS [36, 37]. It is as yet unclear how hierarchical in-ference will be performed with next-generation GW data, and developing these methods early is essential to enable extensive projection studies [38–40]. To solve this data analysis problem, the community requires hierarchical inference methods that are both scalable and se-quential. Scalability addresses the cost of hierarchical infer-ence, which grows as more events are added to the catalog. Recent works have shown the potential of GPU accelerators to achieve scalable hierarchical inference for populations [41– 47], the NS EOS [48], cosmology [49, 50], or a combination of those [51–55]. Besides scalability, it is also crucial to use a sequential approach that reuses previous inference results and updates posteriors as soon as we acquire new data. However, so far, only a few works have formulated full Bayesian hierar-chical inference algorithms that can sequentially process new data in ‘real time’, without having to restart from a wide prior distribution [51, 56, 57] (although recent developments suc-cessfully achieved it for single-event GW analyses [58–60]). Moreover, none have demonstrated the applicability of such an approach to the inference of the EOS from NS mergers in the context of future detectors like ET. In this work, we provide a recipe for scalable, sequential hierarchical inference, and use it to constrain the EOS with BNS mergers observed by ET. Our approach combines a sequential Monte Carlo algorithm that adaptively processes the observations in batches, with fast inference delivered by GPU accelerators. We demonstrate that, with current hardware, we can run the hierarchical inference process on one month of ET observations of BNS mergers in a matter of hours, and we project that one year of observations would take a few days. 

Scalable, sequential hierarchical inference — After the in-dividual BNS mergers have been detected and analyzed, our hierarchical inference constrains the EOS from the posterior distributions of the component masses and tidal deformabili-ties. In this work, we do this by directly sampling the EOS parametrization from Ref. [5], combining a nucleonic meta-model and an agnostic speed-of-sound extension scheme. Fur-ther details are given in the Supplemental Material. This sampling problem is notoriously computationally ex-pensive, largely because it requires repeatedly solving the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV) equations [61, 62] to predict the NS macroscopic properties for each EOS draw. Therefore, many previous works have resorted to machine-learning surrogates to reduce inference cost (e.g., [63–68]), arXiv:2610.07975v1 [astro-ph.HE] 6 Oct 2026 2although this adds training costs and reduces flexibility in the choice of prior and EOS parametrization. Rather than approximating the solutions for the TOV equa-tions, our method uses GPU hardware acceleration [69, 70]. Beyond a GPU-native TOV solver [48, 71], two further aspects of our software exploit GPU parallelism. First, we use normal-izing flows [72, 73], a type of neural density estimator, to turn single-event posterior samples into tractable hyperlikelihoods that can be evaluated at arbitrary points in their parameter space [74, 75]. These flows enable high throughput on GPUs, especially when evaluated at single precision, even for over a thousand events. Second, we sample the posterior using se-quential Monte Carlo (SMC) [76–79], which bridges from the prior to the posterior through a sequence of intermediate den-sities. At each iteration 𝑡 , a set of particles approximating the current density is propagated to the next one by reweighting, resampling, and moving the particles with a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) kernel. Each of these operations is highly parallel and therefore benefits from GPU acceleration. One version of SMC uses likelihood tempering to construct the intermediate densities, by raising the likelihood to an in-verse temperature 𝛽 𝑡 . This artificial temperature can be set adaptively by requiring that each iteration preserves a certain effective sample size (ESS) [80], which measures the diversity in a set of weighted samples. This choice has recently gained popularity in the GW community for analyzing individual GW events [81–86], and we recently used it for hierarchical infer-ence on multi-messenger BNS signals observed by ET [55]. More importantly, SMC is naturally suited for sequential hi-erarchical inference, where data points are added ‘in real time’ and previous posteriors are reused, rather than rerunning the inference from scratch. For this, we take the intermediate den-sities in SMC to be the partial posteriors, i.e., the intermediate density at iteration 𝑡 is the posterior conditioned on the first 

𝑡 data points [78]. A transition between densities is therefore realized by adding an extra data point to the likelihood, and this version of SMC is known as data tempering [87]. Whereas likelihood tempering gradually introduces the full likelihood through an artificial temperature parameter, data tempering does so by gradually introducing individual data points. However, a naive implementation of data tempering, where a transition adds one data point at a time, has a significant drawback. At first, reweighting particles with a highly infor-mative data point can significantly reduce the ESS, leading to low diversity, poor exploration of the parameter space, and ultimately biased posteriors. In this case, it would be better to perform additional substeps within a single iteration of data tempering. However, at later stages, new observations might have little impact on the posterior when many events have al-ready been incorporated, and such an update step may waste computational resources. Taken together, we require a method to adaptively update posteriors with batches of new data points, where the batch size is determined based on the history of past events and the properties of new sources. We then use this catalog to infer the EOS by running our proposed SMC algorithm. We start from an initial EOS pos-terior that is informed solely by the mass measurement of PSR J0740+6620 [89]. The ESS threshold to trigger an up-date step is set to 50% of the total particle number, which is fixed at 2000. Figure 2 shows how the sampler processes events in batches, indicated by the color gradient. The top panel shows the sampling efficiency (ESS divided by total number of samples) after reweighting with each new event, while the bottom panel shows the cumulative wall time recorded after each update step (all analyses in this paper run on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU). Initially, we have little information on the EOS and the first few events cause the ESS to drop quickly below the threshold value, despite most of these events having a moderate SNR (our events are not ordered by SNR, but instead based on their arrival times on Earth). Moreover, different signals have sources at different masses, so that one detection in a previously unseen mass regime will be highly informative. After about 100 events, the posterior starts to stabilize, and updates occur less often. At later stages, several hundred events can be added to the queue before an update step is triggered. In total, we require less than 2 hours to process all events sequentially. In the Supplemental Material, we show that the same set of sources without noise in the detector leads to fewer update steps, showing that data quality can impact the runtime of our algorithm. Figure 3 shows the constraints on the EOS (top panel, with density in units of nuclear saturation density 𝑛 sat ≡ 0.16 fm −3)and the mass–tidal-deformability relationship (bottom panel) after each SMC step. The bulk of the posterior underestimates the pressure at lower densities, and overestimates it at interme-diate densities. While the injected EOS lies outside the 95% credible band, this discrepancy reduces for a catalog without detector noise, as we show in the Supplemental Material. The recovery at the highest densities is less accurate, which is ex-pected since our mock catalog is limited to NSs lighter than 2 M ⊙ . Despite these features, the fiducial tidal deformability curve is within the 95% credible band for all batches, since the tidal deformability is an integral quantity over the EOS and the observable that actually enters the likelihood. In the final batch, we obtain constraints on the order of 1% on the tidal deformability across the masses probed by our catalog. While the median slightly overestimates the tidal deformabil-ity at intermediate masses, this is again largely resolved when considering injections without detector noise. These observations reinforce that the EOS has degeneracies which are hard to resolve from mass–tidal-deformability mea-surements alone [48, 105–108], and independent constraints are needed to break them. This problem is worsened because our injection EOS has a feature resembling a phase transition around 2 .5 𝑛 sat [109, 110]. 

Extrapolating to one year —To assess how well our algo-rithm scales with the number of events, we fit the runtime, recorded at each update step, as a function of the number of events, and extrapolate the fit. We find that the cost for each individual step is essentially flat up to 𝑛 ∼ 400 events, after which our calculations become memory-bound and the wall time increases roughly linearly with the number of events. Therefore, we derive a power-law fit by only using data points with 𝑛 > 400. We use this fit to predict the wall time required to ana-lyze one month of more realistic BNS detections made with ET, in particular, starting the analysis from 5 Hz and using the IMRPhenomXAS_NRTidalv3 waveform [111, 112]. With these settings, we find that one month yields 2 559 sources, and hence expect around 30 000 detections after one year of observation. These numbers should be taken as rough esti-mates, as they depend on, e.g., detector performance [113], the actual mass distributions of NSs [114], and uncertainties in the merger rate [33]. The fit predicts that our hierarchical inference algorithm will take 2 .4 hours (2 .2 days) to process one month (one year) of such more realistic observations on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU.

Specifically, we use normalizing flows to stack single-event posteriors, accelerate likelihoods on GPUs, and parallelize sampling with SMC samplers. We also showed that SMC is a natural framework to sequentially posteriors ‘in real time’, by reusing past inference results and avoid-ing restarting analyses from scratch. Our method determines adaptively when a batch of new events is sufficiently distinct from the current posterior and an update is warranted. This allows us to rapidly analyze a growing catalog of events during observing runs as expected with ET, where tens to hundreds of detections will be made every single day. We demonstrated the performance of this algorithm by in-ferring the EOS from one month of mock BNS events within a few hours on a single GPU. Furthermore, we project that our SMC sampler can infer the EOS from a realistic full year of BNS mergers within a few days. The method presented here can be directly extended to perform joint inference on the EOS, the NS mass distribution, and cosmology, following our recent work [55], although this would require careful application of selection effects [32, 115, 116]. Moreover, we believe our workflow to apply to other hierarchical inference problems as well, such as population studies of black holes, where sequen-tial inference is already becoming crucial in current observing runs [56]. In the future, we will apply our algorithm to more realis-tic mock datasets, considering the full duration of the signal and using more recent waveform models. Moreover, vari-ous effects can impact the EOS inference, such as waveform systematics [59, 117–121], unmodeled physics in the wave-form [122–124], overlapping signals [125–127], or potential noise artifacts [128, 129]. With our scalable framework, we can investigate the robustness of EOS inference under such influences using large catalogs. Future work could also con-strain the EOS from neutron star-black hole mergers detected by future GW observatories [130, 131]. Our algorithm could be optimized further in the following way. We can reduce the cost of the likelihood evaluation by approximating intermediate posteriors from already assimi-lated events with a normalizing flow, following Refs. [56, 85]. This flow would act as a surrogate density for previous partial posteriors, replacing the hyperlikelihood evaluation of already assimilated events, which otherwise dominates its cost. More-over, this would naturally allow us to perform model tempering ,where these posterior distributions can be reused in the analy-sis of an extended model, for instance, when adding parameters to account for NS anisotropy [132] or beyond-GR theories of gravity [133]. This idea has already proven to be successful in the GW community for single-event analysis [85].

> MOCK EINSTEIN TELESCOPE DATASET Determining the fiducial EOS

To compute the tidal deformabilities of the injections, we determine a fiducial EOS using the following NS observations. First, we use the mass measurement of PSR J0740+6620 of (2.08 ± 0.07 ) M⊙ [89]. Second, we use the NICER mass-radius measurements of PSR J0030+0451 [90, 91], PSR J0740+6620 [92, 93], PSR J0437–4715 [94], and PSR J0614–3329 [95]. The latest data releases of PSR J0437–4715 [159], PSR J0030+0451 [160], PSR J0614–3329 [161], PSR J1614–2230 [162], and PSR J2124–3358 [163] were not available at the start of this project and are not used. Finally, we use the posterior samples of GW170817 analyzed with the IMRPhenomXP_NRTidalv3 waveform model available from Ref. [164]. The data are analyzed with jester, using the SMC sampler with likelihood tempering. We take the posterior sample with the maximum-likelihood value as our EOS for simulating the injections. Figure 5 shows the mass-radius relation and sound-speed profile of this EOS, together with the NS data in the mass-radius plane. To be consistent with lower radii at intermediate masses as well as to support NSs with masses above 2 M ⊙ , the maximum-likelihood EOS remains at a low 𝑐 2

We perform parameter estimation on the signals using nested sampling [97, 98], in particular with the dynesty sampler [99, 100] with 2000 live points and otherwise identical settings to its implementation in bilby [101, 102]. To further reduce computational costs, we use the multibanding likelihood [165, 166] and only sample the subspace of intrinsic parameters that is relevant for our EOS inference, namely the detector-frame chirp mass M𝑐 , mass ratio 𝑞 , and component tidal deformabilities Λ1,2, with uniform priors. 𝑑 𝐿 can impact the EOS inference, as it removes the uncertainty in the redshift, which is needed to transform detector-frame masses to source-frame masses. In the current setup, the redshift is fixed by using the Planck2018 cosmology to convert distance into redshift. On the other hand, our mass posteriors are broader since we analyze signals from 20 Hz instead of 5 Hz. With this setup, we achieve a median sampling runtime of around 10 minutes per signal on 128 CPUs, making the analysis of many signals manageable. We analyze the signals both in stationary, Gaussian detector noise, as used for the results in the main text, and in zero noise, for which the EOS recovery is shown in Fig. 6 below. As defined in Eq. (7), the single-event posteriors enter the hierarchical likelihood through an NF that approximates the conditional posterior density 𝑝 (Λ1, Λ2 |𝑚 1, 𝑚 2, 𝑑 𝑖 ). This flow is trained at the end of each bilby run using flowjax [137]. We use the masked autoregressive flow architecture with rational quadratic spline transforms [167, 168]. The training (performed on CPUs) finishes within 2 minutes and therefore does not present a bottleneck in the analysis. With a median runtime of about 12 minutes for each of the 1555 bilby runs (including flow training), the wall time is currently dominated by the analysis of individual events, not by the hierarchical EOS inference, especially considering our simplified settings for the bilby runs. In the future, we can therefore explore the use of recent advances in parameter estimation of long-duration signals [169–176] to sample more parameters, extend the analysis to lower frequencies, make the mock dataset more realistic, and include effects from Earth rotation [177, 178] and overlapping signals [125–127].
