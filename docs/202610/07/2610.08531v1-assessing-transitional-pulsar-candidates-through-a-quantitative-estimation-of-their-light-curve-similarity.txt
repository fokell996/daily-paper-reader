ABSTRACT 

Transitional millisecond pulsars are often identified through X-ray light-curve morphology, especially by visual comparison with the prototype PSR J1023+0038. Such classifications remain qualitative and lack a calibrated notion of how much variability is already present within the prototype itself. We introduce a Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) framework that compares light curves through moving-window similarity distributions and calibrates candidate-prototype distances against the internal heterogeneity of PSR J1023+0038. In this formulation, a source is not simply asked to resemble one prototype observation, but to remain compatible with the range of morphologies exhibited by the prototype across observations. We apply the method to confirmed and candidate tMSPs and to a control sample of non-tMSP systems. The resulting similarity measure provides a reproducible ranking for tMSP-like behaviour and is therefore useful for candidate triage and archival searches. At the same time, the control sample shows that short-timescale accretion variability can partly mimic tMSP-like morphology, demonstrating that light-curve shape alone is a screening diagnostic rather than a definitive class identifier. 

Key words: X-rays: binaries, Stars: neutron, (Stars:) pulsars: general, Methods: data analysis 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Transitional millisecond pulsars (tMSPs) are compact neutron-star binaries that alternate between an accretion-powered state and a rotation-powered radio-pulsar state, thereby providing a direct ob-servational link between low-mass X-ray binaries and recycled pul-sars (see, e.g., Archibald et al. 2009; Papitto et al. 2013; Papitto & de Martino 2022). In the sub-luminous accreting regime, their X-ray emission exhibits a distinctive combination of (i) pronounced stochastic variability on seconds-to-minutes timescales and (ii) re-current switching between quasi-stable luminosity modes, commonly labelled high, low, and flaring modes (see, e.g., Linares 2014; Bog-danov et al. 2015a). Prototypical systems such as PSR J1023+0038 have established this mode-switching phenomenology as the practi-cal signature for identifying possible members of the class. Candidate selection, however, still rests on qualitative pattern recog-nition: light curves are commonly deemed “tMSP-like” by visual comparison with prototypical sources. This approach is effective for choosing targets for additional observations, but it is intrinsically subjective and does not provide a quantitative measure of resem-blance, nor an explicit calibration against the internal heterogene-

ity of the prototype itself. In addition, differences in observation length, cadence, and stochastic variability can bias purely qualita-tive judgments. The issue is therefore not only one of formalizing visual resemblance, but of enabling reproducible candidate ranking across heterogeneous observations, so that morphology can be used as an operational first-pass criterion in source selection and archive searches. Here we address these limitations by introducing a quantitative, morphology-based comparison of X-ray light curves using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW, see, e.g., Berndt & Clifford 1994). The DTW approach is broadly utilized across various fields, from speech recog-nition to analyze market trends. DTW has also been recently used in astrophysics. Examples include the similarity between light curves of gamma-ray bursts (Zhang et al. 2016), the reconstruction of gravi-tational wave signals from core-collapse supernovae (Suvorova et al. 2019), and the assessment of solar wind time series (Samara et al. 2022). Vohl et al. (2024) have employed an unsupervised method involving DTW to categorize radio pulsars by profile shape simi-larity using graph theory. In García & Torres (2025), we use DTW to analyze the population of gamma-ray-emitting pulsars detected by Fermi-LAT 3PC, quantitatively showing that pulsars with similar light curves can have different physical and spectral properties.

2 SOURCE SELECTION AND THEIR X-RAY LIGHT CURVES 

The light curves analyzed here include confirmed and candidate tM-SPs: PSR J1023+0038 (also referred to as the prototypical source or J1023), XSS J12270-4859, IGR J18245-2452, and the candi-dates 1RXS J154439.4-112820, CXOU J110926.4-650224. To assess the methodology’s discriminative power, we also include a control sample of systems (formed by accreting millisecond pulsars (AM-SPs) and cataclysmic variables (CVs)) consisting of: PBC J0801.2-4625, IGR J12123-5802, SWIFT J0746-1611, IGR J17511-3057, and SAX J1808.4-3658. Each system is briefly introduced in Ap-pendix A together with details regarding the observations used to characterize them. The preparation of the X-ray light curves for the application of the methodology is described in Appendix B. The two more relevant steps are the identification of temporal discontinuities and the re-moval of flares. We extract light curve segments with a minimum temporal length of 1h (3600 s, or 120 bins of 30 s, in which all light curves are shown). The size of the temporal bin and the minimum duration to be considered a light curve segment can be chosen ac-cording to data. The aim of these choices is to avoid comparisons with too short light curves which may be inappropriate to define the systems’ mode; and to define a duration of a bin that can be equally used in all systems to be compared. Since the aim of the present method is to compare the recurrent mode-switching morphology of the sub-luminous disc state, we exclude flaring intervals before applying DTW. Otherwise their sporadic oc-currence, broad amplitude range, and source-to-source variability would dominate any distance. When existing, flares of confirmed and candidate tMSPs are identified and removed using the count-rate distribution. A threshold is set at three standard deviations above the peak of the highest-count distribution. This threshold is estimated as-suming an approximate Gaussian distribution of the high mode. Bins with counts exceeding this threshold are classified as flaring modes and are excluded. After flare removal, we require that the remain-ing light curve segments meet the minimum duration requirement of 3600 s. Each resulting light curve is normalized using the min-max scaler (see Appendix B, Eq. B1). This ensures that each light curve is eval-uated mainly based on its morphology, regardless of flux variations, and that its values are adjusted between 0 and 1. Note that to prevent distorting the light curve shapes, the min-max scaler is applied col-lectively to all those segments originating from the same observation. Table B1 shows the number of observational campaigns available for each system, along with the resulting number of light curve segments obtained. Appendix B shows all the light curves used in this study.

4 RESULTS 

The moving-window process was carried out with 𝑤 𝑠 = 40 bins and 𝑠 𝑠 = 1 bin, ensuring maximum overlap between consecutive segments, and the largest possible number of windows. The DTW alignment was computed using a Sakoe-Chiba band with 𝑤 𝑤 = 4bins. Throughout the analysis, the hyperparameters introduced in § 3.1 and § 3.2 were held constant, see Appendix E. Figure 1 shows the results obtained. Among the lowest values of 𝜎 eq , and in particular, in the second low-est, we find XSS J12270-4859, CXOU J110926.4-650224, 1RXS J154439.4-112820; the former is a confirmed tMSP, while the latter two are well-established candidates. A known tMSP, IGR J18245-2452 appears with a high score. This is the result of it having a less structured light curve (showing a bin-by-bin step-like appearance), which, even qualitatively, does not resemble those of the prototype. Because the source is in a low-count regime, each 30 s time bin con-tains only a small integer number of detected photons. As a result, the background-subtracted count rate takes on discrete values rather than varying smoothly, producing a step-like appearance. Consequently, applying DTW to this Chandra light curve is less straightforward than to the smoother XMM-Newton data, and the results should be interpreted with caution. We retain the comparison with IGR J18245-2452 here because it is a bona fide tMSP, while noticing that, a priori, we do not expect a close behavioral matching. See Appendix A for references and details of the light curves used. One source, IGR J12123-5802, a nova-like CV appears with a low 

𝜎 eq , providing an interesting handle regarding what the methodology is sensitive to (see Appendix B and especially Fig. B6 for the system’s light curve). Generic accretion-driven flickering can mimic switching on short timescales. In CVs, variable absorption from structures in the disk, stream, or a disc rim can produce repeated dips or intervals of lower count rate. When binned at 30 s and viewed in segments, this can resemble alternating high/low intervals. Also, as we keep for each window only the best DTW match in the other light curve, a source can score well if it contains a non-trivial subset of windows that resemble some J1023 windows, even if the global behaviour is different. Thus, there is no contradiction in a nova-like CV achieving moderate similarity to a tMSP prototype under a morphology-only metric with best-window pooling. Rather it is a warning that the method can also capture generic accretion variability patterns that are not unique to tMSPs. In fact, two of the prototypical tMSPs, PSR J1023+0038 and XSS J12270–4859, were initially classified as CVs (Bond et al. 2002; de Martino et al. 2010). 

5 CONCLUDING REMARKS 

We introduce a quantitative, morphology-based framework to com-pare X-ray light curves of transitional millisecond pulsar candidates against a prototype source, using DTW and ECDFs to summarize the resulting similarity distributions. The aim is to replace purely visual classification with a reproducible and calibrated comparison scheme. Our conclusions are: 

• The intrinsic heterogeneity of the prototype, PSR J1023+0038, can be characterized through the ensemble of prototype-prototype ECDFs, whose median 𝐺 (𝑠 ) provides a natural central reference and whose spread defines the expected level of variability compatible with the class. 

• The methodology succeeds in separating, at the ensemble level, confirmed or candidate tMSPs from the control sample in a statisti-cally interpretable way, even though the classification is based only on light-curve morphology and does not use source labels or physical priors. 

• The only clear exception is that of a known tMSP, IGR J18245-2452, which appears with a high score. This was expected, as the light curve used shows a bin-by-bin step-like appearance. 

• Restricting the prototype band to the ECDFs closest to the me-dian makes the compatibility criterion more stringent, but does not substantially shift the median reference curve itself. The resulting fil-tered ensemble therefore characterizes the representative, internally consistent behaviour of the prototype against which the compatibility of other systems is assessed. Thus, the main effect of narrowing the heterogeneity band is a stricter statistical calibration, not a qualitative redefinition of the typical prototype behaviour. 

• The method is sensitive to morphology, not directly to source class. Accordingly, a non-tMSP system can achieve moderate similar-ity to the prototype if it contains a non-negligible subset of light curve segments that resemble some of the J1023 windows, even when its overall behaviour is different. In particular, different accretion-driven states in systems that are not tMSP candidates can individually pro-duce ECDFs that lie close to the prototype median. This does not imply that such systems are physically akin to tMSPs as a whole; rather, it shows that some short-timescale accretion patterns are not unique to transitional pulsars and can mimic isolated pieces of their phenomenology under a morphology-only metric. 

• For that reason, the most meaningful discriminator is not the existence of a single ECDF close to 𝐺 (𝑠 ), but the aggregate be-haviour of the full comparison ensemble associated with a source, as represented by 𝐹 𝑍 or, similarly, by the median of that ensemble. 

• As a particular case, IGR J12123-5802 is informative rather than contradictory: it highlights that the method can capture generic accretion variability patterns that overlap morphologically with parts of the tMSP phenomenology, and thus clarifies both the scope and the present limitation of the approach. Overall, the analysis supports the use of DTW-based ECDF compar-isons as a useful quantitative screening tool for tMSP-like behaviour, while also showing that morphology alone is not fully specific. The method is informative even in cases when it does not uniquely iden-tify class membership: it quantifies how far morphology alone can take the inference, and therefore where additional diagnostics become necessary. The method is therefore best interpreted as a first-stage similarity filter, certainly beyond a visual comparison. A practical use of the method is candidate triage: when several sources show qualitatively suggestive variability, the DTW-based score provides a reproducible way to prioritize those most compatible with established tMSP phe-nomenology for deeper observational campaigns. Because the pro-cedure is automatic once light curves are prepared, it can be applied systematically to archival datasets to search for previously unrecog-nized tMSP-like variability patterns. While here used for assessing individual candidates to tMSPs, it does not preclude additional phys-ical or observational diagnostics. It may play a role in automatic catalog searches for candidates presenting a particular light curve behavior, beyond that of tMSPs.

APPENDIX A: SOURCE SAMPLE & OBSERVATIONAL DETAILS 

This appendix summarizes the nature of the sources included in our comparison sample and the observational material used for each of them. The emphasis here is put on the literature most relevant to the accreting or sub-luminous states from which the light curves analyzed in this work were extracted. 

• PSR J1023+0038 (J1023). J1023 is the prototype and best-studied confirmed tMSP. It was first recognized as an unusual CV candidate, then identified as a radio MSP with evidence of a pre-vious accretion disc, and it re-entered an accretion-dominated sub-luminous state in 2013, remaining the benchmark source for tMSP mode-switching phenomenology (Bond et al. 2002; Archibald et al. 2009; Stappers et al. 2014; Patruno et al. 2014; Bogdanov et al. 2015b; Archibald et al. 2015). In this work we use 17 XMM-Newton/EPIC observations obtained during this current active state, since they define the prototype ensemble against which the rest of the sam-ple is compared. These observations encompass the canonical rapid switches among high, low, and flaring modes. Later campaigns show that the same phenomenology persists across the subsequent moni-toring of the source (Campana et al. 2016; Coti Zelati et al. 2018; Papitto et al. 2019; Miraval Zanon et al. 2022; Baglio et al. 2023). 

• XSS J12270-4859 / PSR J1227-4853 (J12270). XSS J12270-4859 is a confirmed tMSP that spent years in a sub-luminous disc state before transitioning in late 2012 to a radio-pulsar state (de Martino et al. 2010, 2013; Bassa et al. 2014; Roy et al. 2015). The source is especially relevant here because its pre-transition X-ray be-haviour closely matches the tMSP phenomenology defined by J1023, and coherent X-ray pulsations were later detected in the 2011 sub-luminous-state data (Papitto et al. 2015). We consider two observa-tions overall; the two key disc-state XMM-Newton observations are those performed in 2009 and 2011. For these, we use the summed EPIC- MOS1+MOS2 light curves. 

• IGR J18245-2452 / PSR J1824-2452I (J1824, M28I). IGR J18245-2452 is the first system directly observed to swing between accretion-powered and rotation-powered states, providing the clear-est proof of the recycling link between LMXBs and radio MSPs (Papitto et al. 2013; Linares et al. 2014; Ferrigno et al. 2014). Un-like J1023 and J12270, its transitional behaviour was caught during a bright X-ray outburst in the globular cluster M28 in 2013, in-cluding extreme “hiccup” accretion and order-of-magnitude intensity changes on short time-scales (Ferrigno et al. 2014). In our sample, we consider a Chandra light curve in which the source is observed in a subluminous state. 

• 1RXS J154439.4-112820 / 3FGL J1544.6-1125 (J1544). 

J1544 is one of the strongest tMSP candidates identified among previ-ously unassociated Fermi-LAT sources (Bogdanov & Halpern 2015; Bogdanov 2016). Its optical spectrum shows an accretion disc, while the X-ray light curve displays the same rapid high/low-mode switch-ing that characterizes confirmed tMSPs (Bogdanov & Halpern 2015; Bogdanov 2016). Recent simultaneous radio/X-ray observations fur-ther strengthen the tMSP interpretation by showing behaviour con-sistent with the radio/X-ray phenomenology already seen in J1023 (Gusinskaia et al. 2025). A recent dedicated multi-band study by Illiano et al. (2025) further probed the source’s mode switching and broad-band variability, reinforcing its standing as one of the best cur-rent tMSP candidates. In this work we consider three XMM-Newton observations. 

• CXOU J110926.4-650224 (J1109). J1109 is a strong tMSP can-didate that has remained in a prolonged sub-luminous disc state since its recognition as the X-ray counterpart of an INTEGRAL hard-X-ray source (Coti Zelati et al. 2019, 2021). Multiwavelength follow-up has shown the rapid bimodal variability, broad-band spectral prop-erties, and variable radio emission expected for the class, while the latest Fermi-LAT analysis now supports its association with a GeV counterpart (Coti Zelati et al. 2024; Manca et al. 2025). We consider two XMM-Newton observations for this source. Regarding the sources that are not tMSPs or candidates, 

• PBC J0801.2-4625. It is a magnetic CV of the Intermediate Polar type with a spin period of 22min (see Bernardini et al. 2017; Halpern et al. 2018; Irving et al. 2024). 

• IGR J12123-5802. It is supposed to be a magnetic CV, but no fast periodicity is found. It is classified as a nova-like CV (see Bernardini et al. 2013). 

• SWIFT J0746-1611. It is a magnetic CV of the IP type that, when in a lower state, did not show fast periodicity and was suggested to be possibly a LMXB. However, when found a few years later in a high state, a spin of 38min was found (see again Bernardini et al. 2017 and Bernardini et al. 2019). For this source, the light curve is in a low state, which could be helpful to compare with the tMSP light curves. 

• IGR J17511-3057. The EPIC light curve, binned at 30 s, from the XMM-Newton observation of the AMSP was obtained during the late stages of its recent outburst (Illiano et al. 2026). At that point, the X-ray luminosity had decreased below 7e33 erg/s, consistent with levels observed in tMSPs in the sub-luminous state. Even at this level, we would expect to be in the propeller regime, but we detected coherent X-ray pulsations. This behavior was also observed in another AMSP using NICER (Bult et al. 2019). 

• SAX J1808.4-3658. Light curve of the AMSP during the final stage of its 2022 outburst, observed with EPIC pn and binned at 30 s (see Ballocco et al. 2026). All XMM-Newton observations listed in Table A1 were processed following standard procedures with the XMM-Newton Science Anal-ysis System (SAS; Gabriel et al. 2004). After screening out intervals affected by enhanced particle background, source products were ex-tracted from circular regions centered on the target for imaging-mode observations, with background regions selected away from the source but on the same CCD. For timing-mode observations, source and background events were instead extracted from strip-shaped re-gions centered on, and offset from, the source columns, respectively. Background-subtracted light curves in the 0.3–10 keV band were then produced with epiclccorr , which applies the standard expo-sure and instrumental corrections. Whenever more than one EPIC detector contributed simultaneous good-time intervals, the corre-sponding light curves were combined over the strictly overlapping intervals. The final light curves were binned at 30 s, which provides a practical compromise between signal-to-noise in individual bins and sensitivity to short low-mode intervals. The Chandra data for J1824 were reprocessed with chandra_repro 

in CIAO 4.18 using CALDB 4.12.3 (Fruscione et al. 2006). We extracted source events from a circular aperture of radius 1 .5′′ cen-tered on the source position. The background was estimated from an annulus with inner and outer radii of 6 ′′ and 16 ′′ , after masking neighboring point sources detected in the same field. Background-subtracted light curves were then produced in the 0.5–8.0 keV band with dmextract , which applies the standard exposure correction on a bin-by-bin basis, using 30 s time bins.

APPENDIX B: PREPROCESSING LIGHT CURVES 

The preparation of the light curves for application of the developed methodology consists of three main steps: treatment of negative count rate values, identification of temporal discontinuities, and removal of flaring modes. Negative count rate values are set to zero, under the assumption that they arise from statistical fluctuations around a null signal and do not represent physical emission. Temporal discontinu-ities are identified as gaps in which no count value is recorded. Since the light curves are binned consecutively in time with a resolution of 30 s, any time difference between two adjacent bins exceeding 30 s (corresponding to 1 bin) is interpreted as a temporal jump. In such cases, the light curve is split into independent segments (resulting in different light curves). Only segments with a minimum temporal length of 3600 s (1 hr) are retained for further analysis. The removal of flaring modes is carried out using the count-rate distribution, as explained in the main text. Each resulting light curve is normalized using the min-max scaler:

APPENDIX E: MODEL HYPERPARAMETERS 

This methodology involves three user-defined hyperparameters, 𝑤 𝑠 ,

𝑠 𝑠 , and 𝑤 𝑤 , which act at different levels of the analysis. The window size 𝑤 𝑠 defines the characteristic timescale over which the light curves are compared and constitutes the method’s primary scale. Its choice is constrained by the total length of the light curves, as it determines both the temporal resolution and the number of seg-ments (windows) that can be extracted according to Eq. (3), together with 𝑠 𝑠 . Increasing 𝑤 𝑠 reduces the number of available windows while emphasizing larger-scale structures. Given the minimum light curve duration of 3600 s (see § 2), we set 

𝑤 𝑠 = 40 bins, equivalent to 1200 s, corresponding to one third of the minimum allowed light curve length. This choice balances the need to capture global features while retaining a sufficient number of windows for statistical characterization. The step size 𝑠 𝑠 determines how these windows are sampled. In this work, we adopt 𝑠 𝑠 = 1. This choice includes all possible window positions and maximizes the sampling of the light curve. This leads to strongly overlapping windows. In contrast, setting 𝑠 𝑠 = 𝑤 𝑠 suppresses the moving window process. Once the windows are defined, the parameter 𝑤 𝑤 regulates the DTW alignment within each window, as introduced in § 3.1. The Sakoe-Chiba constraint follows the implementation in the 

dtaidistance Python package (Meert et al. 2020) and limits the number of bins in one light curve that can be matched to a single bin in another, thereby controlling the degree of temporal compression. This Sakoe-Chiba band-type constraint requires that the indices in the two time series satisfy |𝑖 − 𝑗 | < 2𝑤 𝑤 + | 𝑛 − 𝑚 |, thereby restricting the admissible region of 𝑊 ∈ A . For time series of equal length ( 𝑛 = 𝑚 ), this reduces to |𝑖 − 𝑗 | < 2𝑤 𝑤 . This implicitly limits the number of times a single bin in one light curve can be connected to different bins

in the other light curve (also defined as multiplicity). Indeed, still for the case of 𝑛 = 𝑚 , for a fixed index 𝑗 , the constraint restricts the admissible indices 𝑖 to the interval [ 𝑗 − 2𝑤 𝑤 + 1, 𝑗 + 2𝑤 𝑤 − 1], which contains at most 4 𝑤 𝑤 − 1 values. Since each pair (𝑖, 𝑗 ) can appear at most once in the warping path due to the monotonicity condition, and the band constraint restricts the admissible indices, a given bin can be aligned no more than 4 𝑤 𝑤 −1 times. We explored 𝑤 𝑤 = 1, 2, 4, 8, 20, as well as the unconstrained case, in order to assess the impact of alignment flexibility on the resulting similarity distributions. Note that 𝑤 𝑤 = 1 is equivalent to the Euclidean distance case, where the alignment between light curves is strictly one-to-one (no warping). As 𝑤 𝑤 increases, the alignment becomes more flexible. For interme-diate values (e.g., 𝑤 𝑤 = 4, 8), the results remain stable, improving the separation in 𝜎 eq between the candidates and control cases (see Fig. 1) while allowing moderate temporal distortions. For larger values, such as 𝑤 𝑤 = 20 or in the unconstrained case, the alignment becomes excessively flexible. Although the resulting 𝜎 eq 

values remain consistent, the multiplicities become very large, im-plying that single bins may be matched to many bins simultaneously. In this regime, the DTW distance is artificially reduced by highly degenerate alignments, compromising the physical interpretability of the comparison. From a physical perspective, large multiplicities are not meaning-ful, as they imply that a single temporal feature may correspond to multiple independent features in the other light curve. Also, very small multiplicities (towards the Euclidean distance) would imply that large similarities would require strict correlation, which is phys-ically implausible across different systems. Therefore, the choice of 𝑤 𝑤 must balance alignment flexibility with a physically plausible mapping between time bins. Given this trade-off, we adopt 𝑤 𝑤 = 4. This choice preserves the separation between can-didate and control cases, as shown in Fig. 1, while keeping multiplic-ities moderate, with a maximum value of 7 seen across all analyzed window alignments, thereby maintaining physical interpretability.
