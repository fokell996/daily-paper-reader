# Adaptive proposals for nonparametric equation-of-state inference with RIFT: iterative Gaussian-process refinement in a compressed sequence representation 

R. O’Shaughnessy 1 and Z. Zhu 1

> 1

Rochester Institute of Technology 

Nonparametric equation-of-state (EOS) inference evaluates the likelihood on a finite ensemble drawn from a prescribed functional prior. Reweighting that ensemble suffices for the binary neutron stars detected so far, and loses effective sample size only once the likelihood concentrates — which needs third-generation catalogues, or extra electromagnetic and nuclear-theory information folded into the same target. We develop and validate an adaptive-proposal procedure that leaves the base prior fixed: data-informed generations are combined by multiple importance sampling and, where that degenerates at large catalogue size, by a calibrated Gaussian projection. We implement it in two representations — a compressed principal-component parametrization of the neutron-star sequence, whose draws need no stellar-structure solve, and a sound-speed parametrization with a differentiable Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff solver — both on a constrained low-density-to-pQCD bridge. Because that anchor, and not the bridge, fixes the low-density prior, we rebuild the ensemble with a crust-plus-chiral-EFT anchor in place of the wide nuclear metamodel: the maximum-mass prior is unmoved, the radius gains a hard prior ceiling at the measured NICER value. GW170817 with the specified NICER and radio factors gives the calibration-conditional 

R(1 .4 M⊙) = 11 .7 ± 0.4 km; in a synthetic 1000-event catalogue it reproduces the mean fixed-prior Λ(1 .4 M⊙) to 0 .4%, 1 .5 times wider, as the fixed archive’s effective sample size falls from ∼ 470 to 22. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

The equation of state (EOS) of cold matter above nu-clear saturation density is a central open problem con-necting nuclear physics, gravitational-wave (GW) astron-omy, and X-ray timing. The binary-neutron-star inspi-rals GW170817 [1–3] and GW190425 [4, 5] provide GW information about the tidal deformability Λ. NICER X-ray pulse-profile modeling constrains the mass–radius re-lation for individual pulsars [6–10], while radio timing of massive pulsars [11, 12] provides lower bounds on the maximum mass. Early second-generation forecasts es-tablished how stacking binary-neutron-star observations can reconstruct the EOS [13]. More complete catalog treatments show, however, that the loudest events do not dominate EOS inference: numerous weaker detec-tions contribute critically, and the mass distribution con-trols which density ranges are resolved [14, 15]. Third-generation gravitational-wave observatories — Cosmic Explorer [16] and the Einstein Telescope [17, 18] — are expected to detect binary neutron stars in the hundreds of thousands. Forecasts using a range of Bayesian sam-plers and Fisher-matrix approximations project corre-spondingly sharp constraints on the equation of state [15, 19–21]. As they probe complementary density ranges and have distinct systematics, these high-precision astronom-ical probes will complement laboratory and theoretical programs on the same matter: parity-violating electron scattering measurements of the neutron skin [22, 23] and their implications for the symmetry energy [24], heavy-ion collisions and rare-isotope measurements [25, 26], and ab initio chiral-effective-field-theory calculations of neutron-rich matter [27, 28]. That said, combining these extremely heterogeneous constraints requires both a physical EOS model and a statistical representation of uncertainty over EOS functions. Joint GW–NICER EOS analyses have already established the importance of a common EOS, explicit prior choices, and consis-tent source-level likelihoods [29–33]. As these constraints tighten, the range of viable solutions narrows, posing a practical computational challenge: exploring the space of valid nuclear models efficiently enough that the surviving region is resolved rather than merely bracketed. Contemporary astronomical constraints are sufficiently uninformative that uniform prior draws have been a highly effective and widely adopted sampling strategy. However, a third-generation network is expected to de-tect binary neutron stars at rates orders of magnitude above the current one [19–21], and because the EOS like-lihood is a product over events, the variance of the log weight grows with catalogue size — so the effective sam-ple size of a fixed ensemble decays while the ensemble itself does not grow at all. Even with contemporary con-straints, drawing from a fixed prior becomes increasingly inefficient as NICER measurements and nuclear-theory information concentrate the viable EOS region. Two broad modeling strategies are widely used. Para-metric families, including piecewise polytropes [34] and spectral representations [35], are low-dimensional and tractable, but their restricted functional forms can produce representation-dependent inference systemat-ics [36–39]. Nonparametric constructions instead place a Gaussian-process (GP) prior over an auxiliary func-tion, commonly related to the sound speed c2

Complementary learned representations include normalizing-flow EOS emulators [45], physics-informed flow priors for compact-binary parameter estimation [46], direct mass–radius pri-ors [47], and neural EOS inference and stellar-structure arXiv:2610.07424v1 [astro-ph.HE] 5 Oct 2026 2emulators [48–51], and neural posterior models [52, 53]. Microscopic nuclear calculations are also beginning to use differentiable, correlated GP models [54]; unlike the sequence-space compression developed here, these regress microscopic matter predictions. These approaches differ in their physical coordinates and inductive assumptions, but all must resolve a posterior that can occupy a small fraction of the original functional ensemble. Existing EOS analyses generally specify the functional prior before conditioning on the data, as required for a well-defined Bayesian target. Richer choices — including microscopic model mixing [55], crust-to-pQCD construc-tions [44], and semiparametric model-trained GPs [56] — alter that target model, but do not by themselves solve a separate computational problem: a finite draw set from the chosen prior may poorly resolve a concentrated pos-terior. Loss of effective sample size is therefore a failure of the sampling proposal, not a reason to replace the sci-entific prior after inspecting the data. Iterative proposal and surrogate refinement are well developed for GW pa-rameter inference, including the RIFT pipeline [57, 58]. This motivates an analogous adaptive proposal in EOS function space, with an explicit correction back to the fixed base-prior posterior. While a previous study with RIFT performed iterative refinement for the EOS within the context of a fixed set of EOS prior draws, for single-event inference [59], this proposal did not re-sample EOS realizations from a common prior. In this paper, we adapt RIFT’s inference-driven re-finement strategy to EOS inference. First, we formu-late adaptive proposal refinement using multiple impor-tance sampling: draws from successive data-informed proposals are combined with balance-heuristic weights to target the posterior defined by a fixed base prior, provided the proposals retain support over that tar-get. Second, we implement the loop in two representa-tions — a compressed principal-component parametriza-tion of the neutron-star sequence {M, R, Λ}(hc) and a conventional sound-speed parametrization with a differ-entiable Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) forward map. Third, we construct the base prior with the con-strained bridge of Ref. [60], but supply our own low-density anchor: where that work fixes a crust below 0.34 nsat and draws the anchor from a chiral-effective-field-theory ensemble, we resample a nuclear meta-model [61]. That choice is deliberate. A prior that al-ready encodes every available constraint is a poor test bed for a method whose subject is what data do to a prior: with the crust fixed, the symmetry sector pinned and the maximum mass implicitly bounded, the poste-rior sits almost on top of the prior and there is nothing to see. We instead take a low-density model broad enough to carry real crust and symmetry-sector uncertainty, and correspondingly uninformative in Mmax , so that ordinary measurements — radio masses of massive pulsars above all — visibly move it. The cost is a sub-saturation band wider than any honest crust model, and we measure that cost by rebuilding the ensemble both ways. Fourth, we validate the estimator on GW170817 and apply the fixed-data workflow to the specified NICER and radio-pulsar data. For synthetic catalogues of up to 1000 events, we calibrate the sequential Gaussian-projection approxima-tion against a large fixed-prior reference. The synthetic study separates posterior contraction caused by addi-tional data from the sampling-efficiency gain and projec-tion error introduced by proposal adaptation. This paper is organized as follows. In Section II we develop the meth-ods: neutron-star structure and the nonparametric prior (Sec. II A 1–II A 2), the EOS evidence supplied by GW and electromagnetic observations (Sec. II B), the two rep-resentations and the adaptive refinement loop (Sec. II C), and the real and synthetic data sets (Secs. II E–II F). In Section III we validate the solver, the compressed repre-sentation, and the prior construction. In Section IV we apply the loop to GW170817, NICER, and radio-pulsar data, demonstrate adaptive proposal refinement for the maximum-mass constraint, and present the large-catalog forecast and its symmetry-energy translation. Finally, in Section V we summarize and place this work in context.

> C. Representations of the equation of state

The adaptive scheme is deliberately agnostic to how the equation of state is represented. It requires only (i) a generative family of candidate EOS controlled by a finite parameter vector c with a tractable density q(c); (ii) a forward map from c to the neutron-star observables that enter the likelihood (Sec. II B); and (iii) the importance-reweight-and-refit of q(c) described in Sec. II D. Noth-ing in the loop privileges a particular function being parametrized. Two natural choices trade computational cost against directness — one in the space of stellar ob-servables , one in the space of the microphysical EOS — and our pipeline supports both behind a single accessor that returns Λ( m), R(m) and Mmax per draw; the refine-ment loop is identical for each. Before describing the two implementations, we make precise how they are related.

Physical consistency and stability. A Gaussian over sequence coefficients is not guaranteed to generate physi-cally realizable stars: the three channels are drawn jointly with only the empirical cross-covariance tying them to-gether, so a fresh draw far from the training manifold can pair an R(m) with a Λ( m) that no causal EOS pro-duces, and its M (hc) may lack the turning-point struc-ture of a genuine TOV family. Two structural safeguards limit the exposure. First, all observables enter the like-lihood only through the stable rising branch: turning points are located from the sign of dM/dh c along each reconstructed sequence, quantities are interpolated on that branch alone, and draws without a nontrivial rising branch are rejected at the sampling stage together with non-finite or negative curves. Second, the generation-0 ensemble consists of TOV-solved training sequences; the coefficient Gaussian, conditioned on the same valid-ity filter, supplies the operational base density used by the importance correction in Eq. (21). Fresh coefficient-Gaussian draws are used only as proposals, and must pass the same branch and validity filters. These are the only per-draw rejection criteria used here. No compactness–Love relation enters this acceptance rule. We retain Λ( m) as an independent channel be-cause the GW likelihood measures it directly; imposing an empirical relation to C = GM/Rc 2 would instead in-troduce an unintended prior. Appendix C 2 reports an exploratory compactness–Love stress test and explains why we do not use it for sampling. For a posterior localized around one EOS, the linear distance D⊥ of Eq. (16) provides a more direct optional consistency test. It asks whether the measured combina-tions of M , R, and Λ can be produced by a nearby TOV solution, using their actual uncertainties rather than a selected universal relation. We do not apply it to the broad prior used here, for which no single tangent space is adequate. In a localized analysis it should first be reported as a diagnostic, with the Jacobian updated as the posterior moves; promoting it to an acceptance rule would also require the truncation normalization of Ap-pendix C 1. When physicality by construction is required, the EOS-space representation below remains the appro-priate proposal family.

The adaptive loop answers two distinct questions, and we separate them because they can look superficially alike. 

First: does refining the proposal change the answer for fixed data? It must not, and the two estimators we use differ in how exactly they enforce that. Refitting the coefficient-space Gaussian to the current weighted draws and sampling a fresh EOS set changes the proposal, not the model. > D. A large-catalogue forecast, and the limits of translating to nuclear parameters

We finally push the synthetic demonstration to asecond-generation HLV catalogue of 1000 binary neutron stars (SNR ≥ 8). We adopt a deliberately lazy cosmol-ogy — luminosity distance uniform in comoving volume to 300 Mpc with no mass-redshift, and no selection be-yond the SNR cut — which is adequate for a forecast; this is well below third-generation event rates but an order of magnitude beyond the coming decade, and it shows how the method behaves as information accumulates. Two messages emerge (Figs. 11, 12). First, the EOS measurement sharpens as expected, and the fixed 972-draw archive becomes under-resolved. Af-ter 3 refinement rounds the adaptive posterior on Λ(1 .4) contracts to a ±16 ( ∼ 4%) interval consistent with the in-jected truth (400) within its uncertainty, and the Λ( M )90% band tightens onto the truth across the sampled mass range (Fig. 11a,c). The final width should be com-pared against the Fisher expectation, and the compari-son only makes sense if the forecast resolves the Λ( M )shape. Following the approach of Ref. [15], we accumu-late the per-event Fisher information in the sequence-coefficient space — derivatives ∂ ln ˜Λ/∂c about the truth, the linear limit of Sec. II C 1, accumulated over all 1000 catalogue events — and project the resulting posterior covariance onto ln Λ( M ). The accumulation costs sec-onds, so no subsampling is used; estimating the same quantity from 100-event subsamples instead scatters it by 9%, which would dominate the comparison below. This shape-resolved forecast predicts σΛ(1 .4) ≈ 11 .6, and its mass-resolved band tracks the posterior’s, widening off the sampled masses in the same way (Fig. 11c). Com-pared against the converged reference of Sec. IV — ±11 .2from 10 5 fixed-prior draws, the appropriate yardstick be-cause the archive-sized reweighting is starved — their standard deviations differ by 4%. The Fisher matrix uses every catalogue event, so this figure carries no subsam-pling noise; it remains a statement about this catalogue and this injected EOS rather than a general validation. A rigid-shape stack, treating ln Λ( M ) as shifting uniformly, fares distinctly worse: it predicts σΛ(1 .4) ≈ 8.4, low by a factor 0 .75, with no mass dependence at all. Second — and more cautionary — the precise EOS measurement translates only weakly into the nuclear symmetry energy. Symmetric matter is comparatively well known, so a neutron-star measurement is nominally a measurement of the symmetry sector; but the map from the tidal observables to the symmetry parameters is loose. Conditioning the prior’s nuclear empirical pa-rameters on the measured Λ( m) (a linear-Gaussian con-ditioning that uses all prior draws, so it does not suf-fer importance-sampling starvation), the slope improves only from σLsym = 29 to 21 MeV and the curvature barely at all ( σKsym : 114 → 114 MeV), even though Λ(1 .4) is by then measured to a few percent (Fig. 12). The joint 

Lsym –Ksym region contracts mostly along a degeneracy direction and the marginals saturate well before the EOS does. This makes quantitative the difficulty emphasised by Zhu & O’Shaughnessy [15]: inspiral tidal measure-ments, however numerous, constrain the EOS only over the narrow density range set by the observed masses and are degenerate among the nuclear parameters, so terres-trial experiments and low-mass or post-merger informa-tion remain necessary to pin the symmetry energy. The adaptive prior quantifies this limitation rather than re-moving it. We cross-check this forecast independently with the differentiable forward map of Sec. both weak and degenerate with Lsym in its effect on Λ. Propagating a 3% Λ(1 .4) measurement through this Ja-cobian (a linearized Fisher forecast) gives σLsym ≈ 20 MeV with Esym and Ksat unmoved from their priors — in close agreement with the sample-based σLsym = 21 MeV. Ksym

The agreement supports the interpretation that the loose translation is driven by the narrow observed mass range and symmetry-sector degeneracies within the tested models, rather than solely by the empirical prior covariance. It also demonstrates the end-to-end sensi-tivities available in the EOS-space representation; their numerical accuracy is assessed through the solver valida-tion rather than inferred from automatic differentiation itself. Because the limitation is one of density coverage , het-erogeneous measurements help where more of the same 17 do not. Repeating the conditioning while varying which masses are measured makes this quantitative: a single Λ(1 .4) barely moves Lsym (29 → 27 MeV), the binary-neutron-star pair near 1 .2–1 .4 M⊙ reaches 21 MeV, and adding a low-mass, crust-dominated probe near 1 .0 M⊙

— which samples a lower density — sharpens it to 17 MeV, or 13 MeV for a measurement spanning 1 .0– 1.6 M⊙. The gain is the longer lever arm in density, not the number of events; Ksym stays at its prior width (≈ 114 MeV) throughout. Low-mass neutron stars there-fore help the symmetry slope a little and its curvature 

not at all — consistent with the modest returns antici-pated by Ref. [15]; a dedicated multi-messenger, multi-mass study is left to follow-up work. Rare-isotope and heavy-ion experiments probe the same symmetry sector directly and at densities not cov-ered by the observed neutron-star mass range [95– 97]. We therefore repeated the sample-based and Ja-cobian calculations after replacing selected symmetry-sector marginal prior widths with the illustrative labo-ratory scenarios in Table I. Both calculations give the same pattern: the laboratory scenarios constrain Esym ,which the GW catalogue leaves unchanged, and reduce the Lsym width from 21 .2 to 13 .5 MeV. They move Ksym 

only when its width is imposed directly and leave Ksat 

unchanged. The two calculations agree on Lsym to within a MeV when no curvature width is assumed. These scenario widths are our choices, not published program commitments, and they idealize laboratory in-formation as marginal widths on expansion coefficients. A physical measurement instead constrains neutron-rich pressure over a density range and should enter as a density-tagged likelihood, as in Refs. [25, 26]. The adap-tive target supports that form below the matching den-sity n ≤ 2nsat (Secs. II B and IV B); extending it across the full experimental density range is left to future work. > V. CONCLUSIONS

We have introduced an adaptive-proposal procedure for nonparametric neutron-star equation-of-state infer-ence that leaves the scientific prior fixed while con-centrating new draws in the region supported by the data. We implemented the procedure both in acompressed principal-component representation of the neutron-star sequence, whose draws require no stellar-structure solve, and in a sound-speed representation with a jax -differentiable TOV solver. For fixed data, multi-ple importance sampling combined successive proposal generations into an estimate of the base-prior posterior, subject to the proposal-density and finite-archive qual-ifications of Sec. II D. For long catalogues, where those weights became degenerate in the 24-dimensional coeffi-cient space, we instead used a sequential Gaussian mo-ment projection and calibrated it against a 10 5-draw fixed-prior reference. For the catalogue and schedule tested here, the projection reproduced the mean to 0 .4% and broadened the standard deviation by a factor 1 .5. A fixed ensemble remains adequate for the binary neutron stars observed so far [1, 4]; adaptive proposals become useful when a large catalogue or additional electromag-netic and nuclear information concentrates the posterior relative to the prior. As a proof-of-concept application to GW170817, four NICER sources, and radio maximum-mass measure-ments, our nested posterior-overlap calculation gave 

R(1 .4) = 11 .7 ± 0.4 km, Λ(1 .4) = 309 ± 90, and Mmax =2.4 ± 0.2 M⊙. These summaries are conditional on the deliberately broad, QCD-consistent prior [60], its bridge calibration and finite archive, and the approximations and inherited sampling priors used for the NICER likeli-hoods; they are not a robust radius determination. In the synthetic 1000-event HLV catalogue, the effective sam-ple size of the fixed 972-draw archive fell from ∼ 470 to 

Neff ≃ 22, while proposal refinement kept the calcula-tion resolved. The resulting percent-level measurement of Λ(1 .4) nevertheless reduced the uncertainty in Lsym 

only from 29 to 21 MeV and left Ksym essentially un-changed. Extending the measured mass range helped more than adding events over the same range, while the illustrative laboratory priors sharpened the symmetry-sector parameters that the catalogue did not constrain (Table I). These results support a simple interpretation: at fixed experimental precision, the observable map sets the num-ber of EOS or nuclear-parameter combinations that the data can resolve. Repeated measurements over the same mass range narrow the combinations already visible; a broader mass lever arm, laboratory information, or a new observable is needed to expose additional direc-tions. This conclusion applies to the smooth, cold, beta-equilibrated stellar sequences studied here. Strong first-order transitions can introduce moving features and dis-connected stable branches for which the present smooth basis, single-branch representation, and local lineariza-tion are not faithful [70, 98, 99]. Natural next steps are therefore to develop transition-aware representations, use the differentiable solver for gradient-informed refine-ment and adaptive basis growth, and combine the EOS proposal with a jointly inferred neutron-star mass pop-ulation. The companion paper [71] separately examines how much resolved structure is induced by the bridge prior rather than by the data.

A value near zero means that neighboring draws support the fit there, while ℓi ≃ 1 means that the fit can reproduce that draw largely because the draw itself is present. Removing such a point forces an extrap-olation rather than an ordinary interpolation. There are Nfit = 25 fitted columns, including the con-stant, so the mean leverage is Nfit /N EOS ≃ 0.026. One draw instead has ℓi = 0 .9999. When that draw lands in the test subset, the remaining draws provide almost no local support for its prediction. As a result, ordi-nary ten-fold cross-validation — repeatedly fitting on 90% of the archive and predicting the other 10% — changes by more than an order of magnitude when the random division into folds is changed. This behavior is not cured by changing units or standardizing the coef-ficient columns: such rescalings leave the least-squares projection unchanged, and we recover the same cross-validation result to machine precision. We therefore apply a response-blind geometric screen before cross-validation. We remove the draw with the largest ℓi, recompute the leverages of the remaining en-semble, and repeat until max i ℓi < 0.5. Because this rule never examines aik , it cannot preferentially remove sound-speed curves that are hard to predict. It removes 9 of 972 draws; the held-out unrecovered fraction then becomes stable and agrees with the in-sample value, at 20 .8%. For the production archive, this geometric diagnosis has a direct physical counterpart. All nine removed draws have 1 .08 < M max /M ⊙ < 1.70, and seven do not support a 1 .4 M⊙ star, so even Λ(1 .4) is undefined. Al-though the prior imposes no hard maximum-mass cut, these configurations receive negligible weight from the measured massive-pulsar likelihood used in the inference (Sec. IV). Thus the screen used to validate Fig. 3 removes no production draw that would retain appreciable sup-port after the most elementary astrophysical information is applied. It is not an additional rejection in the infer-ence pipeline. The agreement is strong but not an identity, which is why we retain both arguments. Across the production archive and the three independently generated ensem-bles below, 29 of the 31 leverage-screened draws have 

Mmax < 1.9 M⊙; the other two have Mmax = 2 .20 and 2.35 M⊙. Leverage should therefore not be interpreted as a physical criterion. It ensures that held-out predic-tion is interpolation among supported coefficient vectors, while the separate Mmax audit shows that the production result is not stabilized by hiding otherwise viable EOS. The statistical answer is also insensitive to reasonable choices of cutoff: 0 .8, 0 .5, 0 .3, and 0 .2 remove between 0.2% and 3 .0% of the draws and give 20 .5–21 .4%. More aggressive rules, tested at 0 .1 and at twice the mean lever-age, are self-defeating. Every removed row raises the av-erage leverage to Nfit /N keep , where Nkeep is the number of surviving draws; the fixed cutoff can therefore become impossible to satisfy. We next test whether this conclusion depends on the particular finite archive. Splitting it into 20 half-samples — 10 independent random 50 /50 divisions, retaining both halves — and drawing 20 bootstrap replicas (same-size samples drawn with replacement) gives held-out un-recovered fractions of 21 .5 ± 0.8% and 21 .3 ± 0.4%, re-spectively. Each resample is screened independently, and the quoted spread is the variation among resamples, not an uncertainty on their mean. Resampling one archive cannot reveal a feature shared by every realization of the prior, so we also generated and TOV-solved three new 1000-draw ensembles with inde-pendent random seeds. They give 21 .7–23 .0%; across all 4 ensembles the held-out result is 22 .1 ± 0.9%. Each new ensemble again contains geometrically isolated draws: the same screen removes 3–14 draws. We conclude only that such isolated points recur in the bridge construction. Because a separate principal-component basis is fitted to each ensemble, this test does not show that the same direction in coefficient space is responsible every time. In one ensemble the 0 .5 cutoff leaves noticeable variation among cross-validation folds ( ±2.3%); tightening the cut-off to ℓi < 0.3 removes the remaining isolated draw with-out shifting the central values. The four-ensemble result 23 is then 21 .7±0.8%, with at most ±0.1% fold-to-fold vari-ation in any ensemble. The direction of this regression should not be confused with the consistency projector of Eq. (16). Here we fit an inverse reader, from observable coefficients to sound-speed amplitudes, to quantify how much EOS informa-tion the observables retain. A physical-image test in-stead starts from the forward derivative J of a specified EOS model and examines the component of an observable draw orthogonal to its local image. The former motivates a compact basis; only the latter can diagnose EOS con-sistency of the chosen observable modes at linear order. 

> Appendix C: Choosing the operating points

The loop of Sec. II D contains several apparently ad-hoc parameters: the redraw threshold, the per-generation ensemble size ndraw , and the refit regularizers (tempering 

β, shrinkage). In the synthetic headline run, N min eff = 10 3,

ndraw = 10 4, and refits occur after events 1, 74, and 690. This configuration is specific to that run. Under the full mixture weight Eq. (21) none of them touches the target: that estimator is consistent for any refit schedule and proposal sequence, so the operating points are a cost– variance trade-off, not modeling assumptions. Under the Gaussian-projection ansatz of Sec. IV the schedule does enter the target, through the sequence of projections, and the reported Neff measures sampling of the projected dis-tribution rather than of π0L1: n. A large Neff therefore certifies that the Monte Carlo noise is small; it does not 

by itself bound the projection error, which must be cal-ibrated externally — against the converged reference of Sec. IV, or against a well-sampled archive reweighting at smaller catalogues. Both diagnostics are cheap, and we report both; the brute-force cross-checks, not Neff , are the acceptance test.

It is worth prototyping the high-accuracy operat-ing point explicitly, because the common requirement is stronger than stable moments: one wants the 90% credible-interval endpoints reliably estimated. A quan-tile at probability p carries Monte Carlo error ∼pp(1 − p)/N eff /f (xp) in probability mass, so endpoint stability at the few-percent level needs Neff >

∼ 10 3, and visually noise-free bands ∼ 10 4. The recipe is mechani-cal: raise N min eff to the target and scale ndraw to roughly ten times it. The cost is modest because the per-event evidence is vectorized over draws: on a single current-generation core our per-event evaluation costs ≈ 0.35 s against 400 draws and only ≈ 0.5 s against 10 4 (roughly 0.33 s + 0 .05 s per thousand draws), so the full 10 3-event catalogue runs in ≈ 25 minutes at the N min eff = 10 2 oper-ating point, about an hour at 10 3, and a few hours (or tens of minutes parallelized over events) at 10 4 — hours, not weeks, and negligible against the cadence at which such analyses are actually performed. For the real-data pipeline the driver is instead the per-EOS RIFT evidence (∼ 40 s per EOS on a cluster node), so a 10 4-draw gen-eration costs ≈ 10 2 CPU-hours — an afternoon on a condor pool. Accordingly, the synthetic headline figures in this paper are produced at the N min eff = 10 3 operating point; the 10 2 results they replaced agree within their (larger) Monte Carlo scatter, as the schedule-invariance above guarantees.  

> 2. Other rejection proposals considered

We considered whether a compactness–Love relation could provide an additional rejection rule for observable-space draws. As an exploratory stress test, a cubic 

P3(log C) was fitted to the TOV-solved archive over 1 .1 ≤

m/M ⊙ ≤ 1.9 in steps of 0 .1, and a sampled mass point was labeled an outlier when | log Λ −P3(log C)| > 3σtrain .Among structurally valid draws, the outlier fractions are 1.2% for the broad Gaussian and 0 .3% after the pulsar-conditioned refit, compared with 0 .2% in the training archive. We did not adopt this proposal. The training fit it-self has rms scatter 0 .315 in ln Λ, so it detects only a conspicuous added tail. More fundamentally, the poly-nomial, mass grid, and cutoff are archive choices, and agreement is neither necessary nor sufficient for the exis-tence of a common EOS; quasi-universal relations retain EOS-dependent residuals [21, 100–102]. The calculation is therefore retained only as a stress test of the fitted cross-channel covariance. It never rejects or reweights a draw and is not used to validate the inference.
