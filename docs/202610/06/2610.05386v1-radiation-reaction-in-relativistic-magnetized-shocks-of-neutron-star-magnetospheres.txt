(Dated: October 6, 2026) The steepening of fast magnetosonic waves in the inner magnetospheres of magnetars can produce monster shocks, whose precursor emission may account for fast radio bursts (FRBs). For sufficiently bright bursts, particles crossing the shock radiate their energy within a single gyration period, raising the question of how radiation reaction affects the shock structure and its coherent emission. We investigate strongly cooled, relativistically magnetized shocks using a stationary multifluid model generalized to include the Landau–Lifshitz radiation-reaction force, together with particle-in-cell simulations of monster-shock dynamics. We show that the kinetic-scale shock transition is governed by only two dimensionless parameters: the Alfvénic Mach number and a cooling parameter τ that measures radiative losses over a gyration period. Coherent precursor emission is triggered above a critical Mach number that increases with τ , at which the beam dynamics becomes chaotic. Above this threshold, radiative cooling shifts the peak frequency of the precursor to higher values, scaling as τ 2/7. Remarkably, monster shocks remain far above the emission threshold even under strong radiative cooling. Radiation reaction therefore does not quench the precursor emission; instead, it primarily shifts the spectral peak into the kinetic regime. The luminosity of the precursor wave is determined solely by the luminosity of the driving kHz wave and the conversion efficiency, ϵX ∼

10 −3 − 10 −2.

I. INTRODUCTION 

The origin of fast radio bursts (FRB) is yet an open is-sue. The seemingly distinct behavior of repeating FRBs and their non-repeating counterparts, suggests that it is quite likely that more than one class of astrophysical ob-jects generate these bursts. It has been widely thought that the class of repeaters is associated with magnetars - strongly magnetized neutron stars. The recent detec-tion of a FRB from a known Galactic magnetar (although at the faint end of the population [1]) provides support to the hypothesis that magnetars can produce FRB [for review see 2]. Relativistic magnetized shocks have emerged as plau-sible emission sites of coherent radio bursts that may ac-count for the observed FRB [3–8]. Such shocks can form in the inner magnetosphere of a magnetar upon abrupt steepening of a millisecond fast magnetosonic wave [8– 12], or in shocks that form by the interaction of an es-caping magnetosonic pulse with the surrounding medium well beyond the light cylinder [3–5]. Recent plasma ki-netic simulations have revealed inherent generation of a high frequency precursor wave within a nonlinear solitary structure that form at the foot of the shock transition layer [6–8, 11, 13]. The details of the underlying emis-sion mechanism of the precursor wave have not been fully explored yet, although it likely involves the synchrotron maser instability, owing to formation of momentum ring distributions in the plasma crossing the solitons, prior to the onset of dissipation. However, the presence of har-monics associated with the distance between consecutive

In a previous paper [14] we constructed an analytic model of magnetized shocks, that treats the plasma as a large collection of stationary cold beams coupled through the magnetic field, and used it to compute the kinetic-scale shock transition. We have shown that the model can robustly reproduce the global shock structure ob-served in the Particle-In-Cell (PIC) simulations and, in addition, elucidates the underlying physics. In partic-ular, we demonstrated that shock dissipation does not require collective plasma interactions, as in the case of unmagnetized shocks, rather it stems from nonlinear dy-namics of charged particles in a stationary, quasiperiodic system of collisionless fast-magnetosonic solitons. The detailed shock structure is controlled by two parameters: the upstream Mach number and temperature. However, our analysis ignored the effect of radiation reaction on the shock structure, which might be important in mon-ster shocks [10], and conceivably also in far-wind models under certain conditions. The primary goal of this paper is to study the effect of strong cooling on the generation of precursor-wave emis-sion in strongly magnetized shocks, particularly mon-ster shocks. To this end, we employ the Entity PIC code [15, 16], adapted to the strong-cooling regime, in combination with the multifluid shock model described in [14]. Here, we generalize the multifluid model by incorpo-rating the radiative backreaction force into the fluid equa-tions. Combining PIC simulations with semi-analytic so-lutions of the shock equations enables us to derive ap-proximate scaling relations for the shock properties that can be extrapolated to the regime expected in astrophys-ical systems. > III. COHERENT EMISSION

.In the previous section, we saw that radiation reaction modifies the shock structure, with τ as the controlling pa-rameter. In Vanthieghem and Levinson [14] we showed that the leading-order structure of relativistically mag-netized shocks is not set by the unstable modes responsi-ble for the emission of extraordinary ( X) modes through the synchrotron-maser instability. That conclusion rests on weak coupling between the upstream plasma and the forward-emitted wave, an assumption that is not always satisfied here, as discussed in Sec. IV. Zhang et al. [17] report that cooling alters the emissivity of such a shock and can enhance it, albeit over a narrow range of pa-rameters. We are still missing a general scaling of the emission threshold in terms of the two shock parameters, 

τ and MA, which we derive below. The synchrotron-maser instability is driven by ring-type distributions, and its growth rate depends strongly on both the radius of the ring in the (˜ u∥, ˜u⊥) = (˜ ux, ˜uy )

plane and on its annular width, i.e. the ring temper-ature. Cooling acts on these two quantities in opposite ways: it reduces the ring radius, lowering the growth rate, and it narrows the annulus, raising it. The net depen-dence on τ is therefore non-monotonic, and the emission is expected to be most efficient at intermediate cooling strength. Figure 2 illustrates the effect on the phase-space distribution for τ = 1 , MA = 100 and u0 = 10 4,using N = 10 4 fluid beams. In contrast to the uncooled case of Vanthieghem and Levinson [14], in which phase mixing rapidly broadens the ring, the distribution here remains cold over extended downstream distances. We stress that these are genuine rings in the local velocity distribution at fixed ˜x, and not the apparent rings ob-tained by averaging a helical trajectory over several gy-ration periods. Such a configuration is favorable to the development of population inversion and to the associ-ated coherent emission [18, 19]. Downstream of the transition, the ring relaxes at a rate set by the jump conditions, which are modified by cooling. Beloborodov [10] showed that the shock Lorentz factor relative to the downstream plasma scales as γsh |d ≃ (1 + τ )1/7√σu. The multifluid model recovers this scaling, where solutions with different Mach num-bers collapse onto a single curve (top panel of Fig. 3). At larger τ they depart from it earlier and reach larger val-ues for smaller compression at lower MA. Recovering the correct jump conditions before and near this transition requires retaining the κ term of Eq. (2), which is usu-ally neglected in the strongly magnetized limit. As can be seen, this regime corresponds to a full suppression of the solitary structure, for which no emission is expected. However, efficient emission of the precursor wave is sup-pressed already when the flow approaches the subcritical regime, before complete quenching of the solitary struc-ture. If the emission of X modes requires the formation of a localized ring distribution, then a necessary, but not sufficient, condition is the onset of chaotic beam tra-jectories associated with the supercritical regime of the shock, and the ensuing phase mixing. Without radia-tion reaction, chaos sets in only above a critical Mach 5number, MA > M crit A ≃ 2 [14]; below it the shock transition is subcritical and the multifluid solutions col-lapse onto the single-fluid solution. To summarize, radiation reaction controls the coher-ent, X-mode emission of the monster shock through the single cooling parameter τ , which measures the radia-tive losses over a gyration time. From the multifluid model we obtained a general threshold for coherent emis-sion, M crit A ≃ 2 + 12 .6 τ 0.45 , set by the onset of chaotic beam dynamics; a peak frequency ω|d ≃ 5 τ 2/7√σu ωp for 

τ ≫ 1; and, for the emission efficiency, a kinematic cut-off fξ ≃ 10 −3 τ −2/7 following from the increased shock velocity at strong cooling. The suite of PIC simulations confirms these predictions: X-mode emission is progres-sively suppressed as τ increases and MA/M crit A falls below unity, the peak frequency upshifts as τ 2/7, and the spec-trum becomes narrow-banded near threshold, a feature we attribute to the transition to the subcritical regime rather than to cooling itself. > V. CONCLUSION

We have investigated the effects of radiation reaction on the structure of relativistically magnetized pair shocks and on the coherent precursor emission they generate, combining a generalized stationary multifluid model with fully kinetic simulations performed using the Entity code [15, 16]. Specifically, we extended the multifluid model of Vanthieghem and Levinson [14], in which the plasma is represented by 2N cold beams coupled through the elec-tromagnetic field, by incorporating the reduced Landau– Lifshitz force, given by Eq. (2), into the beam equations of motion. We find that the shock transition is governed by only two dimensionless parameters: the Alfvénic Mach num-ber MA and the cooling parameter τ , defined in Eq. (8), which measures the radiative energy losses over a gy-ration period. Increasing τ reduces the amplitude of the solitary structure, while enhancing its coherence and shortening its wavelength. The competition among these effects leads to a non-monotonic dependence of the pre-cursor emissivity on cooling strength. We have shown that chaotic beam dynamics, which is necessary for the formation of the ring distributions that drive the syn-chrotron maser, develops only above a critical Mach num-ber, MA > M crit A ≃ 2 + 12 .6 τ 0.45 ; below this thresh-old, phase mixing is negligible and no significant co-herent emission is expected. Above it, the peak fre-quency is determined by the spacing between succes-sive solitons and, in the strong-cooling regime, scales as 

ωpeak  

> |d

≃ 5 τ 2/7√σu ωp for τ ≫ 1.A suite of kinetic simulations confirms these predic-tions over five decades in cooling strength. The X-mode emission is progressively suppressed as τ increases and MA/M crit A falls below unity; the peak frequency increases as τ 2/7, and the spectrum becomes strongly narrow-banded near the threshold. Because the same spectral narrowing is observed in an uncooled shock at 

MA ≃ 2, we attribute it to the transition into the subcrit-ical regime rather than to cooling itself. We therefore ex-pect narrow-banding to be a generic signature of shocks operating close to criticality. When recast in terms of 

τ , the density-dependent efficiencies reported by Zhang 

et al. [17] collapse onto a single function of the cooling parameter. Moreover, the residual decline at large τ ,

fξ ≃ 10 −2 σ−1u τ −2/7, is kinematic rather than dynamical in origin. It follows that the fraction of energy tapped into X-modes, when integrated over the whole dissipa-tion region, is only a function of an almost constant 

ξB ∼ 10 −2 − 10 −1 [17]. The fraction of the available wave energy converted into X-mode emission is there-fore only weakly affected by cooling and maximized at large τ .Applied to monster shocks in magnetar magneto-spheres, these results lead to a simple picture. The cool-ing parameter is large at the base of the dissipation zone 

rs but declines steeply with radius, τ ∝ (rs/r )11 . By con-trast, the Mach number remains far above the threshold for X-mode generation throughout the dissipation zone, with MA/M crit A ∼ 10 3 (rs/r )−0.95 . Monster shocks are therefore both strongly cooled and strongly supercriti-cal for any reasonable choice of magnetospheric param-eters. Radiation reaction consequently does not quench the precursor emission, regardless of the luminosity of the driving kHz wave: the luminosity of the resulting burst is determined by the luminosity of the kHz wave and by the production efficiency ϵX ∼ 10 −3 − 10 −2, close to the value found by Vanthieghem and Levinson [8]. Strong cooling leads to ϵX ∼ 10 −2 and alters the spec-trum and geometry of the emitting region. It shifts the spectral peak to higher frequencies, pushes the escaping wave deeper into the kinetic regime, and extends the dis-sipation zone. Bright bursts, for which cooling is most important, should therefore be emitted at the highest frequencies. Several aspects of the problem remain open. The scal-ing relations derived here assume weak coupling between the shock and the precursor wave. Whether the wave can escape after dissipation at the shock is complete is a sep-arate question that cannot be addressed by our present setup, which follows the shock only across the dissipa-tion zone. The subsequent evolution of the wave will be investigated in a forthcoming study. The physical mech-anism underlying the coherent X-mode emission also re-mains to be clarified and will be addressed in future work building on Vanthieghem and Levinson [14]. Finally, the simulations presented here are one-dimensional; the mul-tidimensional dynamics of the soliton train and the re-sulting angular structure of the precursor emission are left for future investigation.

> Appendix D: Coupling between the precursor wave and the upstream flow

When cooling is strong but the shock remains above the emission threshold, MA > M crit A , the precursor wave is emitted and can decelerate the incoming plasma, so that γu falls below the value given by Eq. (16) and the up-stream magnetization increases by the same factor. The scaling relations of Sec. IV should therefore be regarded as upper bounds. In monster shocks, the precursor wave is in the strongly nonlinear regime, a0 ≫ 1 (Eq. (23)), and its interaction with the plasma ahead of the shock could decelerate the upstream flow and self-regulate the emission [22]. Interestingly, our simulations show a simi-lar deceleration, although the precursor wave remains in the linear regime, a0 ≪ 1.
