(Dated: October 5, 2026) We study the impact of a first-order phase transition from hadronic to deconfined quark matter on the evolution of proto-neutron stars. To maximize the applicability of our results, we construct an equation of state that is highly parameterizable, where we use a relativistic mean field approach to model hadronic matter and a constant speed of sound approach to model quark matter. We approximate the cooling behavior of proto-neutron stars as a sequence of decreasing entropy at constant baryonic mass. Some parameterizations lead to so-called ”twin-stars”, where two neutron stars have the same (gravitational) mass but different radii. Detecting them is regarded as a smoking gun signal for phase transition. We find, however, that proto-neutron star evolution suggests that gravitational twin stars are only possible if the hybrid star does not have, in addition, a baryonic twin. This rules out most twin star pairs, especially if the phase transition occurs at large densities. In a mass-radius diagram the unpopulated hybrid branch appears to be “in the shadow” of the hadronic branch. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Proto-neutron stars (PNSs) are the immediate rem-nants formed during the core collapse of massive stars. During the collapse of the stellar core the density can surpass multiple times nuclear saturation density 

ρ0 ≃ 2.7 × 10 14 g cm −3, which leads to a stiffening of the equation of state (EoS) due to repulsive short-range nuclear interactions and turns the infall into an ex-plosion. This “bounce” of the core generates a shock wave that initially propagates outward but loses large amounts of its kinetic energy through photo-dissociation of heavy nuclei and neutrino losses in the outer core. Several hundred milliseconds after core bounce, the shock wave may be revived through neutrino heating in the region behind the stalled shock, a process known as the delayed neutrino heating mechanism, which is the standard paradigm for core-collapse supernova explo-sions [1, 2], see [3–6] for reviews on the topic. During the early stage of the explosion, the PNS is extremely hot ( T ≳ 10 MeV), lepton-rich, and rapidly evolving. Over timescales of tens of seconds, it cools and delep-tonizes primarily through intense neutrino emission, eventually settling into a cold, stable neutron star [7–15]. Due to their high central densities, neutron stars are ideal investigatory objects to probe the EoS under extreme conditions. One interesting prospect is the onset of a phase transition from hadronic to quark matter somewhere within the density range of a neutron star core. Such a configuration is called a hybrid star and it features a quark matter core together with a hadronic mantle [16–24]. Hybrid stars cannot easily be distinguished from purely hadronic neutron stars,

While this approach does not contain any sub-stantial microphysical information, it allows us to make generalized statements about the occurrence of phase transitions without a strong model dependence. In fact, even though we refer to the CSS parameterized phase as quark matter, other exotic types of matter preceded by a sufficiently strong phase transition are equally applicable. > II. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK A. Description of Matter
> 1. Hadronic Matter

To explore the effect of a phase transition on the evo-lution of PNSs, we investigate a plethora of hadronic EoSs in section III before we include quark matter. The EoSs we employ are SRO(SLy4) [42–45], SRO(APR) [43, 46–48], HS(DD2) [49–52], HS(IUF) [50, 53–55], and SFH(SFHo) [50, 52, 56, 57], which we obtained from the CompOSE website [58–61]. In addition, we use multi-ple parameter sets of the Frankfurt-Barcelona (FB) EoS, which is a finite temperature extension [62] of the rel-ativistic mean field model used by Hornick et al. [63]. In this model the symmetry energy J, slope parame-ter L and effective nucleon mass m∗ are varied within the limits set by chiral effective field theory predictions and nuclear constraints. Here we set J = 32 MeV and 

L = 60 MeV, which maximizes the range of possible m∗.This approach allows us to connect in a consistent way the effective mass to the EoS’s stiffness, where smaller effective masses correspond to stiffer EoSs [64]. We use the values m∗/m n = 0 .65, 0 .70, and 0 .75, where mn

refers to the nucleon mass. A value of m∗/m n = 0 .65 leads to the stiffest FB EoS that is still compatible with GW170817 [63], while m∗/m n = 0 .75 yields the softest FB EoS that can still reach 2 M⊙. We refer to these EoSs as FB65, FB70, and FB75. In addition, we in-clude one case where we change the slope parameter to 

L = 40 MeV, which we denote as FB70L40. In section IV A we focus on the impact of phase transi-tions, we therefore limit ourselves to only one EoS. We chose the FB65 because it is the stiffest EoS in the FB family that is compatible with astrophysical results, pro-vided there is no phase transition. The FB EoS only describes bulk matter, therefore, we at-tach a low-density region described by the HS EoS [50]. In some cases our tables are insufficient in the small en-tropy and density regime. In such cases we switch to the zero temperature BPS EoS [65, 66].

> B. Proto-Neutron Star Evolution

This relies on the assumption that the timescale to establish hydrostatic equilibrium is much shorter than the neutrino cooling timescale [10, 15]. In either case, the mass-radius relation can regain stability at higher central density, forming asecond branch entirely composed of hybrid stars. Based on this, Alford et al. In the latter case, the hybrid part of the first branch is exceedingly small [35, 38]. For this reason, we will not distinguish between cases B and D later in this work. We provide a schematic depiction of this ABCD-categorization in Fig. 1. Furthermore, discretization errors (step size in TOV solver) can lead to hybrid stars that appear to be stable in the first branch, even if the Seidov limit is exceeded [83]. In order to counteract such errors, we do not use the Seidov limit to distinguish between absent and connected cases. Instead, we employ an entropy-dependent hybrid mass difference between 0 .01 M ⊙ and 0.02 M ⊙ as the deciding factor. EoSs with smaller mass differences are considered to be absent of hybrid stars, even if numerically some hybrid stars might be located at the top of the first branch. The presence of a second branch (i.e., cases B and D) provides a unique opportunity, because it allows two stars to have the same mass but vastly different radii. One of these stars has a quark matter core, while the other is purely hadronic. Such pairs are called twin stars; they are considered to be ’smoking gun’ signals for phase transitions in neutron stars. Christian et al. [38, 40] suggested a categorization scheme based on the maximum masses of both branches for these twin stars. Category I contains mass-radius relations in which both branches exceed 2 M ⊙, in category II only the first branch reaches 2 M ⊙. In category III only hybrid stars can reach 2 M ⊙ and category IV is a subset of category III, where purely hadronic neutron stars do not exceed 1 M ⊙, which would imply all currently known neutron stars to be hybrids. In Fig. 2 four representative examples for these categories are shown. The goal of this categorization is to provide an intuitive guideline to infer phase transition properties from the visual changes of the mass-radius relation. Particularly, the distance between the first and second branch can be related to the strength of the phase transition, where larger values of ∆ ε increase the separation, while the shape of the second branch indicates the transition pres-sure (and by implication, the transition density). Higher values of ptrans flatten the second branch and increase the length of the first one. This means categories I and II require a large transition pressure and feature second branches nearly parallel to the x-axis, while categories III and IV have much steeper second branches.   

> III. PURELY HADRONIC PROTO-NEUTRON STARS A. Entropy Dependence of the Maximum Mass Configuration

In this section we analyze the behavior of the maximal mass configuration of purely hadronic PNSs. We find that all zero-temperature mass-radius relations have a maximum located at smaller (gravitational) masses than their high entropy counterparts. This is expected, as the binding energy of individual PNSs increases during the cooling process, therefore reducing their gravitational mass at constant baryonic mass. When comparing the baryonic mass-radius relation at low and high entropy stages, the opposite behavior emerges. The maximum (baryonic) mass is increasing as the star cools down. As a result, there is a region at the top of the cooled-down mass-radius relation with higher baryonic masses than the maximum mass at the initial entropy stage. Stars in that region are unstable during large parts of the cool-ing process and can therefore not be produced by CCSN. However, once a neutron star has cooled down, subse-quent mass-accretion can populate this region. The mass range of this unpopulated region is highly dependent on the underlying EoS and on the initial entropy. An ex-ample for these unpopulated regions is shown in Fig. 3 for the FB65 EoS. The neutron stars at s/n b = 0 .1 that cannot be realized by CCSNe following our argument above are indicated by a thick black line. In Fig. 4 we show the behavior of the maximum mass as a function of entropy for all the EoSs we used in this work. On the left side the maximum gravitational mass is shown, and on the right side the baryonic mass. There are two EoSs for which the maximal baryonic mass de-creases with entropy, SLy4 and IUF. This would imply a destabilization of very massive PNS during the cool-ing process, instead of a region of unpopulated masses. However, we stress that this is a side effect of assuming a constant charge fraction throughout the PNS evolu-tion. When considering two “snapshots” to approximate a more realistic scenario at a high charge fraction for 

s/n b = 3 .0 and beta-equilibrium at s/n b = 0 .1, these two EoSs also feature an unpopulated region. Likewise, the unpopulated region of the remaining EoSs increases for such a comparison. All EoSs used by us are without strangeness. However, if hyperons are included in the EoS, their onset leads to a softening of the EoS, which, in turn, can lead to a decreasing maximum baryonic mass during the cooling phase, even under more realistic conditions [7–11, 75].  

> B. Proto-Neutron Star Density Profiles

Here we explore the density profiles during the cooling process of PNSs. In general, a hot PNS features a larger radius than its cooled-down counterpart with the same baryonic mass. This means globally the density of the star is increasing when cooling down. For most stars, this is also locally true, i.e., for every fixed enclosed baryonic mass, the density is increasing as the entropy is decreasing. In other words, the density profile is shifted to higher densities as the PNS cools down, but it retains its general shape, the hot core and hot mantle are less dense than the cold core and crust, respectively. This is not the case for the most massive neutron stars. Counterintuitively, their core region becomes less dense as the star is cooling down, while the outer regions behave normally. Nevertheless, even for the most massive stars, the density rises monotonically from the PNS surface to the core. We give an example of this behavior in the first row of Fig. 5, where the density profile at various entropy stages is shown. We use the FB65 EoS, which is a particularly stiff case and therefore features a large maximum mass. On the left side, the density profiles of a MB = 2 M ⊙ PNS are shown, on the right side, the maximum stable mass. For the MB = 2 M ⊙ case, the density profile retains its general shape during the entire evolution; only the drop in density at the PNS surface becomes more pronounced as the star cools down. This is the expected behavior that most stars exhibit. However, on the right side of Fig. 5 significant changes in the density profile can be seen during the cooling process. As the star cools down, the core decreases in density, while the outer regions increase in density. This unexpected behavior warrants a closer look. The most massive track of Fig. 3 corresponds to the density profile shown in the upper right panel of Fig. 5. Since its gravitational mass is monotonically decreasing, the binding energy of the entire PNS is increasing, even as the core becomes less dense. Therefore, we can assume that these evolutionary paths are energetically stable. Furthermore, we verified that this behavior is not caused by our assumption of flat charge and entropy profiles by again comparing two density snapshots of a high charge fraction at s/n b = 3 .0 and beta-equilibrium at s/n b = 0 .1. Nevertheless, we cannot fully rule out that this behavior is due to our quasi-static approach and it might not occur in a fully dynamical simulation. If a PNS features such an unusual density profile evolution, it implies an expanding core region and a shrinking mantle as the star cools down. As a result, it is possible for particularly massive PNSs to increase their total radius during the early cooling stages if the initial entropy was sufficiently high. An example of this can be seen in Fig. 3, where the radius of the most massive track increases slightly for the first entropy stages before the PNS starts to contract normally. The extent to which this counterintuitive density profile is exhibited depends on the EoS. Some EoSs do not show this behavior at all. An example of an EoS where the density profile shows the same behavior for all its masses is depicted in the second row of Fig. 5 for SLy4. It should be noted that the maximum mass of SLy4 is significantly smaller than FB65. For the EoSs that we investigated, we found that FB65, FB70, DD2, and APR4 show a decreasing central density at high masses and entropies. For the FB75 and SFHo EoSs, the shape of the density profile changes significantly, but the central density at high entropies remains roughly constant. The SLy4 and IUF EoSs show qualitatively similar density profiles for all stable masses.

> B. Twins in the Shadow

In this section we examine the special case of twin stars in more detail. For this purpose, we need to differentiate between baryonic and gravitational twins. We find that hybrid stars are generally stronger bound and therefore have a smaller gravitational mass. An example of this is shown on the left side of Fig. 8 with a purple dashed line. On the right side of the same figure, the baryonic and gravitational twins of the first branch maximum and the second branch minimum are shown. Baryonic twins are again indicated by purple dashed lines, and gravitational twins are shown by green dashed lines. Due to the difference in binding energy, there are regions where gravitational twins do not have a baryonic twin. Specifically, these regions are located at the upper end of the twin star mass range on the hybrid star branch and at the lower end of the twin star mass range on the hadronic branch. All other neutron stars in the twin star mass range have a baryonic twin. This is important for the evolution of PNSs, because baryonic twins must have followed different evolutionary paths despite having the same baryonic mass, making it likely that only one of them is realized in nature. Under our assumption of equilibrium, no hadronic PNS reaches the necessary central pressure to become a hybrid PNS, as long as there is also a stable hadronic solution available. The hydrodynamical simulations performed by Espino et al. [87] support this point. They perturbed configurations in the unstable region between the hadronic and hybrid branches, leading to radial oscillations and bouncing between both phases. Ultimately, these configurations settled onto the stable hadronic branch. This suggests that a baryonic hybrid twin may need to be produced in a quasistatic way to circumvent the bounce back (see also [88]). However, this is not possible in the model we investigate here, since reaching the phase transition point would trigger a collapse on the dynamical time scale. Therefore, a hadronic PNS cannot become a hybrid PNS unless it is the only stable solution. In other words, we find that no cooled-down hybrid star can have a baryonic twin, with only one exception: the initial PNS is already a hybrid star with a baryonic twin. This can be checked by comparing Fig. 7, which shows all PNS paths in our model. However, CCSN simulations performed by Naseri 

et al. [85] suggest that only the hadronic solution can be realized, even if both a hadronic and a hybrid PNS are possible for a given baryonic mass. The collapsing core first reaches the stable hadronic solution, and the collapse stops there. The core bounce happens below the transition pressure even for extreme initial perturbations. Nevertheless, Naseri et al. [85] find that once the PNS starts to cool and the transition point is reached, the resulting collapse can be stabilized before the PNS turns into a black hole. Following this line of reasoning, we find that hy-brid stars can only be formed if they have no baryonic twin. Fig. 9 highlights this by showing a representative mass-radius relation with evolutionary tracks of different PNSs. The area not reached by any track on the second branch appears to be “in the shadow” of the first branch. We therefore refer to this phenomenon of unpopulated twin stars as “twins in the shadow”.

> VI. CONCLUSION

In this work, we systematically studied the evolution of proto-neutron (PNS) stars with and without phase transitions to quark matter. We approximated the cooling phase of PNSs by taking “snapshots” of the mass-radius relation at decreasing entropy stages. In addition, we assumed flat profiles of entropy and charge fraction for each PNS. By assuming that no mass is accreted during the cool-down, we were able to track the evolutionary path of individual PNSs by following constant baryonic masses through the mass-radius relations. In section III we investigated purely hadronic cases and outlined that the maximum baryonic mass con-figuration is decreasing with entropy, in other words, the slope of MB,max (s) is negative. This leads to a range of masses at the top of a zero temperature mass-radius relation that cannot be populated by the PNS evolution alone. The size of this unpopulated region is determined by the EoS. Furthermore, we found that for some EoSs the most massive stars exhibit a counterintuitive behavior in their density profile. the average density is increasing as the star cools down, some EoSs show a decreasing central density com-pared to their hot counterparts. This counterintuitive behavior is not present for all EoSs, but seems to be associated with EoSs that feature high maximum masses. In section IV we added a phase transition to one of the stiffer EoSs of the previous section, which gives us the largest parameter space to explore. We found that the top of the zero temperature mass-radius relation still has an unpopulated region at its top, even with the onset of quark matter. However, if the EoS contains a phase transition, the maximum baryonic mass of purely hadronic stars decreases as the star cools down, because it is determined by the transition pressure, which decreases with decreasing entropy. We identify four broad scenarios for a proto-neutron star’s evolution: 1. The transition is not triggered at all, the PNS will remain purely hadronic. 2. The transition takes place during the evolution, once the necessary pressure is reached in the center of the PNS. The PNS will become a hybrid star. 3. The transition takes place during the supernova, i.e. the initial PNS is already a hybrid and will remain so. 4. The PNS collapses or explodes before a stable hy-brid star with the same baryonic mass can be formed. We further find that a hadronic PNS will not reach a sufficient central pressure to trigger a phase transition, as long as a stable hadronic solution is possible. This affects the second scenario significantly, since a hadronic star can only become a hybrid star, if it stars to collapse. As a result we find that a cold hybrid star can only be produced, if it has no baryonic twin, i.e. a hadronic star with the same baryonic mass. The only exception to this rule would be the third scenario, where the initial PNS is already a hybrid. However, dynamical simulations (e.g. Naseri et al. [85]) suggest that the hybrid twin is less likely to form. In an even more recent work Naseri and Paschalidis [88] reinforce this point, by showing that a hadronic twin is favored, despite the hybrid twin being energetically favorable. They argue that this is due to the realistic cooling timescale being significantly longer than the timescale of a collapse. We refer to the phenomenon that cooled down hybrid stars cannot have a baryonic twin as “twins in the shadow”, since the stable hybrid star solutions on the disconnected branch of a mass-radius relation appear to be “in the shadow” of the purely hadronic branch. While baryonic twin stars are ruled out in this ap-proach gravitational twin stars are still possible, because hadronic and hybrid stars have different binding energies. Finally, in section V we show that this “twins in the shadow” phenomenon has a strong impact on the feasibility of twin stars, especially if the phase transition takes place at high pressures and features a large jump 14 in energy density. In such cases the disconnected hybrid star branch can be fully within the shadow of the hadronic branch, ruling out twin stars for these parameter sets entirely. We also show that this can be somewhat mitigated by assuming relatively small initial entropy for the PNS, but CCSN simulations suggest that higher masses imply higher initial entropy [89]. By employing the constant speed of sound parameteri-zation for quark matter and a quasi-static approach for the PNS evolution we made our approach as general as possible. However, this also means that we had to make some approximations, which could effect our results. In particular, we could not investigate the timescale of the evolutionary steps, since in our approach there is no suitable way to approximate how much time passes between snapshots in our quasi-static approach. A full dynamical simulation of particularly interesting paths could help to better understand the underlying physical processes. Furthermore, our approach implicitly assumes a rapid conversion rate from hadronic and quark matter, but a slow conversion rate is hypothesized to stabilize parts of the region separating hadronic and hybrid stars. Changing the conversion rate could potentially increase the parameter space that allows for twin stars. Apart from these caveats our results should be applicable to a broad range of EoSs and quark matter parameterizations. Recent works have suggested that twin stars are unlikely to be compatible with astrophysical constraints but not entirely ruled out [40, 90–92]. Our analysis adds another argument against their realization if the phase transition occurs at high densities. However, phase transitions that do not produce twin stars are not strongly affected by our analysis, have been shown to fit astrophysical constraints [40, 90] and appear likely to occur [93–95].
