A dark-matter core and a soft nuclear equation of state can produce similar mass–radius signatures, motivating a complementary probe through the f -mode spectrum. We compute quadrupolar f -mode frequencies and gravitational-wave damping times in full general relativity for cold, nonrotating neutron stars admixed with self-interacting bosonic dark matter (DM). Our two-fluid survey spans 21 nuclear equations of state covering nucleonic, hyperonic, ∆-admixed, and combined hyperon–∆ compositions at FDM = 1–20%. The main sequences use λ = π and boson masses of 500 and 100 MeV for the core and halo studies, respectively. Independent normal-matter (NM) and DM displacements couple through the metric, yielding NM-led and DM-led f -mode branches whose radiative damping is determined by outgoing-wave boundary conditions. At 1 .4 M⊙, the core DM-led damping time decreases from order 10 3 s at FDM = 1% to order 1 s at 20%, approximately following an empirical F −2DM dependence, while NM-led damping times remain within 0 .12–0 .33 s. Halo DM-led damping instead increases with dark fraction: at the same stellar mass and FDM = 20%, dark radii approach 88 km, frequencies fall to 0 .14 kHz, and damping times reach approximately 450 s. Selected high-mass core sequences exhibit strong DM-led damping-time upturns not reproduced by the leading mass-quadrupole diagnostic. Representative core calculations show greater DM-led sensitivity to the bosonic self-coupling and retain both branches at ℓ = 3. These results show how dark-core and dark-halo configurations affect the oscillation spectrum and radiative damping. Under the adopted nonrotating, circular-binary prescriptions, no evaluated configuration satisfies all geometric and perturbative conditions for a controlled linear tidal resonance.

The binary-neutron-star event GW170817 provided the first direct gravitational-wave (GW) constraint on neutron-star tidal deformability, with the initial low-spin analysis yielding ˜Λ ≤ 800 at the 90% credible level [1]. Complementary mass–radius posteriors have been ob-tained from the Neutron Star Interior Composition Ex-plorer (NICER) pulse-profile analyses of PSR J0030+0451 [2, 3], PSR J0740+6620 [4, 5], PSR J0437 −4715 [6], and PSR J0614 −3329 [7]. At the low-mass end, carbon-atmosphere X-ray spectral modeling of the central compact object in the supernova remnant HESS J1731 −347 has been used to infer an unusually light neutron star with M ≃ 0.77 M⊙ and R ≃ 10 .4 km; these values, however, remain sensitive to the assumed atmo-sphere and source properties [9, 10]. Static mass–radius and tidal observables primarily constrain the equilibrium structure, whereas quasinormal-mode (QNM) asteroseis-mology probes its dynamical response. Earlier full-GR calculations of DM effects on the f mode employed effec-tive single-fluid descriptions [31, 32], whereas two-fluid Cowling studies identified component-associated modes in core and halo configurations but could not determine their gravitational-wave damping times [33, 34]. Kumar et al. subsequently formulated the full-GR polar problem for in-dependently conserved, gravitationally coupled fluids and computed representative real-frequency spectra for mirror-DM stars, without extracting radiative damping times [35]. Zheng et al. later imposed an outgoing-wave condition and obtained complex frequencies, damping times, and core-to-halo mode evolution for self-interacting fermionic DM using one microscopic normal-matter EOS [36]. Here we perform a systematic full-GR complex-mode survey for self-interacting bosonic DM using 21 cold, beta-equilibrated normal-matter EOS spanning nucleonic, hy-peronic, ∆-admixed, and combined hyperon–∆ compo-sitions. We consider FDM = MDM /(MNM + MDM ) = 1% , 5% , 10%, and 20%, and construct both dark-core and dark-halo sequences. For each configuration, we solve the coupled polar perturbation equations for the two in-dependent fluid displacements and their shared metric perturbations. Matching the quadrupolar ( ℓ = 2) solution to a purely outgoing Zerilli wave yields the complex eigen-frequency and hence both the mode frequency and its radiative damping time. We track the two f -like families by spectral continuity and classify their local character us-ing the node structure and relative amplitudes of the two fluid displacements, supplemented in halo configurations by frequency proximity to the corresponding single-fluid NM mode. We additionally calculate the octupolar ( ℓ = 3) modes for a representative EOS, examine the influence of the bosonic self-coupling within the core configuration, and compare the equilibrium mass–radius sequences with published observational regions inferred from GW170817, NICER, and HESS J1731 −347. Across the EOS ensemble, the NM-led family remains continuously connected to the ordinary single-fluid f

mode, whereas the additional DM-sensitive family re-sponds strongly to the dark fraction, spatial distribution, and self-interaction. Extended halos reach radii approach-ing 90 km at FDM = 20%, shifting the DM-led oscillation into the sub-kilohertz regime and substantially increasing its radiative damping time, while selected core configura-tions become weakly radiating. The broad EOS coverage separates these geometry-driven effects from variations associated with the normal-matter composition. Finally, for the nonrotating circular binaries evaluated here, none of the isolated-star modes satisfies all necessary condi-tions for a clean linear resonance before contact or Roche overflow. The tidal analysis therefore determines the accessibility of this excitation channel rather than estab-lishing detector-level detectability. The paper is organized as follows. The normal- and dark-matter EOS are described in Sections II.1 and II.2. The full-GR two-fluid perturbation framework is presented in Section II.3. The numerical implementation and val-idation tests are described next, followed by the tidal-dephasing formalism in Section II.5. The core and halo results are discussed in Sections III.1 and III.2, and the ac-cessibility of tidal resonances is examined in Section III.3. Section IV presents the physical interpretation, obser-vational prospects, comparison with previous work, and limitations. The detailed tidal derivation, representative eigenfunctions, dark-matter self-interaction scan, higher-multipole results, and numerical diagnostics are collected in the Appendices. Finally, Section V summarizes the main findings.   

> II. METHODOLOGY II.1. Nuclear-matter equations of state

For the normal-matter sector we use 21 cold, beta-equilibrated equations of state, chosen to sample the two axes along which dense matter is least well determined: its composition, and its stiffness at supra-saturation density. Where present, the model-name suffix identifies the composition: N denotes purely nucleonic npeμ matter, 

Y the inclusion of the hyperon octet, and D the inclu-sion of the ∆(1232) isobars; N Y D contains both exotic species. For the parameterizations of Set 3, the label also encodes the two nuclear-matter parameters that are varied: the nucleon effective mass m∗/m N at saturation and symmetry-energy slope L. These parameters probe different aspects of the EOS: variations in m∗/m N pre-dominantly affect the high-density stiffness and Mmax ,whereas variations in L predominantly affect the pressure near saturation and R1.4.The ensemble is organized into three sets. Set 1 

comprises five unified nucleonic models (EOS1, EOS2, EOS6, EOS11, and EOS20) selected from the nonlin-ear relativistic mean-field ensemble of Malik, Pais, and Providˆ encia [37], sampling a broad range of nucleonic stiffnesses. Set 2 combines three separately supplied DD2-based EOS with five models extracted from the CompOSE database [38, 39]. The first group comprises DD2 ND, containing ∆ isobars; DD2 NY, containing the hyperon octet; and DD2 NYD, containing both hyper-ons and ∆ isobars. The CompOSE group comprises the hyperonic DD2Y model [40]; the density-dependent hy-peronic DDHdY4 model [41, 42]; the nonlinear relativistic mean-field GM1Y5 model with hyperons [42, 43]; the chi-ral mean-field CMF-crust model [44–47]; and the purely nucleonic, nonrelativistic Skyrme model SLy4 [48, 49]. 

Set 3 comprises eight nonlinear relativistic mean-field (RMF) parameterizations based on the parameter-variation scheme of Ref. [50], with the symmetry energy at saturation fixed at J = 30 MeV. The four hyperonic mod-els use m∗/m N = 0 .55 or 0 .60 with L = 50 or 60 MeV, whereas the four nucleonic models use m∗/m N = 0 .70 or 0.75 with L = 40 or 50 MeV. Thus, within each fixed composition and effective mass, the models form pairs differing only in L; effective mass and composition re-main correlated across the set and cannot be separated independently. 3Hyperons and ∆ isobars can soften the EOS by re-distributing the baryonic degrees of freedom, but their onset and effects on Mmax and R1.4 depend on the meson– baryon couplings [39, 51]. Since composition and dark matter both modify stellar compactness and the f -mode spectrum, the EOS survey is needed to distinguish their effects. The model-by-model results are reported in Ta-ble I.

> II.4. Numerical implementation and validation

The two-fluid TOV equations are integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme using a default radial spacing ∆ r = 0 .5 m. For each trial complex frequency, the regular interior basis solutions are evolved outward, the two fluid-surface conditions are imposed by affine shoot-ing and linear combination, and the resulting solution is matched to the outgoing Zerilli exterior. The com-plex eigenfrequency is obtained by solving Ain (ωα) = 0 with a complex Newton iteration. The default calculation uses double-precision complex arithmetic; weakly radi-ating modes are recomputed with compiled long-double 5and IEEE binary128 implementations, while the most cancellation-sensitive poles are refined with 40–60-decimal-digit arbitrary precision. Convergence is tested against the interior radial spac-ing, exterior step and matching radius, initial root seed, and arithmetic precision. In the single-fluid limit, the mass agrees at the quoted precision, while the radius and NM-led frequency agree within 0 .013% and 0 .001%, respectively. At real frequency, the complex interior re-produces the original implementation to 10 −15 for the NM-led mode and 3 × 10 −12 for the DM-led mode. For the 157 halo targets with certified DM-led complex modes, independent long-double and binary128 implementations differ by at most 0 .0025% in frequency and 0 .129% in damping time. The largest final spatial-resolution change in the halo calculation is 0 .253% in the damping time, while halving the exterior step and doubling the matching radius changes it by at most 0 .023%.

Fig. 2 presents the full-GR quadrupolar f -mode fre-quencies of the dark-matter-core configurations of Fig. 1. Solid and dashed curves denote the normal-matter-led and dark-matter-led two-fluid branches, and dotted curves the pure normal-matter single-fluid reference. Two branches appear because each fluid carries its own displacement field while both source and respond to the same metric perturbation [35, 36]: the NM-led branch is dominated by motion of the outer normal fluid and continuously approaches the single-fluid f -mode as FDM → 0, whereas the DM-led branch is carried by the inner dark component and has no counterpart when the dark fluid is removed. The higher DM-led frequency is set by the compact spa-tial support of the mode rather than by the dark-matter density alone. The oscillation is confined to the inner dark 8core and responds to the total gravitating mass enclosed within that region, giving it a shorter dynamical timescale than the NM-led mode. Across the plotted fixed-FDM 

sequences, both branches show an overall increase in fre-quency with mass as the configurations become more compact. At a common mass, increasing FDM shifts both branches upward: the NM-led shift follows the contraction of RNM in Fig. 1, whereas the DM-led shift also reflects the restructuring of the dark core and its coupling to the shared metric, and therefore cannot be reduced to the total stellar compactness alone. Across the EOS ensemble at fixed FDM , both branches broadly follow the normal-matter compactness ordering: models with smaller R1.4 generally produce higher fre-quencies. This ordering reflects the complete density and sound-speed profiles rather than the presence of hyper-ons or ∆ isobars alone. At FDM = 10%, the nucleonic EOS6 model ( R1.4 = 10 .87 km) gives the highest NM-led frequency, while DD2 NYD ( R1.4 = 11 .26 km) gives the marginally highest DM-led frequency; the large-radius RMF m55 L60 NY model ( R1.4 = 13 .22 km) gives the lowest frequency on both branches. III.3. Accessibility of the tidal resonances 

Fig. 6 applies the accessibility conditions of Eq. (20) to the halo sequences of Sec. III.2. Across the 52 evalu-ated configurations (13 EOS and four dark fractions), the resonance frequency exceeds the equal-mass L1 reference frequency by a mean factor 1 .68 with population scatter 0.06. Thus, under this criterion, the halo reaches L1 be-fore resonance. The weak variation of this ratio follows from the shared dynamical scale: both the DM-led fre-quency and the equal-mass L1 reference frequency scale approximately as pGM/R 3DM . Increasing FDM lowers fα

(Fig. 5), but the accompanying halo expansion (Fig. 4) also lowers fL1; none of the evaluated configurations thereby passes the pre-overflow condition. A broader evaluation uses all 1 ,527 positive-frequency halo points from the full sampled sequences, including points outside the retained plotting sample. Testing each against 1 ,301 companion masses from 1 .0 to 2 .3 M⊙ gives zero cases satisfying the adopted Roche-lobe criterion, with a maximum RL/R DM = 0 .906 (Table III). These tests use isolated spherical stellar radii and the corotat-ing Roche geometry of Eq. (22). Nonsynchronous Roche models show that the critical geometry also depends on the rotation state [81]. For an approximately irrotational inspiral, a quantitative overflow boundary therefore re-quires an appropriate binary treatment. The zero-pass result is consequently conditional on the adopted geomet-ric prescriptions, rather than a dynamical prediction of tidal mass loss from the halo. The compact core configurations fail for the complemen-tary reason: their dark-matter-led frequencies are high (Sec. III.1), so the resonance separation lies inside the summed material radii. For a 1 .4+1 .4 M⊙ binary with a 12-km ordinary-neutron-star companion, the DM-led resonance separation lies 5 .7–9 .8 km inside the contact sep-aration in all 82 configurations, and no tested companion mass up to 2 .3 M⊙ changes this at the assumed compan-ion radius. > IV. DISCUSSION IV.1. What the two branches measure

The two f -mode branches have complementary sensitiv-ities. The NM-led frequency broadly tracks NM compact-ness and depends only weakly on λ in our EOS1 core scan, whereas the DM-led frequency responds more strongly to the dark component’s distribution and self-interaction through their effects on pressure support and the shared gravitational field. For core configurations at 1 .4 M⊙,the DM-led radiative damping time decreases by roughly three orders of magnitude between FDM = 1% and 20%, approximately following the empirical trend τ 1.4DM ∝ F −2DM .These complementary sensitivities matter because nu-clear softening and a compact dark core can both reduce stellar radii and maximum masses; a frequency governed mainly by compactness need not distinguish their origins. The stronger fraction dependence of τDM provides addi-tional information, but not a unique measure of FDM . At 1.4 M⊙, the damping time instead increases with frac-tion in halo configurations, and low-fraction halos overlap higher-fraction cores in damping time. Joint measure-ments of fNM , fDM , and τDM could therefore help dis-tinguish the dark distribution from nuclear-EOS effects. Whether these observables break parameter degeneracies requires a dedicated inference analysis, which is beyond the present survey.  

> IV.2. Detectability

Assessing whether the predicted modes are observable with the Einstein Telescope or Cosmic Explorer requires an excitation model and a detector-response analysis [82– 84]. Koehn et al. have studied Einstein Telescope con-straints on fermionic DM through its effect on equilibrium tidal deformability [85]. Our tidal analysis instead tests the accessibility of mode resonances and does not provide a detector forecast. The core-induced increase in the NM-led frequency could bias a single-fluid interpretation of the nuclear EOS if the dark component were neglected, but its measurability cannot be established from the frequency shift alone. For the DM-led core branch, increasing the dark frac-tion shortens the radiative lifetime but also raises the frequency; neither change alone establishes improved de-tectability. For a narrowband ringdown observed over its full decay, the matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio scales as SNR ∝ E1/2GW /[Df pSn(f )], up to angular factors, where 

EGW is the radiated energy, D the source distance, and 

Sn the one-sided detector noise spectral density [86]. A longer lifetime reduces the instantaneous radiative power at fixed mode energy but extends the signal; its effect on detectability depends on the observing duration and competing dissipation. The tabulated frequencies and damping times therefore do not, by themselves, deter-mine the relative detection reach of the branches. An excitation model and detector-level analysis are required. For resonant inspiral signals, Yu and Weinberg found hyperonic g-mode phase shifts of order 10 −3 rad diffi-cult to detect; their favorable stacking prospects con-cern the larger muonic-mode signals [87]. A study using full-GR frozen-composition g1 modes reported |∆Φ g1 | ≤ 

1.410 × 10 −3 rad, about 21 times below its adopted 0 .03-rad favorable-event ET benchmark [88]. This benchmark is not a universal detection threshold. A detector-level analysis of our DM-led modes with ET or CE lies outside the present survey [89, 90]. The large-fraction halo modes reach the hundred-hertz range, but this does not make their isolated resonances ac-cessible. Halo expansion lowers both the mode frequency and the Roche-overflow frequency through their shared dy-namical scale. In the evaluated halo sample, the adopted Roche criterion is reached before resonance (Section III.3), so Eq. (18) provides no controlled phase estimate for these cases. This restricts the clean-resonance channel in the adopted nonrotating, circular-binary model, not all possi-ble GW signatures. Off-resonant tides, rotation, eccentric-ity, tidal mass loss from the halo, and post-contact exci-tation remain outside the present calculation. Numerical-relativity simulations have explored mergers of neutron stars admixed with fermionic DM [91]. Simulations of rapidly and retrogradely spinning neutron-star binaries have also resolved f -mode tidal resonance [92], while a dedicated Bayesian study has assessed tidal-resonance detectability with the Einstein Telescope [93]. Extending the present bosonic two-fluid analysis to rotating binaries and merger dynamics remains for future work. 16  

> IV.3. Relation to previous work

The core–halo structural trends are qualitatively con-sistent with earlier studies of repulsively self-interacting bosonic DM [23, 80]. Component-associated mode fami-lies and low-frequency halo oscillations were already iden-tified in two-fluid Cowling calculations [33, 34]. Kumar et al. developed the full-GR polar framework and computed real-frequency spectra for mirror-DM configurations, with-out extracting radiative damping times [35]. Zheng et al. obtained complex frequencies and damping times across core and halo configurations for self-interacting fermionic DM with one microscopic nuclear EOS [36]. The present study complements these developments with a full-GR complex-mode survey of bosonic DM across 21 nuclear EOS, examining the dependence on nuclear composition, dark fraction, and core–halo geometry. Our strongest dark-core endpoint upturns are qual-itatively consistent with the high-mass suppression of outgoing-wave coupling identified by Zheng et al., rather than their low-mass matter-quadrupole cancellation [36]. In our pronounced upturns, RQ increases toward the end-point, while the leading-quadrupole diagnostic underpre-dicts the strongest damping-time growth. The ensemble establishes how the occurrence and strength of this weakly radiating behavior vary with the nuclear EOS and dark fraction. We additionally test whether the computed modes can undergo a controlled linear tidal resonance before contact or Roche overflow, finding no configuration that satisfies all required conditions under the adopted binary prescriptions (Section III.3).  

> IV.4. Limitations

We consider nonrotating, unmagnetized stars com-posed of cold barotropic NM and a repulsively self-interacting bosonic condensate; the dark sector is fixed to a bosonic condensate throughout, and we do not at-tempt the fermionic-vs-bosonic comparison carried out elsewhere [94]. The two components interact only through gravity; direct NM–DM interactions, finite-temperature effects, crustal elasticity, and NM superfluidity are omit-ted. Their effects on the mode spectrum are therefore not quantified here. The main survey fixes λ = π, while the self-coupling scan is restricted to EOS1 core config-urations; the corresponding dependence of halo modes remains unexplored. The calculated damping times describe GW emission within the ideal-fluid QNM model. Shear and bulk vis-cosity, together with possible dark-sector dissipation, can modify the physical decay time. Establishing the domi-nant damping mechanism requires evaluating these pro-cesses using the relevant microphysics and each mode’s fluid motion. The present calculation therefore does not establish a dark-fraction threshold above which the radia-tive damping time equals the total physical lifetime. The dark fraction is prescribed rather than derived from a formation or capture history. Percent-level admixtures require a scenario beyond standard ambient capture in the solar neighbourhood [16]. Finally, although the isolated stellar modes are calculated in full GR, the tidal-dephasing estimate uses leading-order source and orbital matching, 

κtide = κorb = 1, subject to the applicability conditions of Eq. (20) and the accessibility tests of Section III.3. The Roche criterion is a quasi-equilibrium prescription, not a dynamical calculation of halo mass transfer, and the phase estimate is not a complete binary waveform.  

> V. CONCLUSION

We have computed quadrupolar f -mode frequencies and radiative damping times of bosonic DM-admixed neu-tron stars in full GR, using 21 nuclear EOS spanning nucleonic, hyperonic, ∆-admixed, and combined hyperon– ∆ compositions. The survey covers FDM = 1% , 5% , 10%, and 20% in both core and halo geometries. The spec-trum contains two principal branches: the NM-led branch connects to the ordinary single-fluid f mode, while the additional DM-led branch reflects the independent motion of the dark component. For the adopted core configurations, increasing the dark fraction compresses the NM component, generally raises the mode frequencies, and reduces the maximum mass. The most pronounced spectral change is in the DM-led radiative lifetime. At 1 .4 M⊙, it decreases from 395–1317 s at FDM = 1% to 0 .7–1 .6 s at 20%, approximately following an empirical F −2DM dependence. NM-led damping varies much less strongly. Selected high-mass core sequences also develop pronounced damping-time upturns, showing that radiative efficiency cannot be inferred from frequency alone. Extended halos produce the opposite DM-led trend: increasing the dark fraction lowers the frequency and lengthens the radiative lifetime, while separating the NM surface from the outer dark radius. The canonical 20% halo models reach frequencies near 0 .14 kHz and damping times of several hundred seconds. At large dark fractions, the halo maximum masses increase, contrasting with the core sequences. The EOS1 core self-coupling scan shows greater sensi-tivity of the DM-led branch than of the NM-led branch, and the representative octupolar calculation retains the two-branch structure. These results motivate joint fre-quency and damping-time diagnostics. Both branches, however, depend on the coupled fluid and spacetime struc-ture; demonstrating independent constraints on the nu-clear EOS and dark sector requires a dedicated inference analysis. For the evaluated nonrotating circular binaries, no con-figuration satisfies all conditions for a controlled linear tidal resonance under the adopted applicability crite-ria. Halo and core DM-led resonances are pre-empted by Roche overflow or contact, while the remaining NM-led candidates fail the energy, timescale, or point-mass conditions. This bounds the tested resonant-inspiral chan-nel, not general detectability. Excitation and detector 17 modelling, together with rotating and finite-temperature extensions, are needed to assess other observational chan-nels. Tables I–II supply the isolated-mode inputs for that modelling, spanning roughly 0 .14–4 .9 kHz across the evaluated configurations.

> 2. Representative core-mode eigenfunctions

Fig. 7(a) shows the NM-led mode f O, with nodeless motion in both components. The NM displacement ex-tends through the normal-matter envelope, while the dark displacement terminates at the inner surface RDM . The dark core nevertheless participates dynamically: it is not simply a fixed contribution to the background gravita-tional field. This branch connects continuously to the ordinary NM f -mode as the dark fraction vanishes, while the finite dark component introduces an accompanying fluid response through the common metric perturbations. Panel (b) shows the distinct spatial structure of the DM-led mode f I. The dark displacement remains nodeless, but the NM displacement changes sign just outside RDM ,as highlighted by the inset. This node separates NM regions with opposite radial oscillation phases; it does not contradict the fundamental character of the branch, whose dominant dark-fluid displacement has no node. The NM response continues beyond the dark surface, illustrating that the dark-core oscillation is coupled to the surrounding normal matter rather than confined to an isolated bosonic object. Its higher frequency is consistent with the shorter dynamical timescale of the compact inner region, where dark-fluid pressure and the gravitational field generated by both components provide the restoring dynamics. The contrast between panels (a) and (b) therefore concerns the organization of motion throughout the two-fluid star, not merely a shift in the frequency of the same NM oscillation. Fig. 8 extends the comparison to a 1 .4 M⊙ core con-figuration and shows the fluid motion together with its spacetime response. In the top row, the NM component dominates both the radial and transverse displacements, while the dark core also participates within its own sur-face. The top-right metric profiles remain appreciable throughout the NM envelope and at its outer boundary, where the interior perturbation is matched to the outgoing Zerilli solution. The NM-led mode consequently retains a radiative damping time near 0 .30 s, characteristic of the ordinary neutron-star f -mode. The bottom row shows a different distribution of the perturbation. The radial and transverse motion is dom-inated by the compact dark component, with a much smaller NM response. The metric perturbations are largest in the inner region and decrease strongly across the NM envelope, becoming small at the outer surface. Thus, a mode can involve substantial internal fluid mo-tion while coupling only weakly to outgoing gravitational waves. The radiative calculation quantifies this contrast: the DM-led lifetime is approximately 1 .1 × 10 3 s, more than three orders of magnitude longer than that of the NM-led mode. For this DM-led solution, the opposing NM and DM contributions partially cancel in the leading matter-quadrupole diagnostic. The full lifetime, however, follows from the complete fluid–metric eigenmode and its exterior-wave matching. The shared metric therefore both couples the fluid motions and connects them to the radi-ation channel; these dynamical spacetime perturbations are omitted in the Cowling approximation.  

> 3. Dark-matter self-interaction

Fig. 9 shows that the sensitivity of the maximum mass to the dark self-coupling grows with the dark fraction. The four coupling choices give nearly coincident curves at FDM = 1%, whereas at 20%, increasing λ from 0 .5π

to 2 π raises Mmax from approximately 1 .76 to 2 .00 M⊙,recovering about 0 .25 M⊙. The canonical radius is much less sensitive: at 1 .4 M⊙, the same coupling variation increases RNM by only 0 .08 km even at 20%. Increasing the dark fraction nevertheless reduces both R1.4 and Mmax 

throughout this scan. This contrast follows from the repulsive bosonic EOS: increasing λ raises the pressure at fixed dark energy den-sity (Eq. (1)). At fixed total mass and dark fraction, the stronger repulsion supports a more extended dark core, re-distributing its gravitational influence on the normal fluid. This support acts within the dark component; the nor-mal matter responds through gravity, not direct pressure coupling. Consequently, stronger self-interaction partially offsets the reduction of the maximum mass caused by the dark core, with the largest effect occurring when the dark component carries a substantial fraction of the mass. This maximum-mass trend agrees with the bosonic survey of Karkevandi et al. [23]. λ lowers the resolved DM-led frequencies. For example, at 1 .4 M⊙ and FDM = 10%, increasing λ from 0 .5π to 2 π

Although stronger repulsion raises the dark pressure at fixed density, it also expands the dark core and changes its equilibrium profile (Fig. 9); the DM-led frequency de-creases along these readjusted configurations. Its restoring dynamics involve the dark-fluid pressure and the gravita-tional field generated by both components, rather than the dark mean density alone. The smaller NM-led shift is consistent with the weak response of RNM to λ at fixed mass. Thus, this scan identifies the DM-led branch as the more sensitive probe of the dark self-interaction, with-out implying that the NM-led mode is independent of dark-matter admixture. Fig. 11 shows that increasing λ generally lengthens the DM-led damping time, reversing the frequency ordering in Fig. 10. At 1 .4 M⊙ and FDM = 10%, increasing λ from 0.5π to 2 π raises τDM by a factor of approximately 2 .6. The canonical NM-led damping time is much less sensitive, varying by less than 1 .2% across the couplings at each fraction. Increasing FDM instead shortens the canonical DM-led lifetime by several hundredfold between 1% and 20% for the complete coupling sequences, consistent with the approximate inverse-square trend discussed in Sec-tion III.1. Even at 20%, the resolved canonical DM-led modes remain approximately 8–15 times longer-lived than their NM-led counterparts. Stronger self-repulsion expands the dark core and low-ers its mode frequency, while also changing the relative fluid motion and metric response. The leading quadrupole diagnostic, ( τ quad )−1 ∝ ω4|QN + QD|2/J , illustrates why a lower frequency favors weaker radiation at fixed nor-malized quadrupole strength; that strength, however, is not fixed across the scan. The full-GR damping time is determined by the complete coupled eigenfunction and its outgoing-wave matching, rather than by the frequency fac-tor alone. The high-mass upturns, especially pronounced at FDM = 1%, further illustrate this distinction: the ra-diative lifetime can increase sharply while the frequency changes only modestly. 

4. Higher-multipole ( ℓ = 3 ) modes 

Fig. 12 shows that the NM-led and DM-led families persist at ℓ = 3, with both branches lying above their quadrupolar counterparts. Increasing ℓ changes the an-gular restoring response of the coupled fluid–metric per-turbations without changing the equilibrium star. At fixed mass, both octupolar frequencies increase with FDM ,continuing the core trend of Fig. 2. Their multipolar spacing responds differently, however: at 1 .4 M⊙, increas-ing FDM from 1% to 20% decreases f3/f 2 from 1 .36 to 1.30 for the NM-led branch, but increases it from 1 .24 to 1 .28 for the DM-led branch. The two families there-fore cannot be related across multipoles by a common, fraction-independent factor. Both ratios lie below the Kelvin value p15 /7 ≃ 1.46 for a homogeneous, incom-pressible Newtonian fluid sphere; that limiting result does not capture the nonuniform, compressible two-fluid struc-ture considered here. The NM-led ℓ = 3 and DM-led 

ℓ = 2 frequency bands overlap. Since different ℓ sec-tors decouple in the spherical, nonrotating background, such frequency coincidences do not imply mode mixing. They nevertheless make a single observed frequency insuf-ficient to distinguish an NM-led octupole from a DM-led quadrupole without additional information about the star or the oscillation.
