ABSTRACT 

Pulsar glitches are thought to involve sudden unpinning and fast transport of large numbers of pinned vortices, possibly triggered by breaking of the neutron star crust solid. Such a vortex avalanche can take place on a fast timescale only if there are pathways for fast vortex motion. Formation of local vortex “traps” (regions containing larger than average density of pinned vortices) will provide vortex-free regions surrounding the traps, which can connect to form pathways for fast vortex motion. We propose a simple two-dimensional model for the neutron star crust superfluid, with the vortex pinning landscape tiled into traps and surrounding vortex-free regions which can connect to azimuthal pathways for fast vortex motion. The trap and tile sizes are estimated from the physics of the crust solid and vortex pinning, namely the critical strain angle of the crust solid and the critical velocity for vortex unpinning. Unpinned vortices move with the superfluid flow in the azimuthal direction while also scattering radially outward, by a small angle, due to mutual friction. The model qualitatively explains the glitch parameters and their evolution in young pulsars and provides an explanation for the bi-modality in the distribution of glitch magnitudes. Laboratory experiments and simulations on vortex systems with spatially inhomogeneous vortex pinning landscapes are likely to be more realistic models for pulsar glitches. Breaking the spatial scale-invariance and monitoring the results may also lead to better understanding of collective behaviour in pinned vortex systems in general. 

Key words: dense matter – stars: neutron – pulsars: general – stars: rotation 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Sudden unpinning of quantized vortices has been proposed as an explanation of pulsar glitches (Anderson & Itoh 1975; Alpar et al. 1984a). While this model has been successful in evaluation and in-terpretation of glitches and inter-glitch timing behaviour (Alpar et al. 1984a, 1989; Gügercinoğlu & Alpar 2020; Gügercinoğlu et al. 2022), a detailed understanding of how sudden vortex unpinning develops into an avalanche involving a large number of vortices has not been modelled (Haskell & Melatos 2015; Zhou et al. 2022; Antonopoulou et al. 2022; Antonelli et al. 2022). Laboratory experiments in terres-trial superfluids and Bose-Einstein condensates and computational models aiming to simulate pulsar glitches are also of intrinsic in-terest for understanding the collective behaviour of pinned vortex distributions in these systems. In pioneering experiments (Tsakadze & Tsakadze 1980), a container filled with superfluid 4He and with roughened inner surfaces of the container was set into rotation, to pro-vide pinning centres inhibiting smooth motion of quantised vortices in the superfluid. The system was subsequently spun down by an ex-ternal torque applied to the container, and the rotation and spin-down rates of the container were then monitored. Analogous experiments have been performed on vortex systems such as superfluid 4He and

2.3 Vortex Creep 

Pinned vortices can get unpinned and re-pinned by thermal activation against pinning potential wells and barriers (Alpar et al. 1984a, 1989). As the crust and normal matter are being spun down by the pulsar torque, vortex pinning leads to a lag 𝜔 ≡ Ωs − Ωc. There is a bias favouring radially outward vortex motions in the ˆ 𝑟 direction, which reduce the lag, compared to motion in the − ˆ𝑟 direction. This leads to a vortex current in the ˆ 𝑟 direction, achieved by the dissipative azimuthal drag forces on unpinned vortices. There is a steady-state of creep dynamics, in which the radial vortex motion allows the superfluid to spin-down at the same rate as the normal matter-crust, sustained by a steady-state value 𝜔 ∞ of the lag. 4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 

We have developed a simple model for the development of pulsar glitches as avalanches of vortex unpinning in the inner crust su-perfluid of neutron stars. Vortex unpinning is triggered in a vortex trap, which holds a high density of pinned vortices. Each trap is sur-rounded by vortex-free regions with high local superfluid velocity circulating around the trap. Vortices unpinned from any particular trap will move rapidly through the vortex-free regions, essentially in the azimuthal direction around the neutron star’s rotation axis, while also moving radially outward, at a much slower rate compared to their azimuthal motion with the superfluid flow. The radial motion is caused by the dissipative interactions of vortex lines with am-bient normal matter and energy transfer to vortex line excitations, azimuthal drag forces leading to radial vortex motion in accordance with the Magnus equation of motion for vortices. The scales of vortex traps and surrounding vortex-free regions are provided by two physical parameters: (i) The breaking strength of the crust solid: the neutron star crust solid is an unscreened Coulomb lattice due to the relativistic kinetic energies of the electrons. The dimensionless parameter that defines the critical strain of the solid, set by the fine structure constant and the nuclear charge 𝑍 , is likely to be of order 10 −1, as demonstrated by molecular dynamics simulations. (ii) The critical (maximum) difference between superfluid veloc-ity and the velocity of vortices pinned to the normal matter solid that can be sustained by the maximum available pinning force per unit length. Vortices unpin when this critical velocity difference is reached. Although the pinning energies and the distances between successive pinnings along a vortex line vary throughout the crust superfluid, typical pinning energies are of order 𝐸 p ∼ 1 Mev, and the distance 𝑏 between successive pinning sites is expected to be of order 𝑏 ∼ 100 fm. These physical scales determine the size of traps and the extra vortex density they sustain. The traps are formed by crust breaking events, which themselves are triggered by stresses arising from pin-ning forces. When a young neutron star cools down through the super-fluid phase transition, within a few hundred years of its formation (Ho et al. 2015), it will start with a spatially connected pinning landscape and a uniform vortex density (Sauls 2026). As more vortex traps are formed with time, the pinned superfluid becomes “tiled” into vortex traps and surrounding vortex-free regions to a progressively greater extent (Cheng et al. 1988). The sizes of vortex avalanches, the extent of the tiled pinning landscape through which the avalanche moves and therefore the glitch sizes will grow with a pulsar’s age, eventually reaching the full extent of the pinned crust superfluid, corresponding to a maximum glitch size (Alpar & Baykal 2006; Gügercinoğlu & Alpar 2020). Comparison of the observed glitch parameters of the Crab and Vela pulsars and PSR J0537–6910 shows that our avalanche model is in qualitative agreement with this evolutionary picture. The vortex avalanche moves in the radially outward direction by virtue of the dissipative forces acting on the free vortex lines, de-scribed by mutual friction parameters (Haskell & Melatos 2015). With the hypothesis that repeated glitches occur when similar con-ditions are reached, we make an estimate of the radial scattering parameter which is in agreement with current calculations of the mutual friction parameters. In addition, a lower bound on the ra-dial scattering parameter obtained from the best resolved limit on the glitch rise time in the Vela pulsar is also in agreement with the calculated mutual friction parameters. A vortex avalanche can proceed in a fast timescale only if there are pathways for fast vortex motion carried along with the super-fluid flow. This is not possible within vortex pinning regions where gradual radially outward vortex motion is realized diffusively and continuously in the vortex creep process, on the spin-down timescale dictated by the external torque. The scale-invariance in an extended homogeneous pinning landscape is broken if the scale-invariance in the spatial distribution of pinned vorticity is broken by the formation of local vortex traps surrounded by vortex-free regions which can connect to form pathways for fast vortex motion. The breaking of spatial space-invariance also leads to a qualitative understanding of the bimodality in the observed distribution of pulsar glitch magni-tudes. We conclude by emphasizing that the design of experiments and simulations on vortex systems to emulate pulsar glitches should in-clude spatially inhomogeneous vortex pinning landscapes. Breaking
