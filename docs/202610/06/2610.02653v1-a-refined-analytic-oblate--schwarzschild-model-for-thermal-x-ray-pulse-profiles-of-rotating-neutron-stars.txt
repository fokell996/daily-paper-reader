A Refined Analytic Oblate–Schwarzschild Model for Thermal X-Ray Pulse Profiles of Rotating Neutron Stars 

Xi Yang, 1 Tong Zhao, 1 and Renxin Xu 1

> 1School of Physics, Peking University, Beijing 100871, China

ABSTRACT Thermal X-ray pulse profiles constrain neutron-star masses and radii through the effects of gravita-tional light bending and rotation on surface emission. The precision anticipated from the enhanced X-ray Timing and Polarimetry (eXTP) mission motivates more accurate analytic light-bending formu-lae that retain the efficiency needed for repeated model evaluations. We derive a refined analytic flux expression within the oblate–Schwarzschild (OS) approximation that combines bending, propagation-delay, and lensing approximations with a spheroidal surface. We compare its profiles with numerical Schwarzschild and second-order Hartle–Thorne ray tracing on the same spheroidal surfaces. The com-parisons use a selected two-spot configuration at 300 Hz with isotropic blackbody emission and three choices for surface eccentricity: a freely specified value, the Maclaurin relation for a uniformly rotating Newtonian fluid, and a fit to rotating-neutron-star surfaces. Across these cases, the refined OS profiles have RMS flux differences of 1 .76 − 1.98% relative to the Hartle–Thorne reference, compared with 6.50 − 21 .39% for the spherical Schwarzschild-plus-Doppler (S+D) model. All differences are normal-ized to the phase-averaged reference flux. The corresponding differences between Schwarzschild and Hartle–Thorne ray tracing remain below 1 .1%. These comparisons separate errors in analytic photon transfer from the effects of surface deformation and the rotating exterior spacetime. For the configu-rations examined, accounting for oblateness substantially improves agreement with ray tracing. The model retains the speed of an analytic calculation while making the physical origin of profile changes explicit. 

Keywords: Millisecond pulsars; X-ray astronomy; Neutron stars 

1. INTRODUCTION Gravitational light bending connects radiation emit-ted near a compact object to the images, spectra, and light curves measured by a distant observer. In Schwarzschild spacetime, the light-bending equation re-lates the local emission angle α, measured from the radial direction, to the angle ψ between the emission radius vector and the observer’s direction. This rela-tion determines which emission directions reach the ob-server, while its angular derivative enters the lensing factor that converts an emitting area into an observed solid angle (K. R. Pechenick et al. 1983; A. M. Be-loborodov 2002; J. Poutanen 2020). Accurate evaluation of both quantities is therefore essential for predicting the observed flux from neutron-star surfaces and accretion disks around black holes. For neutron-star surfaces, this mapping is central to thermal X-ray pulse-profile modeling. Thermal radia-tion probes the surface temperature, atmospheric prop-erties, and internal structure of a neutron star (D. J. Helfand et al. 1980; V. E. Zavlin 2009). Localized hot regions can arise from heating of the magnetic polar caps by particles returning from the magnetosphere (A. K. Harding & A. G. Muslimov 2001), while a nonuniform surface temperature also produces rotational modula-tion in cooling stars (V. E. Zavlin 2009). Light bend-ing makes part of the far side of a neutron star visible and changes the pulse amplitude and shape. For broad, nearly isotropic emission, it generally reduces the mod-ulation (K. R. Pechenick et al. 1983; A. M. Beloborodov 2002). The resulting profiles depend on compactness, viewing geometry, and the angular distribution of the emitted intensity. Doppler shifts, aberration, and propa-gation delays provide additional sensitivity to the stellar radius and rotation (J. Poutanen & A. M. Beloborodov 2006). Combining profiles at different photon energies helps constrain these correlated parameters (D. Psaltis et al. 2014). Analyses of thermal pulsations from pulsars with the Neutron Star Interior Composition Explorer 

(NICER) have demonstrated the use of this approach arXiv:2610.02653v1 [astro-ph.HE] 2 Oct 2026 2 Yang and Zhao 

to infer neutron-star masses and radii and constrain the dense-matter equation of state (M. C. Miller et al. 2019; T. E. Riley et al. 2019). Pulse profiles can also constrain stellar properties when the emitting regions are produced by thermonu-clear burning or accretion. In models of X-ray burst oscillations, rotating brightness asymmetries generate oscillations near the stellar spin frequency. As in ther-mal polar-cap models, gravitational bending controls the visibility and apparent area of the emitting region, so the waveform carries information about compactness to-gether with the spot and observer inclinations. K. H. Lo et al. (2013) used simulated energy-resolved burst wave-forms to study the mass and radius constraints obtain-able from this information, including their dependence on geometry, photon counts, and background. M. C. Miller & F. K. Lamb (2015) extended such inference cal-culations to oblate stars, showing the importance of in-cluding rotational deformation in burst waveform mod-els. Light bending also enters models of persistent pul-sations from accreting millisecond pulsars, where mag-netically channeled accretion creates bright surface re-gions (J. Poutanen & A. M. Beloborodov 2006). The transfer of radiation from accretion disks around black holes involves a similar mapping between emis-sion and observation. For a disk around a Schwarzschild black hole, bent photon paths determine the apparent disk image, including the distorted view of its far side and secondary images (J.-P. Luminet 1979). Together with gravitational redshift and the Doppler effects of orbital motion, lensing determines the relative contribu-tions of different disk regions to the observed continuum and line spectra (C. T. Cunningham 1975; J. Poutanen 2020). J. Poutanen (2020) explicitly demonstrated the use of an analytic Schwarzschild bending approximation to calculate disk line profiles. For rotating black holes, the corresponding transfer calculation requires photon trajectories in Kerr spacetime, as in C. T. Cunningham (1975). Both surface-emission and disk-emission models thus rely on accurate photon transfer to connect source properties to observables. Using these models for parameter inference requires repeated ray tracing over extended emitting regions and photon energies, as well as rotational phase for pulse profiles. Even in Schwarzschild spacetime, the exact light bending must be evaluated by solving geodesic equations, or represented by interpolation tables. An-alytic approximations reduce this computational cost and make parameter dependencies explicit. A. M. Be-loborodov (2002) introduced a simple approximate re-lation between cos α and cos ψ, which underlies useful analytic descriptions of pulsar profiles (J. Poutanen & A. M. Beloborodov 2006). J. Poutanen (2020) subse-quently improved the accuracy of the bending relation and its associated lensing factor over a wider range of emission radii and observer angles. Because the ob-served flux depends on the angular derivative as well as the bending angle, the accuracy of both quantities must be assessed when using these approximations in waveform calculations. The need to control these approximation errors be-comes more pressing as observational precision im-proves. The higher photon statistics anticipated from the enhanced X-ray Timing and Polarimetry (eXTP) mission are expected to improve the precision of neutron-star mass and radius measurements through pulse-profile modeling (S.-N. Zhang et al. 2025). To ex-ploit this improvement, waveform models must keep er-rors from approximate photon propagation small enough that they do not limit the precision of the inferred pa-rameters. As statistical uncertainties decrease, resid-ual errors in the predicted pulse shape can introduce significant biases. Analytic models therefore need ac-curate light-bending and lensing formulae whose pre-dicted fluxes can be checked against numerical ray trac-ing, while remaining fast enough for repeated parameter inference. Meeting these accuracy requirements also calls for a consistent treatment of the emitting surface of a rotating neutron star. The Schwarzschild-plus-Doppler (S+D) approximation includes special-relativistic surface mo-tion but assumes a spherical star. Rotational oblateness changes the local emission radius, proper area, surface normal, and visibility of each emitting element. Ray-tracing studies show that these geometric effects can ex-ceed the corrections associated with the rotating exterior spacetime (C. Cadeau et al. 2007; D. Psaltis & F. ¨Ozel 2014). The oblate-Schwarzschild (OS) approximation captures much of this effect by placing an oblate surface in a Schwarzschild exterior (S. M. Morsink et al. 2007). Combining this geometry with accurate analytic bend-ing and lensing formulae provides a way to improve both surface and light-bending treatments within an efficient pulse-profile model. In this work, we derive a refined analytic OS model for thermal X-ray pulse profiles. We combine a spheroidal surface and its local normal with the bending and lensing approximations of J. Poutanen (2020), special-relativistic effects, finite emitting regions, and propa-gation delays. The observed flux is expressed in terms of redshift, Doppler, lensing, projected-area, and emis-sion factors. We compare the model with numerical Schwarzschild ray tracing on the same surface and with Hartle–Thorne ray tracing through second order in rota-Analytic OS Profiles 3tion (J. B. Hartle & K. S. Thorne 1968). Surface eccen-tricity is specified directly, calculated from the Maclau-rin relation for a uniformly rotating Newtonian fluid, or obtained from the neutron-star surface fit of S. M. Morsink et al. (2007). Empirical universal relations con-nect the stellar inputs to the angular momentum and quadrupole used in the Hartle–Thorne calculation (C. Breu & L. Rezzolla 2016; K. Yagi & N. Yunes 2013). Comparing these profiles separates the effects of surface deformation, approximate photon transfer, and the ro-tating exterior spacetime. Section 2 presents the flux calculation, oblate geome-try, photon transfer, and stellar parameter prescriptions. Section 3 compares the models for a two-spot configura-tion at 300 Hz under the three eccentricity prescriptions. Section 4 summarizes the conclusions.

Comparing S+D, refined OS, and S-ray with HT-ray separates the effects of surface shape, analytic photon propagation, and the rotating spacetime. In the 200 Hz, single-spot example of S. Bogdanov et al. (2019), the S+D and OS profiles nearly overlap in the profile panel, although the spherical model still has a measurable residual. In our selected 300 Hz, two-spot configura-tions, the spherical S+D curve instead separates clearly from refined OS and HT-ray, strongest for the manually specified eccentricity and weakest for the Morsink pre-scription (Figure 2). The refined OS curve follows the ray-traced profiles much more closely in all three cases. For the deliberately flattened e⋆ = 0 .35 surface, the di-rect S+D–refined-OS RMS difference reaches 20.7% of the phase-averaged HT-ray flux. Thus, the near-overlap in the 200 Hz example does not set a general upper limit on the effect of oblateness. The S+D RMS difference grows from 6.50% for the Morsink surface to 21.39% for the most oblate manual surface (Table 1), showing that the spherical approxima-tion becomes less reliable as the deformation increases. Refined OS remains within 2% RMS of HT-ray, while the S-ray–HT-ray difference remains below 1.1% RMS. The latter represents the rotating-spacetime correction; the remaining refined-OS–S-ray difference arises from the analytic photon-transfer approximation. 

4. CONCLUSIONS The near-overlap of S+D and OS profiles in the 200 Hz single-spot example of S. Bogdanov et al. (2019) is not generic. For the selected 300 Hz configurations, ne-glecting the latitude-dependent radius, proper surface element, oblate surface normal, and spot visibility pro-duces appreciable phase-dependent discrepancies. In-cluding these effects substantially improves agreement with ray tracing. By contrast, the smaller difference be-tween Schwarzschild and Hartle–Thorne ray tracing in-dicates that frame dragging and the exterior quadrupole are secondary corrections in the present cases. The main contribution of this work is a refined an-alytic OS model that captures these leading geomet-ric effects while retaining the speed and physical trans-parency of an analytic calculation. It provides an inter-mediate description between the spherical S+D approx-imation and full ray tracing, and is therefore suitable for rapid pulse-profile calculations and future fitting ap-plications. Extensions to realistic atmosphere emission and numerical rotating-star spacetimes will be needed for more complete parameter inference. APPENDIX 

A. INITIALLY INGOING RAYS Initially ingoing rays require a separate analytic treatment, which retains a geometric limitation. The oblate normal can direct an outward-emitted photon initially toward smaller Schwarzschild radius. This branch is absent from the simplest spherical outward-ray treatment. Our analytic calculation uses the first-order small-ingoing-angle correction of S. M. Morsink et al. (2007) for the bending and propagation delay, matched continuously to the outgoing analytic 8 Yang and Zhao 

branch. The current implementation applies this local branch only when the surface-normal visibility test admits the photon. It does not yet trace the full path back against the oblate surface; a global re-intersection rejection test is therefore retained as a limitation. 

B. NUMERICAL CONVERGENCE AND REPRODUCIBILITY Resolution checks on nested grids assess whether image-plane discretization affects the reported residuals. All calculations use 64 phase bins, the same adaptive image-plane window for S-ray and HT-ray, exactly nested nodes, and 8 × 8 subpixel coverage at hot-spot boundaries. For the manual and Maclaurin prescriptions, the 193 2–385 2 checks give maximum HT-ray profile changes of 0.481% and 0.470%, and maximum changes of the HT-referenced residuals of 0.492 and 0.476 percentage points. For the Morsink prescription, the 385 2–769 2 check gives 0.162% and 0.275 percentage points, respectively. Changes in the reported maximum residual remain below 0.12 percentage point in every case. 

C. HARTLE–THORNE INTERNAL VALIDATION Independent tests of the HT metric and its derivatives complement the profile comparisons. Metric tests give maximum inverse-identity and analytic versus finite-difference Christoffel errors of 6 .94 × 10 −18 and 8 .52 × 10 −11 .The Schwarzschild, linear-in-J, spin-squared, independent-quadrupole, and asymptotic component tests all pass; Ap-pendix B supplies the end-to-end resolution test.
