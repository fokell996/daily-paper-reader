A Physically Motivated Compact Parameterization of Neutron Star Atmosphere Emission for Thermal X-ray Pulse-Profile Modeling     

> TONG ZHAO 1AND RENXIN XU11School of Physics, Peking University, Beijing 100871, China

ABSTRACT Thermal X-ray pulse-profile modeling depends on the angular and spectral properties of neutron star atmo-sphere emission. We construct a compact empirical approximation to the specific intensity using eight co-efficients for the angular dependence and five for a normalized spectral kernel. The angular prescription is motivated by the formal solution of the radiative-transfer equation, while the spectral normalization preserves a specified bolometric flux. We calibrate this family to numerical atmosphere tables, focusing on return-current-heated and fully ionized hydrogen atmospheres. When incorporated into a semi-analytic pulse-profile model, the approximation reproduces profiles normalized by their phase-averaged flux to within approximately 4% for antipodal hot spots and 5% for non-antipodal hot spots in the tested heated-atmosphere configurations. We also analyze synthetic eXTP observations using different beaming prescriptions to test the conclusion in our previous work based on a linear description of the angular dependence of the emission. The comparisons suggest that the angular dependence of the intensity cannot be simply characterized by a linear function of the cosine value of the emission angle, because its detailed shape and energy dependence will affect the parameter inference. This parameterization can significantly increase the efficiency for analytic and numerical models, and provide a compact framework for examining how atmosphere assumptions affect neutron star pulse profiles and the inferred stellar properties.  

> 1.

INTRODUCTION Measurements of neutron star masses and radii provide a means of constraining the equation of state of matter at densi-ties beyond those accessible in terrestrial experiments. Ther-mal X-ray pulse-profile modeling offers a particularly valu-able approach because the observed modulation carries infor-mation about both the stellar spacetime and the emitting sur-face. As localized hot regions rotate through the observer’s line of sight, gravitational light bending, gravitational red-shift, Doppler boosting, and aberration modify the amplitude, shape, and energy dependence of the pulse profile. These ef-fects depend on the stellar mass and radius, but also on the geometry of the emitting regions and the angular distribu-tion of the surface radiation. Extracting reliable constraints on neutron star structure therefore requires a consistent de-scription of both photon propagation and the local emission process (Beloborodov 2002; Poutanen & Beloborodov 2006; Watts et al. 2016). Observations with the Neutron Star Interior Composition Explorer (NICER) have demonstrated the potential of this technique for rotation-powered millisecond pulsars. Analy-ses of PSR J0030+0451 yielded joint constraints on its mass, radius, and surface hot-region geometry, establishing ther-mal pulse-profile modeling as a practical probe of neutron star structure(Miller et al. 2019; Riley et al. 2019). Subse-quent analyses of the massive pulsar PSR J0740+6620 ex-tended these measurements to a substantially different mass regime(Miller et al. 2021; Riley et al. 2021; Choudhury et al. 2024; Salmi et al. 2024a,b; Dittmann et al. 2024; Mauviard et al. 2025). These results motivate continued examination of the physical assumptions entering the forward model, par-ticularly those that can change the inferred stellar parameters without being independently constrained by the observations. An essential ingredient of the forward model is the spe-cific intensity emerging from the neutron star atmosphere. Unlike isotropic blackbody emission, atmospheric radiation generally has a coupled dependence on photon energy and emission angle. The angular emission pattern of the at-mosphere is consequently a physical component of the in-ferred pulse shape, rather than merely a spectrum correc-tion. In commonly adopted atmosphere models, this depen-dence is specified by the effective temperature, surface grav-ity and magnetic field, and assumed atmospheric composi-tion. Many NICER analyses have employed nonmagnetic, fully ionized hydrogen atmospheres in which the heat sup-plying the outgoing radiation is deposited beneath the lay-ers responsible for the emergent spectrum (Ho & Lai 2001; Haakonsen et al. 2012). For rotation-powered millisecond pulsars, however, the hot surface regions are thought to be sustained by the bombardment of charged particles return-ing from the magnetosphere. Whether a deep-heated atmo-sphere adequately describes this situation depends on where these particles deposit their energy. Bauböck et al. (2019) showed that the atmospheric structure and radiation pattern depend on the properties of the incident particles and their stopping depths. Salmi et al. (2020) subsequently calculated return-current-heated atmospheres including particle energy deposition and Compton scattering. Their models demon-strated that sufficiently energetic particles can produce emis-sion resembling the deep-heated limit, whereas shallower de-position can generate a hot surface layer and modify both the arXiv:2610.05720v1 [astro-ph.HE] 5 Oct 2026 2spectrum and its angular distribution. Depending on the heat-ing conditions and photon energy, the emerging radiation can depart substantially from the usual limb-darkened pattern and can exhibit limb brightening. The atmospheric angular emission pattern also enters mod-els of thermonuclear (Type I) X-ray bursts from accreting neutron stars. In burst-oscillation models, a rotating surface brightness asymmetry produces an energy-dependent wave-form whose amplitude and harmonic content depend on the local angular emission pattern, making the beaming prescrip-tion relevant to mass-radius inference (Lo et al. 2013; Miller & Lamb 2015). Hot burst atmospheres are often dominated by electron scattering, motivating the use of the correspond-ing limb-darkening law in waveform calculations. Numer-ical atmosphere models nevertheless show departures from this law that depend on photon energy and composition, par-ticularly away from the spectral peak and near absorption edges (Suleimanov et al. 2012). The angular emission pat-tern also matters when integrating the emission over a rapidly rotating, oblate star: the angular weighting of surface ele-ments affects the observed flux and spectral normalization, and thus enters extensions of the burst cooling-tail method that account for rotation (Suleimanov et al. 2020). However, the specific intensity is calculated using radiative-transfer equations and incorporated into pulse-profile calculations through precomputed tables. Repeated table lookup and interpolation can be computationally ex-pensive. This cost becomes particularly important in GPU-accelerated pulse-profile calculations that reduces a single likelihood evaluation from seconds minutes to millisecond, because the computation of intensity takes up the majority of the computation time (Zhou & Huang 2026). The widespread use of atmosphere models therefore motivates compact rep-resentations of the coupled angular and spectral intensity val-ues. In this work, we construct a physically motivated parame-terized representations of the emergent specific intensity and its angular dependence, which can be calibrated to differ-ent atmosphere models, especially for return-current-heated models with different physics parameters. We assess how ac-curately parameterized beaming functions can reproduce nu-merical atmosphere calculations and incorporate these func-tions into a semi-analytic pulse-profile model. We then gen-erate synthetic eXTP-SFA observations and analyze them un-der different assumptions about the surface emission. By comparing the recovered parameters with their injected val-ues, we examine how an incorrect beaming prescription af-fects neutron star compactness, viewing geometry, and hot-region properties, and assess the implications for mass-radius inference. This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces our physically motivated parameterization of the angular and spectral dependence of the emergent intensity. Section 3 presents its calibration to numerical hydrogen atmosphere ta-bles and evaluates the approximation errors. Section 4 com-pares pulse profiles calculated using the parameterized inten-sities with those obtained from numerical tables. Section 5 uses synthetic eXTP-SFA observations to investigate how an incorrect beaming prescription affects the inferred stellar and hot-spot parameters. Section 6 summarizes our findings and discusses the limitations of the model. Additional calibra-tion results for helium and partially ionized hydrogen atmo-spheres are presented in the Appendix.

6. SUMMARY The proposed family separates a normalized angular dis-tribution from a compact spectral description. The formal transfer solution motivates the angular form, and the spectral kernel admits an analytic normalization. This makes the roles of angular beaming and spectral shape explicit with thirteen coefficients per atmosphere. The fitted exponents and their dependence on x, z, and G remain empirical. The construc-tion is therefore a calibrated representation of numerical in-tensities, rather than an independent atmosphere calculation. There are several physical limits to the present representa-tion. For K, p > 0, the angular law is monotonic and cannot describe limb brightening or an angular turnover. Grazing-angle errors can be large even when the contribution to a 10 particular pulse profile is small. That contribution must be checked for the actual emitting geometry, especially when only one spot dominates. In addition, the fits cover the re-tained table points rather than the full range of possible at-mosphere parameters. The larger residuals for partially ion-ized hydrogen in the Appendix show that the same compact family does not provide uniform accuracy across composi-tions and ionization states. Applications requiring smaller errors may need a restricted calibration domain, a more flex-ible functional form, or direct numerical intensities. Our final section demonstrates that the effect of the angular emission pattern on pulse-profile parameter inference is non-trivial. The effect cannot be characterized solely by the over-all strength of the angular anisotropy. It also depends on the detailed shape and energy dependence of the angular emis-sion pattern and on its correlations with the stellar and geo-metrical parameters. A more comprehensive assessment will therefore require exploration of a broader parameter space, including more complex spot geometries and additional at-mosphere models. The broad residual structure in the partially ionized maps indicates a limitation of the adopted global functional form. The free-free scaling used to motivate the coordinates does not by itself describe the additional opacity and ionization dependence of this atmosphere. However, choosing a smaller domain within the numerical table can decrease the fitting error. Especially, our global functional form fails when fitted to the carbon atmosphere, because it has absorption edges and the beaming behavior is also completely different. We believe that our global functional can also fit well to different heated atmosphere models given in Salmi et al. (2020). However, the data is not available. Spectral and angular residuals can reinforce or partly cancel at a given point. Their separate global maxima need not occur at the same atmospheric parameters or emission angle, so they cannot simply be added to obtain the combined maximum. The errors reported here characterize reconstruction on the retained calibration grids.
