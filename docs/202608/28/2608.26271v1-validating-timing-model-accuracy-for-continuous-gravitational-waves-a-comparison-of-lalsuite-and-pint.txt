We present results of a systematic validation of the LALSuite timing model for continuous gravitational waves against Pint , a modern high-accuracy pulsar-timing package. An accurate timing model is essential for tracking the signal phase, and hence for detecting and accurately characterizing continuous gravitational waves. In order to quantify the impact of timing inaccuracies, we derive and validate the leading-order relation μ ≈ (2 πf )2σ2 

> δτ

, where μ is the fractional loss of signal power, f is the signal frequency, and σ2 

> δτ

is the variance of the timing errors. We then compare the solar-system and binary components of the LALSuite timing model against the corresponding models in Pint . With the original LALSuite Einstein-delay implementation, the total disagreement is dominated by that component and has σδτ ≃ 2.3 μs (corresponding to μ ≃ 0.02 % at f = 1000 Hz). With the newer Einstein-delay implementation, the total disagreement (over one year) drops to

We additionally test binary delays using orbital parameters from 474 catalogued binary pulsars and verify the self-consistency of the LALSuite source-time derivatives. Finally, we derive and discuss the LALSuite Shapiro delay for signals passing through the solar interior, a case only relevant to gravitational waves. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Continuous gravitational waves (or continuous waves 

for short) are long-lasting, quasi-monochromatic signals. The amplitudes of such continuous waves are several or-ders of magnitude lower than those of the typical tran-sient signals from the merger of compact binaries that current ground-based detectors routinely observe [1]. Searches for continuous waves have been an area of active research for the past two decades, e.g., see recent examples of targeted searches [2, 3], directed searches [4, 5] and all-sky searches [6, 7], as well as the reviews [8, 9]. The first detection of continuous waves will be a milestone event, unlocking gravitational-wave astronomy beyond the transient sky and opening a new window into the extreme-matter regime inside neutron stars [10]. The primary sources for ground-based detectors are expected to be rapidly rotating neutron stars with de-viations from perfect axisymmetry (e.g., see [11, 12] and references therein). Despite steady improvements in search breadth and depth, with current searches in-creasingly probing plausible neutron-star deformations, no continuous-wave signal has yet been detected. While the absence of a detection is consistent with pre-dicted source populations lying below current sensitivity levels (e.g., [13]), it is important to examine and validate all elements of our search methods and implementations. In this paper we focus on one particularly critical ele-ment: the timing model .The timing model relates detector-frame arrival times to effective source-frame times by accounting for the relevant time-dependent delays. Long integrations re-quire accurate tracking of the signal phase, while a time-dependent timing error produces a phase error (propor-tional to signal frequency). Timing accuracy is therefore essential for detecting and accurately characterizing such signals. Every continuous-wave search method relies on a tim-ing model, and many of the current search pipelines use the timing code in LALSuite [14]. Precision pulsar tim-ing relies on the same barycentric and binary time-delay models, with accuracy requirements at least as stringent as those of continuous-wave searches. This is why we have chosen Pint [15], a modern Python-based pulsar-timing package, as an independent high-accuracy refer-ence implementation for our comparison. The most recent published validations of the 

LALSuite timing-model implementation (against 

tempo [16]) date back approximately two decades, namely [17] (Fig. 2), and [18] (Fig. 6) for binary sources. While there have been internal spot-checks since then, in light of the central importance of this part of the search codes, it is timely and necessary to perform and document a systematic validation study. The plan of this paper is as follows: in Sec. II we in-troduce the full continuous-wave timing model, and in Sec. III we derive and validate an expression linking tim-ing errors to loss of signal power. In Sec. IV we perform numerical timing-model comparisons against Pint , and Sec. V gives our conclusions. In Appendix A we derive the Shapiro delay for signals passing through the Sun. arXiv:2608.26271v1 [gr-qc] 26 Aug 2026 2

> 1. Solar-system Rømer delay

The solar-system Rømer delay ∆ Rømer refers to the purely geometric (flat space-time) delay between a wave-front reaching the SSB (at tSSB ) and arriving at the de-tector at time t, therefore ∆Rømer ≡ t − tSSB (t) = −⃗r(t) · ˆnc , (10) where ⃗r(t) is the instantaneous position of the detector with respect to the SSB, and we are neglecting finite-distance parallax effects. We can write the detector position as ⃗r =⃗r geo +⃗ robs ,in terms of the Earth geocenter ⃗ r geo and the relative detector-position offset ⃗ robs , separating ∆ Rømer into cor-responding geocentric- and observatory contributions, i.e., ∆ Rømer = ∆ Rømer ,geo + ∆ Rømer ,obs .The geocentric position ⃗r geo (t) in the SSB is computed from the JPL solar-system ephemerides [25], which are provided as Chebyshev polynomial fits. These are first converted 2 to ephemeris files holding time-sampled po-sition, velocity and acceleration tables for the Earth, sampled every 2 hours (and for the Sun, sampled every 20 hours). From these the instantaneous position of the Earth ⃗r geo (t) (and Sun ⃗r ⊙(t)) can be computed by ex-trapolation from the closest sample point. To compute the observatory offset ⃗ robs (t) for a detec-tor, the rotational state of the Earth at time t is approx-imated using an analytical Earth-rotation model [27], which also takes leap seconds into account. Note, how-ever, that the pulsar-astronomy codes tempo2 and Pint 

use the more accurate empirical IERS Earth orientation data [28] instead.

> 3. Solar-system Einstein delay

The Einstein delay ∆ Einstein refers to the time dilation experienced by terrestrial clocks due to the Earth’s or-bital motion and the local gravitational potential (domi-nated by the Sun) with respect to an SSB time standard such as Barycentric Coordinate Time (TCB) or Barycen-tric Dynamical Time (TDB) [30, 31]. Here we focus ex-clusively on TDB as the barycentric time standard, which is the default (and better supported) by both LALSuite 

and Pint , so the corresponding Einstein delay is defined as ∆Einstein ≡ tTT − tTDB , (14) where tTT is the time coordinate of an event (such as a wavefront arriving at the detector) measured in the terrestrial-time (TT) standard, while tTDB is the barycentric (TDB) time coordinate of the same event. The Einstein delay therefore only depends on the rel-ative position and velocity of the detector in the solar system, and is independent of the signal parameters. We can separate ∆ Einstein into two contributions, sim-ilarly to Rømer-delay, namely ∆ Einstein = ∆ Einstein ,geo +4∆Einstein ,obs , where ∆Einstein ,geo is the Einstein de-lay experienced at the center of the Earth ⃗ r geo , and ∆Einstein ,obs = −⃗robs · ˙⃗r geo /c 2 ∼ O (2 μs) is the correction due to the offset ⃗ robs of the detector from the geocenter (cf. [20, 32] and implementations in tempo2 , Pint and 

Astropy [33]). 

LALSuite has two different implementations of ∆Einstein :

• the original XLALBarycenterEarth (C. Cutler (2001)), which computes ∆Einstein ,geo using the first 20 terms (of about 800 used by tempo [16]) from an analytical series-expansion approximation [34], and which neglects the observatory correction ∆Einstein ,obs .

• the newer XLALBarycenterEarthNew (M. Pitkin (2012)), which interpolates ∆Einstein ,geo from numerically-computed [35] tempo2 time-ephemeris files, and which includes 3 the ob-servatory contribution ∆ Einstein ,obs .We note that LALSuite always uses the idealized TT(TAI) = TAI + 32 .184 s realization of the TT time standard (where TAI = GPS + 19 s), while Pint can op-tionally use the more accurate TT(BIPM) realization 4,which is empirically corrected for long-term drifts in the atomic-time standard TAI.

V. CONCLUSIONS 

We have performed a systematic validation of the 

LALSuite timing model for continuous gravitational waves, using Pint as an independent high-accuracy ref-erence implementation, comparing the individual solar-system and binary time-delay components. To quantify the impact of timing inaccuracies on searches, we first derived and numerically validated the leading-order rela-tion Eq. (40) between the mismatch μ and the variance 

σ2 

> δτ

of the timing errors. With the original Einstein-delay implementation 

XLALBarycenterEarth , total timing disagreement is dominated by that component and has σδτ ≃ 2.3 μs, corresponding to a mismatch of only μ ≃ 0.02 % at a frequency of f = 1000 Hz, well within accuracy require-ments for continuous-wave searches. With the newer Einstein-delay XLALBarycenterEarthNew , the total dis-agreement drops to σδτ <

∼ 31 ns, corresponding to μ <

∼

4 × 10 −8 at 1000 Hz, see Sec. IV for details. We tested the LALSuite binary-delay implementation using or-bital parameters of 474 binary pulsars from the ATNF catalog and found consistently small disagreements with 

Pint ’s DD BT model of order σBinary  

> δτ

<

∼ O (ns). We also verified the self-consistency of the independently com-puted LALSuite source-time derivatives to absolute er-rors of <

∼ 3.7 × 10 −11 s/s. 12 Finally, we derived LALSuite ’s solar-interior Shapiro-delay expression and compared its underlying density model with a realistic solar model. While this approxima-tion regularizes the Shapiro delay at the solar center, it underestimates the solar-interior delay by up to ∼ 14 .5 μsnear the center. These results validate the LALSuite timing model for the tested continuous-wave search configurations, and provide quantitative information about current accuracy bottlenecks for future improvements, should they ever be needed.
