ABSTRACT The presence of hyperons in the cores of neutron stars opens fast strangeness equilibration channels that can produce bulk-viscous dissipation during binary inspiral. Because these reactions coexist with electron β-equilibration, tidal compression can drive the two coupled chemical imbalances far beyond linear response. We construct the first reaction network that self-consistently evolves the electron and strangeness fractions with a four-dimensional strangeness-dependent chiral mean-field (CMF) equation of state, including nucleonic and hyperonic Urca processes and non-leptonic hyperon reactions. For periodic density perturbations, representative of inspiral oscillations, we find that rapid strangeness conversion can generate a large β-imbalance, after which slow β-equilibration bottlenecks strangeness relaxation. Rather than decaying exponentially, the coupled system consequently exhibits dynamically important algebraic decay in a far-from-equilibrium regime. At the keV temperatures expected during inspiral, this nonlinear response produces a broad enhancement of the effective bulk viscosity, reaching 

∼ 10 31 g cm −1 s−1 for 320 Hz oscillations. A phenomenological estimate of continuous inspiral dissi-pation yields gravitational-wave phase shifts up to ∼ 0.14 rad for neutron stars with hyperonic cores. Self-consistent, far-from-equilibrium strangeness transport may therefore provide a dynamical probe of hyperons in neutron-star interiors.

1. INTRODUCTION Probing extreme states of matter with neutron stars is currently one of the fundamental goals of physics and astrophysics. Their extreme densities beyond nu-clear saturation and high compactness make them ideal probes for using macroscopic properties of stellar struc-ture to constrain the nuclear matter conditions inside them (J. M. Lattimer & M. Prakash 2001). In partic-ular, physicists are interested in the possibility of ex-otic particles and states of matter that may exist within their cores, such as strange particles like hyperons (A. Gal et al. 2016; L. Tolos & L. Fabbietti 2020). Nuclear physics experiments and chiral effective field theory pro-

Tsang et al. 2024), and perturbative quantum chromodynamics provides constraints at den-sities 1-2 orders of magnitude larger than the maximum central densities (O. Komoltsev & A. Several avenues exist to constrain bulk properties of neutron stars, e.g., through pulsar timing (J. Antoniadis et al. 2013; H. T. Cromartie et al. 2019; E. Fonseca et al. 2021), X-ray emissions from hot spots due to magneto-spheric return currents (A. L. Watts et al. 2016; F. ¨Ozel & P. Freire 2016; M. C. Miller et al. 2019, 2021; T. E. Riley et al. 2019, 2021), and gravitational wave obser-vations (E. E. Flanagan & T. Hinderer 2008; T. Hin-arXiv:2608.15527v1 [astro-ph.HE] 16 Aug 2026 2derer et al. 2016; K. Chatziioannou 2022; P. Hammond et al. 2026), which when combined together can provide strong constraints on the underlying equation of state (EOS) (see, e.g., R. Kumar et al. (2024) for a recent review). The first observation of the gravitational wave event GW170817 of two merging neutron stars has already led to several constraints on the dense matter EOS via imprints of the adiabatic tidal deformability on the last orbits of the inspiral (e.g., V. Dexheimer et al. (2019); K. Chatziioannou & S. Han (2020); K. Chatziioannou et al. (2018); B. P. Abbott et al. (2018); E. R. Most et al. (2018); E. Annala et al. (2018); C. Raithel et al. (2018)). Additional, more model dependent constraints can be inferred by assuming the outcome of the merger remnant (L. Baiotti & L. Rezzolla 2017), and using that to constrain the maximum mass of neutron stars (B. Margalit & B. D. Metzger 2017; L. Rezzolla et al. 2018; M. Ruiz et al. 2018; M. Shibata et al. 2019). Additional constraints can be derived using the second (and more massive) neutron star merger event GW190425 (B. P. Abbott et al. 2020) and its potential close connection to prompt black hole formation (A. Bauswein et al. 2020; R. Kashyap et al. 2022; S. D. Tootle et al. 2021). While useful, these constraints cannot yet confirm or rule out specific internal compositions of the core (D. Mroczek et al. 2024). This is because there are intrinsic degeneracies between the EOS and the tidal deforma-bility (R. Gamba et al. 2020; I. Tews et al. 2018; I. Legred et al. 2024; R. Essick et al. 2023; C. A. Raithel & E. R. Most 2023a,b) (though there is strong quasi-universality, e.g., K. Yagi & N. Yunes (2013a,b, 2016); D. A. Godzieba et al. (2021)), and between different nu-clear compositions entering the EOS and the resulting observable bulk properties. This means that even with next-generation facilities, fundamental questions such as the presence of a deconfinement phase transition (M. G. Alford et al. 2013; K. Chatziioannou & S. Han 2020) cannot for all models be determined uniquely from ob-servations of the tidal deformability alone (C. A. Raithel & E. R. Most 2023a; R. Essick et al. 2023; A. Clevinger et al. 2026). Consequently, other potential non-adiabatic imprints have been proposed, in particular associated with mi-crophysical dissipation. Matter inside the neutron star is subject to both strong and weak interactions (S. L. Shapiro & S. A. Teukolsky 1983; D. G. Yakovlev et al. 2001). Akin to a chemical equilibrium system, Urca pro-cesses (G. Gamow & M. Schoenberg 1941; J. M. Lat-timer et al. 1991) regulate the relative lepton fraction so that matter tends towards weak-interaction equilib-rium. However, because of compression present during the inspiral, and in particular during resonances, e.g., for the g-mode (A. Reisenegger & P. Goldreich 1992, 1994; D. Lai 1994; R. Counsell et al. 2024; N. Ander-sson et al. 2026; T. Zhao et al. 2025; S. Ghosh et al. 2026), matter can be driven out of chemical equilibrium (A. Reisenegger & P. Goldreich 1992). For matter con-taining strangeness, e.g., hyperons (J. Schaffner & I. N. Mishustin 1996), chemical equilibration not only affects the lepton chemical potential, but also the strangeness one. The impact of chemical equilibration is that any multi-component system driven back to equilibrium will behave as an effective viscous fluid (L. Gavassino 2023; J. L. Hern´ andez et al. 2026). Viscosity here is to be interpreted in a far-from-equilibrium sense, where the bulk scalar pressure can be large and have a (transient) non-zero equilibrium value (L. Gavassino & J. Noronha 2024). Depending on the effective phase lag of the driv-ing and the amplitude of the oscillations, bulk viscos-ity from the system can become very large (Y. Yang et al. 2024), and potentially affect the inspiral dynam-ics (M. G. Alford et al. 2018; E. R. Most et al. 2021; J. L. Ripley et al. 2023). For npe matter, both the bulk viscosity and its relaxation time can depend sensitively on the nuclear symmetry energy, with variations of its slope changing the viscosity by orders of magnitude (Y. Yang et al. 2025). While this phenomenon has been analyzed using dif-ferent approaches, including Navier-Stokes hydrody-namics (T. Celora et al. 2022) or relativistic first-order hydrodynamics (J. L. Ripley et al. 2023; A. Hegade K. R. et al. 2024a), the general conclusion for leptonic dissipa-tion is that the bulk viscosity may be too small to leave a dynamical impact on the gravitational wave phase in the inspiral itself (M. G. Alford et al. 2023; A. Hegade K. R. et al. 2026). However, when strangeness equilibration is included, in particular in the form of hyperonic mat-ter, the bulk viscosity can be orders of magnitude larger when temperatures are substantially below 1 MeV (P. Arras & N. N. Weinberg 2019). Therefore, it has been argued that exotic phases of matter might be detectable with next-generation gravitational wave facilities (H. Yu & N. N. Weinberg 2017; M. G. Alford & A. Haber 2021; S. Ghosh et al. 2024), especially if oscillations of the neu-tron star are resonantly excited, e.g., at the g-mode or f-mode resonance (A. Reisenegger & P. Goldreich 1994; D. Lai 1994; S. Ghosh et al. 2026). Because hyperonic matter seems to be the most promising channel for producing dynamically important bulk viscosity in the inspiral, self-consistent calculations and the inclusion of all coupled reactions are crucial. For instance, a single decay in isolation is not the same as a coupled, fully non-linear reaction network that is al-3lowed to be far-from-equilibrium, which can significantly change equilibration times (see e.g. J. Noronha-Hostler et al. (2008, 2010)). Previous studies of weak-interaction dissipation in dense matter have largely calculated an effective bulk viscosity from perturbations about chem-ical equilibrium, without evolving the underlying com-position on a dynamical binary-merger background. For hyperonic matter, these calculations generally linearize the non-leptonic rates (M. E. Gusakov & E. M. Kan-tor 2008; D. Ofengeim et al. 2019; M. G. Alford & A. Haber 2021). More recent work has incorporated this linearized hyperonic relaxation into stellar-mode and tidal perturbation calculations (S. Ghosh et al. 2026), but still assumes a static stellar background and does not evolve the coupled β and strangeness compositions. The closest reaction-network precedent (similar to what we will perform here) instead comes from quark mat-ter, where coupled non-leptonic and semi-leptonic reac-tions were treated first in linear response (B. A. Sa’d et al. 2007; J. L. Hern´ andez et al. 2026) and subse-quently with nonlinear reaction rates (I. A. Shovkovy & X. Wang 2011). However, these quark matter reaction network calculations derived the bulk-viscous response of homogeneous matter under prescribed perturbations or near-equilibrium evolution, also not taking into ac-count oscillation conditions extracted from dynamical numerical-relativity simulations. Despite this progress, hyperonic bulk viscosity has not yet been studied using a self-consistent, coupled reaction network in the far-from-equilibrium regime relevant to binary inspiral. As we will show here, the inspiral phase probes exactly this far-from-equilibrium regime, which is not properly described by simple bulk viscosity as-sumptions. Additionally, since both leptonic and non-leptonic rates can simultaneously convert strangeness, a fully self-consistent dynamical equilibration process by means of a full, coupled chemical reaction network is needed. Here, we address this by presenting the first fully cou-pled strangeness reaction network for use in neutron star merger calculations, enabled by the new MUSES EOS framework that allows us to fully self-consistently model strangeness dependence (J. Jahan et al. 2026; M. R. Pelicer et al. 2025). We describe the overall aim and summary of our results in Sec. 2. We then provide de-tails on our methodology in Sec. 3, before providing a detailed discussion of our results in Sec. 4.

At very low temper-atures, nucleonic direct Urca reactions are only possible at proton fractions Yp ≳ 0.11 due to (Fermi) energy and momentum conservation in npe matter, resulting in a minimum density threshold at which direct Urca begins to equilibrate the matter (J. In the low-temperature multicomponent matter consid-ered below, only states near the Fermi surfaces are as-sumed to contribute to the reactions (see Appendix C) and thermal contributions, which are very small com-pared to the modified Urca channels at the considered temperatures, are neglected. Hyperonic matter instead requires the simulta-neous evolution of Ye and YS , with β- and strangeness equilibration, which we formulate here. 2.1. Impact on the inspiral 

During the inspiral of a binary neutron star merger, each star induces a tidal bulge in the companion. This tidal deformation drives fluid motion in the star, which can be damped by dissipative processes such as bulk or shear viscosity. This effect can siphon orbital energy into the fluid motion and subsequent heating of the star, resulting in a phase shift of the orbit compared to the non-dissipative case (L. Bildsten & C. Cutler 1992; J. L. Ripley et al. 2023; S. Ghosh et al. 2025). In particular, the tidal forcing can resonantly excite neutron star g-mode oscillations that enhance the dissi-pative response (A. Hegade K. R. et al. 2026) and impart a sudden phase shift, which could be detectable down to ∆Ψ ≃ 0.03 rad for next-generation detectors (R. Coun-sell et al. 2024; F. Gittins et al. 2026). To solve Equation (14), an out-of-β-and-strangeness-equilibrium EOS depending on the energy density, baryon number density, and composition is required. We use the chiral mean-field (CMF) model computed with the CMF++ code N. Cruz-Camacho et al. (2025). The CMF model is an effective relativistic mean-field model based on a nonlinear realization of the SU (3) sigma model with chiral symmetry restoration (S. Weinberg 1968; P. Papazoglou 1999; V. Dexheimer & S. Schramm 2008; N. Cruz-Camacho et al. 2025). The model is consistent with both nuclear (P. Papazoglou 1999; V. Dexheimer & S. Schramm 2008) and astrophysical con-straints (V. Dexheimer & S. Schramm 2008; V. Dex-heimer et al. 2019, 2015) and is compatible with results from lattice (V. A. Dexheimer & S. Schramm 2010) and perturbative (J. Roark & V. Dexheimer 2018) quantum chromodynamics. Details of the model and parameters can be found in N. Cruz-Camacho et al. (2025). In this work we make use of the C4 parametrization including the full baryon octet ( n, p, Λ, Σ −, Σ 0, Σ +,Ξ−, Ξ 0) using the CMF++ module within the MUSES cyberinfrastructure (C. N. Cruz Camacho et al. 2025) and add electrons to fulfill local charge equilibrium us-ing the Lepton module (M. Pelicer 2025). The particle fractions as a function of baryon density obtained us-ing this EOS are shown in Figure 4. The Λ hyperon remains the dominant strangeness-carrying species over all densities we consider. Therefore, in Equation (14), we evolve the fractions of neutrons, protons, electrons, and Λ hyperons (see Appendix B), and assume that the contributions of the remaining hyperons to strangeness equilibration can be neglected. Bulk viscosity 

After analyzing the rate dynamics which determine the far-from-equilibrium response of the matter, we now move on to computing the bulk viscosity. We extract it using Equation (8) as a function of temperature for small sinusoidal oscillations about equilibrium at the 1.95 M ⊙ g-mode frequency of 320 Hz. We compare the effective bulk viscosity with strangeness in and out-of-equilibrium, and the results are shown in Figure 1. We also show, with fainter lines, the results of 0 .5 % density oscillations. Generally, if the density is large enough for strangeness to emerge, the faster strangeness equilibration rates result in significant bulk viscosity at much lower temperatures than for the strangeness-equilibrated case. Due to its weaker temperature de-pendence, the strangeness-driven bulk viscosity is also relevant for a much wider range of temperatures that are expected during inspiral (P. Arras & N. N. Wein-berg 2019; S. Ghosh et al. 2024). At higher tempera-tures, strangeness becomes equilibrated ( μS ≈ 0), while bulk viscosity emerges due to direct Urca reactions, or modified Urca reactions below the direct Urca thresh-old ( nB < 0.31 fm −3 for the CMF EOS). The resonant peaks we find in the bulk viscosity that are seen for all three equilibration channels are expected in the near-equilibrium response and are in general agreement with previous calculations (M. Finally, we evaluate the bulk viscosity in the fluid el-ements of a binary neutron star inspiral simulation. We evolve the composition with and without strangeness out-of-equilibrium, and extract the density-averaged bulk viscosity and chemical deviations (see Figure 9). We find that, below the hyperon onset, the average bulk viscosity is ∼ 2 × 10 28 g cm −1 s−1, caused by large far-from-equilibrium deviations from β-equilibrium with 

⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 420. Above the hyperon onset, the average bulk viscosity with strangeness out-of-equilibrium is in-stead ∼ 2 × 10 29 g cm −1 s−1, with far-from-equilibrium regimes probed for both strangeness and β-equilibrium, with ⟨| μS /T |⟩ ∼ 80 and ⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 260. For the case with equilibrated strangeness, the bulk viscosity above hyperon onset is instead two orders of magnitude smaller at ∼ 8 × 10 27 g cm −1 s−1, with ⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 250. We thus find that the inspiral fluid elements we ana-lyze probe the far-from-equilibrium dynamics discussed in Section 4.1, and the hyperonic core exhibits a sig-nificantly increased bulk viscosity when strangeness is pushed out-of-equilibrium. However, we note that the bulk viscosity above the hyperon threshold is lower than in Figure 1. Since bulk viscosity generally decreases with oscillation frequency, we attribute this lower bulk viscos-ity to high-frequency oscillations caused by errors in the initial data (H.-J. Kuan et al. 2025). As an additional validation, we manually filter out these high frequency oscillations from the fluid trajectories. We then find that in either case a far-from-equilibrium regime persists above the hyperon threshold, with ⟨| μS /T |⟩ ∼ 25 and 

⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 220. Similarly, below the hyperon thresh-old, we still find a large value of ⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 390. Finally, it is instructive to compare these physically expected temperatures (P. Arras & N. N. Weinberg 2019) to the ones probed in the simulation (see Section 3.2). In line with F. Gittins et al. (2025) our simula-tion has substantial artificial heating of the stars, lead-ing to ⟨| μS /T |⟩ ∼ 0.06 and ⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 0.4. Below the hyperon threshold, we similarly get a much smaller de-viation of ⟨| μβ /T |⟩ ∼ 1.62. This demonstrates that the high temperatures induced by numerical surface heating (F. Gittins et al. 2025) strongly suppress any far-from-equilibrium dynamics, which according to our analysis should be present. Consequently, current numerical rel-ativity simulations fundamentally probe both the wrong thermodynamic and chemical reaction regime. More work with accurate inspiral simulations will be required to address this point fully.

By dynamically evolving the composition of the matter, we evaluate the effective bulk viscosity of matter with and without the effects of strangeness equilibration. Finally, we assess the role of strangeness equilibration on binary neutron star inspirals, and find that, for sufficiently massive stars, the inspiral can probe far-from-equilibrium regimes from both strangeness and β-equilibrium, and the resulting dissipation could sub-stantially affect the inspiral evolution, leading to phase shifts, ∆Ψ ≃ 0.14 rad. Our results indicate that, at the low temperatures during inspiral, hyperonic bulk viscosity could be dom-inant for the dynamical properties of massive neutron stars. In particular, it is possible that substantial heat-ing and orbital phase shifts could be associated with both continuous and resonant hyperonic-induced dissi-pation. However, a full understanding of the extent of these effects will ultimately require self-consistent inclu-sion of strangeness equilibration, β equilibration, and associated neutrino energy losses, as well as control of artificial shock heating in inspiral simulations (F. Git-tins et al. 2025). Such an understanding could poten-tially enable dynamical probes of strangeness to rule on its existence in neutron star matter. Finally, many other channels may alter or induce ad-ditional bulk viscosity. The presence of muons can in-crease the Urca threshold density and widen the temper-ature range of Urca-driven bulk viscosity (M. G. Alford & G. Good 2010; M. Alford et al. 2022, 2023). Moreover, the influence of pions and pion condensates (M. A. Pa-jkos & E. R. Most 2025; S. P. Harris et al. 2025) remains to be understood. Additionally, other hadrons (like ∆ baryons) or hyperons could be included, which would significantly complicate the coupled rate equations. Fi-nally, if a first-order deconfinement phase transition oc-curs in the cores of massive neutron stars, this could induce an interface oscillation mode with stronger tidal excitation than the g-mode (A. C. REACTION RATES For the reactions considered here, we compute reaction rates Γ i using the Fermi surface approximation, which assumes that the matter is degenerate and that only particles close to their Fermi surfaces contribute to the reaction rate. Above T ≃ 1 MeV, thermal blurring invalidates this approximation and, for reactions involving neutrinos, alters the condition for β-equilibrium when neutrinos are free-streaming (M. G. Alford & S. P. Harris 2018; M. G. Alford et al. 2021). However, for the sub-MeV temperatures considered in this work, these thermal corrections are expected to remain negligible.
