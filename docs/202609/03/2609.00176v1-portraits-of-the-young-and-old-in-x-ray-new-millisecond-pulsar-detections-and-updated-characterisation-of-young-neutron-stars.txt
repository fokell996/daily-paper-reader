ABSTRACT 

We present analyses of new and archival Chandra and NuSTAR data on two old millisecond pulsars, a young highly-energetic pulsar, and a young neutron star. PSR J1810 −0623 is a recently-discovered 4.55 ms radio pulsar in a binary with a possible carbon-oxygen white dwarf companion. We tentatively detect the pulsar for the first time in X-ray using a new short Chandra 

HRC-I observation. Its faintness suggests a distance more in accord with the further ( ≈ 1 kpc) of its dispersion measure-inferred distances. PSR J1933 −6211 is a 3.54 ms radio pulsar also with a carbon-oxygen white dwarf companion. Our first-time X-ray detection in an archival HRC-S image indicates its X-ray luminosity is similar to that of the 1 .9 𝑀 ⊙ pulsar PSR J1614 −2230. Next, PSR J1813 −1749 is a 1–2 kyr pulsar with one of the highest known spin-down luminosities. We measure its spin period of 44.7 ms using 2025 NuSTAR and 2026 Chandra ACIS-S data and update its spin evolution model. We show for the first time the pulse profile of PSR J1813 −1749 at 3–79 keV, and we find that the spectra of the pulsar and pulsar wind nebula at these energies did not change significantly between observations in 2018 and 2025. Finally, for the 5–10 kyr neutron star CXOU J182913.1 −125113 in the supernova remnant G18.95 −1.1, we combine ACIS-I observations from 2009 and 2024 to better measure the neutron star’s spectrum. 

Key words: stars: neutron – pulsars: general – pulsars: individual: PSR J1810 −0623, PSR J1813 −1749, PSR J1933 −6211 – ISM: individual objects: G18.95 −1.1– X-rays: stars 

1 INTRODUCTION 

X-ray observations of neutron stars can provide invaluable infor-mation about their properties. For example, thermal X-ray emis-sion from hot spots on the surface of rapidly-rotating neutron stars, or millisecond pulsars, allowed measurements of the stellar mass and radius and thus constraints on the equation of state of dense nuclear matter (e.g., Dittmann et al. 2024; Salmi et al. 2024; Vinciguerra et al. 2024; Mauviard et al. 2025; Miller et al. 2026, for recent works). Non-thermal X-rays from young neutron stars give insights into the pulsar magnetosphere and emission geometry and particle acceleration processes (see, e.g., Becker 2009; Enoto et al. 2019, for review). Regular monitoring of young pulsars, whose spin in some cases are only detected in X-ray, enabled limits to be placed on stellar ellipticities via gravitational wave searches (see, e.g., Abac et al. 2025, 2026). In this work, we report on the first X-ray detection of two old millisecond pulsars, PSR J1810 −0623 and PSR J1933 −6211, albeit the former weakly. We present new mea-surements of the spin of the young pulsar, PSR J1813 −1749, and new and archival spectra of PSR J1813 −1749 and the young neutron star CXOU J182913.1 −125113. Below we briefly describe each pulsar. PSR J1810 −0623 is a 4.55 ms radio pulsar that was recently dis-covered and monitored by the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spher-

2 OBSERVATIONS AND DATA ANALYSIS 2.1 Chandra ACIS and HRC data 

Chandra observed each of our four sources with either ACIS-I (for CXOU J182913.1 −125113), ACIS-S in continuous clocking (CC) mode (for PSR J1813 −1749), HRC-I (for PSR J1810 −0623), or HRC-S (for PSR J1933 −6211). A log of these observations is given in Table 1. We reprocess all data using chandra_repro with de-fault settings from the Chandra Interactive Analysis of Observations (CIAO) package version 4.18 and Calibration Database (CALDB) 4.12.3 (Fruscione et al. 2006). For timing analysis of PSR J1813 −1749 with CC mode data, we follow the recommended procedure 1 and use specextract to extract source events from the one-dimensional image along a 1.5 arcsec length centred on the pulsar position. We select only events in the 2–8 keV energy range. We transform the selected event time stamps  

> 1https://cxc.cfa.harvard.edu/ciao/caveats/acis_cc_mode.html

from Terrestrial Time (TT) to Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB) using axbary and the pulsar position. Acceleration searches for the spin frequency are conducted using PRESTO (Ransom et al. 2002) with a time bin of 2.85 ms. Data are folded at the candidate pulse frequency using prepfold , and a refined frequency is determined. For spectral analysis of CXOU J182913.1 −125113, source counts are extracted from a 5 arcsec radius circular region (a 5.2 arcsec radius is used in Tüllmann et al. 2010), and an annulus with inner and outer radii of 10 and 20 arcsec is used for background determination. We combine the two 2024 spectra using combine_spectra and bin separately the 2009 spectrum and the combined 2024 spectrum using dmgroup until a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 is achieved (same as in Tüllmann et al. 2010). HRC does not possess the capabilities needed for detailed spectral analyses of PSR J1810 −0623 and PSR J1933 −6211. Thus we simply use ds9 to extract photons within specified regions (see below) in order to determine source and background counts and count rates. 

2.2 NuSTAR data 

NuSTAR observed PSR J1813 −1749 twice, in 2018 and last year on 2025 October 17 (see Table 1). We reprocess these data us-ing nupipeline and nuproducts of HEASoft 6.35.2 (HEASARC 2014) and NUSTARDAS 2025-03-11_v2.1.5 and barycentre events with the pulsar’s position and clock corrections 20100101v211 for the 2018 data and 20100101v228 for the 2025 data. The source ex-traction region is a circle of 80 arcsec radius, and the background region is a circle of the same size and placed in a field with low counts. For spectral analyses, we bin with a minimum of 100 counts per bin. For timing analysis, we use xselect to choose only events in the 3–50 keV range. We use PRESTO and prepfold to perform pulsation searches on the merged FPMA and FPMB event list and to determine the spin frequency. To extract rotation phase-resolved pulsed spectra, we first use 

photonphase in PINT (Luo et al. 2021) and the spin frequency to determine the rotation phase of each photon in the data. We then use maketime to determine good time intervals (GTIs) for on-pulse emission, i.e, photons with a rotation phase around the peak of the pulse profile, and off-pulse emission, which are photons with a phase outside the on-pulse phase range. Finally, we run nuproducts with these GTIs to produce on and off-pulse spectra. The off-pulse spectra can be used as the background for the on-pulse spectra, such that the final pulsed spectra are the result of on minus off-pulse emissions. 

2.3 Spectral modelling 

We perform spectral fitting using Xspec 12.15.0d (Arnaud 1996). The spectral models used here are composed of two components. The first component accounts for photoelectric absorption by the interstellar medium, i.e., tbabs with abundances from Wilms et al. (2000) and cross-sections from Verner et al. (1996) and parametrized by the effective hydrogen column density 𝑁 H. A second component, which models the intrinsic spectrum of the source, is a powerlaw (PL; powerlaw ). Once a best-fit is found, we use cflux to calculate flux and its uncertainties while holding the powerlaw normalization at its best-fitting value.

3.4 CXOU J182913.1 −125113 

Following the procedure described in Section 2.1, we extract the spectrum of CXOU J182913.1 −125113 from the 2009 and 2024 ACIS-I data, merging the two from 2024, and fit the two epochs independently, as well as jointly, with the absorbed powerlaw model described in Section 2.3. The best-fitting results are shown in Fig-ure 7 and Table 4. From the independent fits of the 2009 and 2024 spectra, the flux appears consistent within errors. 4 DISCUSSION 

We presented analyses of new Chandra and NuSTAR data, supple-mented by archival data when available, of four neutron stars. In the case of PSR J1813 −1749, we measured its most recent spin frequen-cies and updated its timing model. The spin evolution can be used to search for possible gravitational waves that are emitted by this young and highly energetic pulsar. In fact, timing models such as the one calculated here enabled previous gravitational wave searches of PSR J1813 −1749 to constrain its fractional stellar ellipticity to 

® a few × 10 −5 (Abbott et al. 2017, 2019, 2022a,b; Abac et al. 2025, 2026). A monitoring campaign at radio wavelengths using the Green Bank Telescope could allow a rotation-phase coherent timing model to be determined, which would be extremely useful not only for in-creasing the sensitivity of gravitational wave searches but to also see if PSR J1813 −1749 undergoes large and frequent glitches like the Vela pulsar and PSR J0537 −6910. We measured for the first time the pulse profile of PSR J1813 −1749 up to 79 keV thanks to the new NuS-TAR data, which was previously only known up to 27 keV from RXTE 

data (Kuiper & Hermsen 2015), and improved its spectral character-isation at these hard X-ray energies. For the other young neutron star CXOU J182913.1 −125113 in the supernova remnant G18.95 −1.1, we better determined its powerlaw spectrum with the new Chandra 

data. For the two old millisecond pulsars, PSR J1810 −0623 and PSR J1933 −6211, Chandra HRC images yielded their detections in X-ray for the first time. Unfortunately, they are both rela-tively faint, and significantly better information on them in X-ray may only be achieved with much longer exposures. Nonethe-less, PSR J1810 −0623 and PSR J1933 −6211 are worth further study. These two belong to a small group of binary pulsars with a spin period 𝑃 < 6 ms and a ¦ 0.3𝑀 ⊙ carbon-oxygen white dwarf companion; others include PSR J1101 −6424 (Ng et al. 2015), PSR J1125 −6014 (Reardon et al. 2021; Shamohammadi et al. 2023), PSR J1614 −2230 (Demorest et al. 2010; Alam et al. 2021), PSR J1618 −4624 (Cameron et al. 2020), and PSR J1943+2210 (Scholz et al. 2015). These systems are thought to be descendants of intermediate-mass X-ray binaries (Tauris et al. 2011, 2012). Prior to the X-ray detections presented here, only two had been detected outside of radio: PSR J1125 −6014 in gamma-rays (Smith et al. 2019) and PSR J1614 −2230 at gamma-ray and X-ray energies (Abdo et al. 2010; Marelli et al. 2011; Pancrazi et al. 2012). Their high-energy detections are probably due in part to them being the closest of the seven named above, in particular, PSR J1614 −2230 is at a distance of 670 +50  

> −40

pc (Arzoumanian et al. 2018) and PSR J1125 −6014 is at 1.4 ± 0.3 kpc (Reardon et al. 2021). In contrast, PSR J1933 −6211 is at 1 .6+0.2

kpc (Geyer et al. 2023), PSR J1810 −0623 is at a radio DM inferred distance of 0.2 or 1 kpc (see Zhang et al. 2026 and Sec-tion 1), and the other three are at DM distances of ∼ 2, 4, and 6 kpc, respectively. It is also noteworthy that some millisecond pulsars with high spin-down luminosity ¤𝐸 are bright in X-rays due to heating of their polar caps by return currents (see, e.g., Arons 1981; Harding & Muslimov 2001, 2002). PSR J1614 −2230 and PSR J1125 −6014 have the highest ¤𝐸 of the above seven at 1.2 × 10 34 erg s −1 and 8 ×

10 33 erg s −1, respectively. On the other hand, PSR J1933 −6211 and PSR J1810 −0623 have ¤𝐸 = 2.1 × 10 33 erg s −1 and ¤𝐸 =

9.5 × 10 32 erg s −1, respectively, and the remaining three have ¤𝐸 =

(2.6, 0.6, 0.5) × 10 33 erg s −1. Taken together, especially its relative distance, the X-ray brightness of PSR J1933 −6211 is perhaps unsur-prising. In comparison, the X-ray flux of PSR J1810 −0623 is about 1.5 times lower than that of PSR J1933 −6211, such that if it has the same X-ray luminosity (and same as PSR J1614 −2230), then the distance to PSR J1810 −0623 is about 2 kpc, which is much further than implied by its DM. On the other hand, the ¤𝐸 of PSR J1810 −0623 is more than ten times lower than that of PSR 1614 −2230. A distance of 1 kpc is still possible if its intrinsic X-ray luminosity is four times lower, while a distance greater than 2 kpc is possible if its luminosity is greater than that of PSR J1614 −2230 and PSR J1933 −6211 despite its low DM and ¤𝐸 . As a consequence, we suggest 2 kpc as an estimate of the upper bound on the distance, which means PSR J1810 −0623 is still one of the closer systems in its group and could merit future study, especially since future radio monitoring could yield a measurement of a high neutron star mass (Zhang et al. 2026).
