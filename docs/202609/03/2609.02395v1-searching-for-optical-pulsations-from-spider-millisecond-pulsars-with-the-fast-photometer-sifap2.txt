ABSTRACT 

Context. Optical pulsations from millisecond pulsars (MSPs) have recently been discovered from a transitional MSP and an accreting MSP. Evidence for optical pulsations has also been found for a rotation-powered redback MSP. Observations suggest that optical pulsed emission is produced by different mechanisms depending on whether an accretion disk is present in the system or not. 

Aims. We explore a sample of rotation-powered MSPs in close binaries (P orb < 1 day, redbacks and black widows), searching for optical periodic signals with the SiFAP2 photometer mounted on the Telescopio Nazionale Galileo (TNG). 

Methods. We perform pulsation searches using the epoch folding method with the most updated (radio or gamma-ray) orbital solution for each system, employing already published solutions or recent observations with Nançay, the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope, the Parkes Observatory, and Fermi -LAT. 

Results. We update or confirm radio and gamma-ray ephemerides for five of the presented systems. No optical pulsa-tions were detected with a significance exceeding a 3σ confidence level in any of our searches, despite often achieving sensitivities on the verge of the instrument’s limit. This places correspondingly stringent upper limits on the systems’ optical pulsed magnitude and conversion efficiency. Our upper limits are consistent with the pulsed optical luminosity of optical pulsations from the redback PSR J2339-0533. Compared to accreting and transitional MSPs, redback and black widow systems show much lower efficiency in converting their spin down power into pulsed optical emission, supporting the recently advanced idea that the accretion disk around the neutron star plays a role in accelerating the particles responsible for producing bright optical pulsed emission. 

Key words. stars: neutron – pulsars: individual: PSR J1048+2339, PSR J1653-0158, PSR J1723-2837, PSR 1816+4510, PSR J1959+2048, PSR J2055+1545, PSR J2129-0429, PSR J2215+5135 

1. Introduction 

Millisecond pulsars (MSPs) are neutron stars (NSs) rotat-ing with a spin frequency exceeding 100 Hz. A fraction of the MSP population exists in tight binary systems (orbital periods P orb ∼ hours) with low-mass ( ≲ 1M ⊙) companion stars. These systems are formed via a recycling phase dur-ing which matter expelled by the companion star is accreted

onto the NS. During this phase, the source appears as an X-ray emitter, and the accretion process is accompanied by the transfer of angular momentum, which spins the NS up to rotational frequencies that are characteristic of the MSPs (Alpar et al. 1982; Bhattacharya & van den Heuvel 1991). When the accretion phase is over, the bulk of their emission comes from rotation-powered mechanisms, mainly occur-ring at radio or γ-ray frequencies (Backer et al. 1982; Abdo et al. 2009). As the rotation-powered pulsar wind emitted

by the NS in such close binaries is able to eject matter off its companion’s surface, these pulsars are called “spiders”. These are divided into subclasses according to the com-panion star’s mass. Redbacks (RBs) are defined as binary systems hosting a donor star of M ∼ 0.2 − 0.4 M⊙, while black widows (BWs) host companions with M < 0.1 M⊙.A small subset of spiders has been observed switching be-tween the accretion disk and rotation-powered pulsar states on timescales of weeks to months and has therefore been dubbed transitional MSPs (tMSPs) as a result of their switching between X-ray- and radio-dominated emissions (Archibald et al. 2009; Papitto et al. 2013). Recently, optical pulsations at the NS spin frequency were discovered in a variety of binary systems hosting MSPs. First, optical pulsations have been detected from the tMSP PSR J1023+0038 (Ambrosino et al. 2017, see also Papitto et al. 2019; Illiano et al. 2023), and then in-dependently confirmed (Zampieri et al. 2019; Karpov et al. 2019). PSR J1023+0038 has also been discovered to show pulsations in the UV band (Miraval Zanon et al. 2022; Jao-dand et al. 2021). Then, optical and UV pulsations were observed from the accreting MSP SAX J1808.4-3658 (Am-brosino et al. 2021; Ballocco et al. 2026). Even more re-cently, evidence for optical pulsations has been proposed for the RB PSR J2339–0533 (Papitto et al. 2025). In this regard, the Silicon Fast optical Photometer ( SiFAP2 , Ghe-dina et al. 2018) was key to enabling the discovery of these new detections. In this work, we search for optical pulsations from a sample of eight MSPs observed with SiFAP2 between 2018 and 2025. The sample consists of six RBs (PSR J1048+2339, PSR J1723-2837, PSR J1816+4510, PSR J2129-0429, PSR J2215+5135, and PSR J2055+1545) and two BWs (PSR J1653-0158 and PSR J1959+2048). We use our findings to constrain the efficiency of the conversion of their spin-down power into optical pulsed emission, which is key to understanding the mechanism producing optical pulsations in rotation-powered MSPs as compared to other MSP systems such as accreting and transitional MSPs. 

2. Observations 

We performed our observations with the improved version of the Silicon Fast Astronomical Photometer and Polarime-ter, SiFAP2 (Meddi et al. 2012; Ambrosino et al. 2014; Ghe-dina et al. 2018). SiFAP2 is a non-imaging fast photometer mounted at the 3.6-m Telescopio Nazionale Galileo (TNG, Barbieri et al. 1994). It consists of two detectors covering a field of view of 7 × 7 arcsec 2 centered on the source and reference star (or background sky) positions, respectively. 

SiFAP2 records the times of arrival of individual photons with an 8 ns resolution. Instrumental calibration was per-formed by on-site observations in white light of a series of K0V-K5V stars observed at small zenith distances and com-pared with V-mag catalogued values 1. Observations were carried out in white light configuration (no filters applied) and with a bandpass of 320-900 nm peaked in the B-band at ∼ 450 nm. Background count rates for individual observations were estimated in at least one of two ways. One way is by simul-taneous observations of an empty region of the sky about 

4 arcsec from the source. The other way is via the nodding

3. Timing analysis 

3.1. Arrival times corrections 

In order to search for pulsed signals during our SiFAP2 

observations, we first corrected all photon arrival times to the Solar System barycenter frame of reference using the DE405 ephemeris. We then considered the most updated orbital ephemeris for each source to correct the times of ar-rival for the pulsar binary motion and improve sensitivity to coherent pulsating signals. For PSR J2215+5135, J2129-0429, J1048+2339, and J1653-0158, we obtained the initial ephemerides from the publicly available third pulsars cat-alog (3PC) Fermi -LAT solutions (Smith et al. 2023). The ephemerides used for the timing analysis are reported in Table 2, while additional details on the search parameters and the system’s properties are listed in Table 3 and Sect. 4. In two cases (PSR J1653-0158 and PSR J1816+4510), the available solution was stable enough to be propagated to our SiFAP2 observing times without producing an uncer-tainty on any of the relevant timing parameters that was larger than our search sensitivity. However, other sources from our sample showed strong orbital timing noise, as is often the case for spider MSPs (see, e.g., Ridolfi et al. 2016, and references therein). This is possibly due to the cyclic modulation of the orbital period due to gravitational quadrupole moment exchange between the binary compo-nents (Pletsch & Clark 2015; De Falco et al. 2025). Our 

SiFAP2 exposures are too short to be sensitive to orbital period modulations (on the order of ∆Porb /P orb ∼ 10 −7,see, e.g., Pletsch & Clark 2015; Clark et al. 2021). However, when left unmodeled, such modulations cause the pulsar’s time of passage at the ascending node of the orbit ( TASC ) to deviate from a pure propagation of the initial ephemeris ob-tained by employing a constant orbital period value. Due to such variations, a given ephemeris is typically consid-ered valid only for a specific time period. Within the valid-ity period of the orbital solution, typical variations in the 

TASC value around the average solution are on the order of ∆TASC ∼ 10 s or larger (see also, e.g., Rosenthal et al. 2025). If that is the case, to correct the arrival times we cannot consider the TASC parameter as known, and our periodicity search must also take into account a search for the possible variations of the TASC parameter. Beyond the range of the ephemeris validity, we assumed that varia-tions of the TASC parameter would be of the same order as 

∆TASC (that is, tens of seconds on average, although the actual value differs for each source). The other orbital pa-rameters (the orbital period, P orb , and the projected semi-major axis of the pulsar orbit, a sin i/c ), however, do not typically show variability over the considered time spans and are therefore treated as constant in our search (Rosen-thal et al. 2025). The importance of the TASC uncertainty over other orbital parameters can also be appreciated by inspecting the derivatives of the phase model for a circular orbit (see, e.g., Leaci & Prix 2015). Additional details on the analysis chain are furnished in Ambrosino et al. (2017, 2021); Papitto et al. (2025). The latter, especially, high-lights the potential for pulsation detections from our class of targets under realistic observing conditions.

4. The SiFAP2 sample of MSPs 

4.1. PSR J1048+2339 

PSR J1048+2339 (J1048 hereafter) is an RB system with a 4.7-ms pulsar and an orbital period of about 6 hr (Cromar-tie et al. 2016). It has a spin-down power of 1.2×10 34 erg s −1

and is located at a distance of 1.7 kpc (Deneva et al. 2016). J1048 is detected by Fermi -LAT and is listed in the 3PC catalog with an orbital solution (Deneva et al. 2021). This solution includes several orbital period derivatives that are used to model the stochastic T ASC variability over the va-lidity range. However, the period of validity does not cover our SiFAP2 observation. As an estimate of the number of trials for a search over the T ASC , we proceeded as follows.  

> 3https://www.tng.iac.es/instruments/sifap2/

We first propagated the value of the T ASC and its uncer-tainty to the SiFAP2 observing times, assuming a constant orbital period (i.e., neglecting the orbital period derivatives present in the 3PC solution). We then estimated, within the 

Fermi -LAT solution validity period, the range of variabil-ity of the orbital period compared to a constant value. This value was then added in quadrature to the propagated un-certainty. Finally, we considered a multiplying factor of 6 to account for a 3σ span on each side of the propagated TASC value. 

4.2. PSR J1653-0158 

PSR J1653-0158 (J1653 hereafter) is a BW system with a 1.9-ms pulsar and a 1.2-hr orbital period (Nieder et al. 2020). The binary orbit is among the most compact sys-tems of its type. J1653 is detected by Fermi -LAT and has a solution in the 3PC catalog, whose behavior is remarkably stable. In fact, the Fermi -LAT solution does not require an orbital period derivative. Although the published 3PC so-lution is limited to a time range that does not include our 

SiFAP2 observation times, the source orbit solution based on Fermi -LAT data remains stable and can also be adopted at later times (that is, at least up to MJD 60362, M. Kerr priv. comm.). Following Nieder et al. (2020), we employ a distance of 0.84(4) kpc, consistent with Gaia DR3 (Koljo-nen & Linares 2023). Also, we adopt a spin-down power value of 1.2 × 10 34 erg/s (Romani et al. 2014). 

4.3. PSR J1723-2837 

PSR J1723-2837 (J1723 hereafter) is an RB system showing radio eclipses (Crawford et al. 2013). There is no significant 

γ-ray detection for this source, and a Fermi -LAT solution for it is not publicly available. The NS spin period is 1.86 ms (Faulkner et al. 2004) and the orbital period is about 15 hr. Its Gaia DR3 distance is 0.9 kpc (Koljonen & Linares 2023), while its spin-down power is 4.6 × 10 34 erg s −1 (Crawford et al. 2013). It was recently observed with the IXPE obser-vatory as part of an extended observational campaign, al-though no significant X-ray polarization has been detected (Negro et al. 2026; Sullivan et al. 2026). The most recent timing solution for this source was published by Crawford et al. (2013). However, this solution does not cover our 2019 and 2022 SiFAP2 observations of the source. For this rea-son, Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) observa-tions were performed (Ridolfi et al., priv. comm.) to obtain a radio timing solution that covers our SiFAP2 2019 obser-vations (see Tables 2, 3). We therefore employed that solu-tion to search for pulsations in our 2019 observations. Since a single radio observation does not allow for a reliable fitting of the times of arrival, the radio data were simply folded with the Crawford et al. (2013) solution, changing T ASC in small steps until the highest S/N ratio was reached. This method does not provide uncertainties on the fitted pa-rameters, for which we used those reported in Crawford et al. (2013) instead. Moreover, we propagated the T ASC 

obtained through the GMRT observations to the day of our 2021 SiFAP2 observation (not covered by radio obser-vations), assuming an uncertainty similar to the variability of the orbital phase displayed during the validity interval of the solution by Crawford et al. (2013). This corresponds to a conservative range of 50 s around the propagated T ASC .

4.4. PSR J1816+4510 

PSR J1816+4510 (J1816 hereafter) is an RB system with a 3.2-ms pulsar and an 8.7-hr long orbital period (Ray et al. 2012; Kaplan et al. 2012). It was also observed and charac-terized by the Green Bank Northern Celestial Cap Pulsar Survey (Stovall et al. 2014). The system was also recently observed with the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope (FAST) to study its radio eclipse behavior (Shang et al. 2024). The adopted source distance is 4.36 kpc (Yao et al. 2017; Shang et al. 2024), and the spin-down luminosity is 5 × 10 34 erg/s (Fiore et al. 2023). Similar to J1653, J1816 has a publicly available Fermi -LAT 3PC tim-ing solution that is remarkably steady and can be extended up to at least MJD 60884 (D. Smith, M. Kerr, priv. comm.). Thanks to this, we employed PRESTO 4 (Ransom 2011) to fit 

Fermi -LAT data, obtaining a precise timing solution that covers our SiFAP2 observations, thus performing single trial searches for pulsations using the best-fit T ASC that is near-est to our SiFAP2 observations. Our periodicity search from J1816 returns a 2.1σ confidence level signal which, although not significant, is of relevance among those obtained in this work. We therefore produce a SiFAP2 pulse profile of the 2022 observations (see Fig 2). We compare this to the un-weighted Fermi -LAT profile above 0.1 GeV obtained with a radius of interest of 0.8◦. Although γ-ray profiles can dif-fer from the profiles obtained in different energy bands (see, e.g., Abdo et al. 2010), the similarity of the shapes, the sep-aration of the peaks, and the approximate phase alignment is intriguing.

4.5. PSR J1959+2048 

PSR J1959+2048 (also dubbed PSR B1957+20, J1959 here-after) is a BW system with a spin period of 1.6 ms and an orbital period of 9.2 hours (Fruchter et al. 1988). For this source, the spin-down luminosity value we adopt is 

1.6 × 10 35 erg/s (Koljonen & Linares 2025), while we adopt a distance value of 1.73 kpc (Yao et al. 2017). We employed the ephemeris obtained by the Nançay telescope around our SiFAP2 observing times (see Blanchard et al. 2025). These were fitted with the ELL1 model (Lange et al. 2001) that utilizes the Laplace-Lagrange parameters while also accounting for the possible small eccentricity of the orbit. Given the large uncertainties derived from the propagation of the T ASC to the epochs of SiFAP2 observations in 2024, a large number of trials are necessary for the periodicity search (see Table 3). These span a 3σ interval around the central value. 

4.6. PSR J2055+1545 

PSR J2055+1545 (J2055 hereafter) is an RB system, dis-covered by Lewis et al. (2023a) in the Arecibo 327 MHz Drift-Scan Pulsar Survey. It is the most recently discov-ered source among those analyzed in this work. Although a coincident Fermi -LAT counterpart was detected for this source, no γ-ray pulsations were found. J2055 consists of a NS with a 2.2 ms spin period in a circular 4.8 hr orbit (Lewis et al. 2023a). Following Koljonen & Linares (2025) we adopt the YMW16 distance of 3.7 kpc, while the adopted spin-down power is 4.0 × 10 34 erg/s Lewis et al. (2023a). The system shows radio eclipses as observed by the Arecibo survey Lewis et al. (2023a). For this source, we obtained radio observations with the Murriyang radio telescope in Parkes (NSW, Australia) at the request of Green Time un-der project PX090. The Ultra Wideband Low receiver (704-4032 MHz; Hobbs et al. 2020) was used in search mode, with 1-MHz wide channels coherently de-dispersed at the pulsar’s dispersion measure. Three epochs were scheduled on February 7, 26, and March 30, 2026, at times when the source was predicted to be outside the radio eclipse. Each observation was folded starting from the ephemeris from Lewis et al. 2023b, with a range of T0 covering ± 0.01 in or-bital phase, using the software spider-twister 5, based on 

PRESTO (Ransom 2011). For each observation, the highest signal-to-noise folded data and the related T0 were used as starting points for the subsequent timing analysis. The best local set of ephemeris was obtained with TEMPO2 (Hobbs et al. 2006) fitting for the orbital parameters. Results are shown in Table 2.

4.8. PSR J2215+5135 

PSR J2215+5135 (J2215 hereafter) is a RB system with a spin period of 2.6 ms and an orbital period of 4.1 hr (Hes-sels et al. 2011; Bangale et al. 2024). The distance value we adopt for this source is 3.33 kpc (Sullivan & Romani 2024), while the spin-down power is 5.3 × 10 34 erg/s (Breton et al. 2013). To calculate the T ASC value around our SiFAP2 ob-servations, similarly to J1048 (see Sect. 4.1), we propagated the public Fermi -LAT orbital solution, assuming a constant orbital period. The solution, however, includes a series of ORBIFUNC parameters representing a time-domain inter-polating function to model the orbital phase variations over time. Using these, the T ASC oscillates over a ±6 s range with respect to the T ASC value obtained from the initial constant orbital period ephemeris (see, e.g., Clark et al. 2021). Similar to J1048, we thus add this value in quadra-ture to the propagated uncertainty to obtain a more con-servative search range around the central T ASC value. 

5. Discussion 

Optical pulsations have already been detected in a few sys-tems that were previously identified as radio pulsators, in-cluding five young isolated neutron stars (INSs, Cocke et al. 1969; Mignani 2011), as well as in MSPs (whether accreting, transitional, or RB, see Ambrosino et al. 2017, 2021; Pa-pitto et al. 2025). The common interpretation of the optical emission from INSs is that it is incoherent synchrotron ra-diation produced by relativistic electron-positron pairs ac-celerated in the NS magnetosphere (Pacini & Salvati 1983; Shearer & Golden 2001; Mignani 2011). Its efficiency can be defined as the conversion efficiency of spin-down luminos-ity into optical luminosity in a certain band (e.g., B-band) 

η = Lopt / ˙Esd . For systems such as the Crab, it is on the order of 10 −5, while for other INSs it is even lower, on the order of 10 −8 − 10 −7 (see Fig. 3). On the other hand, the

In Fig. 3, we report the results of our optical obser-vational campaign over the sample of MSPs described in Sect. 4. Results are reported in terms of the conversion efficiency of the spin-down power into pulsed optical lu-minosity. As we do not find any signals above the detec-tion threshold, we were only able to establish upper lim-its on the optical pulse efficiency of the analyzed systems. We nonetheless highlight these as the most stringent con-straints on this type of signal from the considered sources. We also note that the upper limits we obtained on the efficiency values are of the same order as those for PSR J0337+1715, a MSP (2.7 ms spin period) in a triple hier-archical system with two white dwarfs observed with a dif-ferent detector, namely ARCONS (Strader et al. 2013, see Fig. 3). Moreover, they are in agreement with the results for the RB system PSR J2339-0533, where a 3.5σ pulsa-tion detection was found with a pulsed optical luminosity of m B = 27 .0 mag (Papitto et al. 2025, and Fig. 3). Our results support the hypothesis that the optical pulse effi-ciency in spiders is ≲ 10 −5, i.e., up to a couple of orders of magnitude lower than that observed for other types of MSPs in binary systems, namely transient and accreting (see PSR J1023+0038 and SAX J1808.4-3568 in Fig. 3). This is in agreement with recent models (Papitto et al. 2019; Veledina et al. 2019) that highlight the role played by an accretion disk producing bright optical pulsations as synchrotron emission at the intrabinary shock, where the pulsar wind interacts with the disk (Richard-Romei et al. 2026). cate that if optical pulsations are present in spider MSPs, they are generally fainter than in the currently known ac-creting and transitional MSP cases. This is consistent with, but does not unambiguously prove, a role for the accre-tion flow or disk in enhancing optical pulsed emission. On the other hand, similar to INSs, spider systems have no ac-cretion flow influencing the magnetosphere, and the mech-anism producing optical pulsations is expected to be the same (Papitto et al. 2025, and references therein). In fact, as discussed by Papitto et al. (2025), despite a gap in spin-down power of four orders of magnitude between isolated pulsars such as the Crab and its twin in the Large Mag-ellanic Cloud, PSR J0540-6919, and the RB system PSR J2339-0533, their optical pulsed efficiency η is similar. On the other hand, our non-detection of pulsations in spider systems could, in principle, be due to other factors be-sides the intrinsically low efficiency η. Among these, we mention instrument sensitivity, the geometry of the system (which might be such that the beam of optical emission does not cross our line of sight), and the uncertainty in the ephemeris. Moreover, we notice that pulsations from spider systems are often transient in the radio band due to scin-tillation and absorption by plasma ejected by the pulsar wind, which enshrouds the system, thus making an intrin-sically weak signal even harder to detect (Miraval Zanon et al. 2021; Ridolfi et al. 2016; Thongmeearkom et al. 2024). The non-detection of optical pulsations in our sample can therefore also be due to their transient nature. Finally, we mention that the beaming in different energy bands can also play a role in their detectability. Remarkable cases in this regard are the Fermi -LAT γ-ray pulsars PSR J1035-6720 and PSR J1744-7619. In fact, faint radio pulsations were detected for the former only after the discovery of their γ-ray pulsations, while no radio pulsations have been detected so far for the latter (Clark et al. 2021). 

6. Conclusions 

We analyzed a sample of eight spider MSPs observed with 

SiFAP2 in search of optical pulsed signals. We summarize our main findings as follows: 1. We find no significant pulsations in the optical band from the analyzed sample of sources. We have obtained stringent ( 95% confidence level) upper limits on the op-tical pulsed efficiency. These are compatible with the efficiency value obtained for PSR J2339-0533, the only other RB system for which evidence of optical pulsations has been reported with an efficiency η = 3 .3 × 10 −6.2. Our upper limits are a factor of 50-100 below the optical pulse efficiency measured for the transitional and accret-ing MSP systems PSR J1023+0038 and SAX J1808.4-3658, respectively. These findings support the hypothe-sis that an accretion disk around the NS increases the efficiency of producing optical pulsations, while the op-tical pulse efficiency of spider systems possibly has the same origin as the optical pulsation from isolated radio pulsars. 3. Orbital timing solutions for three systems, namely J1723, J1959, and J2055 have been updated using re-cent radio observations. Moreover, for two other sys-tems, namely J1653 and J1816, we verified that their known orbits derived from Fermi -LAT γ-ray data are re-markably stable and still hold as of the time of writing. Here we report their most recent orbital parameters. Our results on the optical pulse efficiency are also sum-marized in Fig. 3. They show that an improvement by a factor of two in pulsed-flux sensitivity would lower the ef-ficiency limits by about 50% , which is sufficient to test whether optical pulse efficiency values similar to those mea-sured for PSR J2339-0533 are typical of spider MSPs. How-ever, sensitivity alone may not secure detection, as it can be affected by geometric, ephemeris, and intermittency lim-itations.
