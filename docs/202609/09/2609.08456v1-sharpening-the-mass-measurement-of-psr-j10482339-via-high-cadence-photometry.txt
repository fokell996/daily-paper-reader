ABSTRACT Neutron stars are among the densest objects in the universe, and the mass measurements are crucial to the understanding of their equation of state. Pulsars in tight binaries can be weighed dynam-ically, but accurate mass estimates are often hampered by uncertainties in orbital inclinations. In this paper, we report on multi-epoch optical monitoring of the pulsar binary, PSR J1048+2339, using the 0.5-m RIFT telescope at Lulin Observatory. Our observations reveal frequent optical flares with eclipses caused by the companion. Using the eclipse duration with archival photometric data and orbital ephemeris in the literature, we constrain the inclination to 78 .◦0 ± 1.◦0 and the pulsar mass to 1 .79 +0 .09  

> −0.08

solar masses. This measurement is among the most precise for any redback system to date, demonstrating a novel way to improve the pulsar mass measures with small aperture optical telescopes. Furthermore, the optical eclipse duration is significantly shorter than that measured in M/GeV γ-rays, possibly suggesting that a fraction of the eclipsed γ-ray emission originates from the apex of the intrabinary shock. Based on these constraints, we estimate the magnetic field strength of the companion to be 30–170 G. This is consistent with the high field strength implied by the X-ray orbital modulation observed.

1. INTRODUCTION In compact millisecond pulsar binaries, the low-mass companion stars are being ablated by strong radiations due to their proximity to the neutron stars. These sys-tems are named “black widow” (with semi-degenerate companions) or “redback” (with non-degenerate com-panions) because of the similarity to the mating be-havior of the spider species (Chen et al. 2013; Hui & Li 2019). The companions in these binaries are tidally locked so that the same sides always face to the radiat-ing neutron stars and get irradiated to higher temper-atures. In addition, the shapes of the companions can be ellipsoid-like due to the strong gravity of the neu-tron stars causing ellipsoidal variation. These result in

orbital modulations in the optical bands, which can be used to extract information of the orbital parameters of the binaries, and hence, the masses of the neutron stars (Romani et al. 2012; Li et al. 2016; Strader et al. 2019). PSR J1048+2339 is a 4.66-ms redbacks millisecond pulsar in a tight binary of an orbital period of 6.01 hours, discovered in radio by Arecibo and Green Bank Tele-scope (GBT; Cromartie et al. 2016; Deneva et al. 2016 and later in M/GeV γ-rays by the Fermi Large Area Telescope (LAT; Abdo et al. 2013. It is also one of a few redback systems that exhibits variable irradiation (Cho et al. 2018), of which the power can increase up to a factor of 6 in just two weeks (Yap et al. 2019). There were several mass measurement attempts for PSR J1048+2339 using optical observations together with the ephemeris of the pulsar (Yap et al. 2019; Strader et al. 2019; Miraval Zanon et al. 2021; Simp-son et al. 2025). data, a wide possible mass range of MNS = 1 .08–2 .3M⊙

was inferred because of the unknown orbital inclination angle. A recent breakthrough came from the Fermi -LAT discoveries of the γ-ray eclipses of two black wid-ows and five redbacks, including PSR J1048+2339. If the eclipses are caused by occultation by the companion, the mass of PSR J1048+2339 can be further constrained to MNS = 1 .44–1 .72 M⊙ (Clark et al. 2023). In this paper, we report on the high-cadence opti-cal photometric data of PSR J1048+2339 taken by the 

Robotic Imager For Transients (RIFT). Unusual “flare eclipses” were observed in the data, with which we fur-ther constrained the pulsar mass and measured the mag-netic field strength of the companion by comparing the eclipse durations detected by RIFT and Fermi -LAT.

RIFT OBSERVATIONS RIFT is a 0.5-m telescope at the Lulin Observatory in Taiwan. It is a corrected Dall-Kirkham (CDK) telescope manufactured by the PlaneWave Instruments with a fo-cal ratio of f / 4.5. The telescope is mounted on the L-series equatorial mount designed by the same company with a tracking accuracy of < 0.′′ 3 in 5 minutes. To-gether with the Andor Technology camera, iKon-L 936, with a 2048 × 2048 charge-coupled device (CCD) array (or 27 .6 mm × 27 .6 mm), the f / 4.5 CDK telescope has a field of view (FoV) of 41 .′7×41 .′7, equivalent to 0.44 deg 2,with a pixel scale is 1 .′′ 22 per pixel. The robotic telescope system is operated by a Python script developed by our own team with the Astronomy Common Object Model (ASCOM ) interface. We carried out a three-month observing campaign for PSR J1048+2339 using the 0.5-m telescope in 2023. From 1st February to 28th April 2023, about 4,000 un-filtered images of PSR J1048+2339 were taken in 48 nights of good weather and grey/dark sky. Each image has an exposure of 200 seconds, equivalent to 0.9% of the orbital period. 2.1. Data Reduction 

The Image Reduction and Analysis Facility ( IRAF )was used to reduce the RIFT observations with stan-dard reduction processes, including bias-frame subtrac-tion, dark-frame subtraction and flat-frame correction. The astrometry was then performed by astrometry.net 

(Lang et al. 2010) to obtain a reliable world coordinate system (WCS) solution for each image. As the field of the PSR J1048+2339 is not crowded, aperture photom-etry was applied using phot with an aperture radius of 3 pixels (or 3 .′′ 7) and an annulus background region (inner radius: 15 pixels; outer radius: 25 pixels). We further employed differential photometry to eliminate the effect from the varying weather condition. A bright (R1 = 14 .64 mag) and nearby (less than 1 ′ away from PSR J1048+2339) field star, USNO-B1.0 1136 −0178206 (Monet et al. 2003) was chosen as the comparison star. Comparing with a check star in the field, the differen-tial magnitude light curve of the comparison star is very stable with a fluctuation less than 0.02 mag. ECLIPSING LIGHT CURVE (ELC) MODELLING The orbital inclination ( i), the component separa-tion ( a), and the size of the companion star of PSR J1048+2339 are tightly correlated by the eclipse dura-tion measure. Provided that the mass ratio ( Q; the neu-tron star mass to companion mass ratio) is known, the component separation projected onto the line of sight can be obtained through pulsar timing (Deneva et al. 2016; Miraval Zanon et al. 2021). This means that the separation is a function of the inclination. For com-pact binary systems like PSR J1048+2339, the degrees of the stellar distortion increase with sizes according to the Roche equipotential surfaces. By modelling the op-tical light curves of PSR J1048+2339, we can investigate the stellar distortion, and hence, the Roche equipoten-tial surface. Using the Eclipsing Light Curve (ELC) code (Orosz & Hauschildt 2000), we re-modeled the g′- and r′-band photometric data of the Lulin One-meter Telescope (LOT) obtained by Yap et al. (2019) with the RIFT eclipse duration (i.e., θ = 0 .0325 ± 0.0075 orbits) to measure the pulsar mass. In Yap et al. (2019), there were two groups of observations, in which the optical variability was dominated by the ellipsoidal variation and the pulsar heating, respectively. We adopted the dataset dominated by the ellipsoidal variation that pro-vides more information on the stellar distortion. We note that the LOT light curves are slightly asymmetric, as mentioned in Yap et al. (2019). The feature could be modelled by adding hot/cold spots on the stellar surface, but the fitting result will then be more model-dependent as well. For a conservative result, we decided not to assume any hot/cold spots on the surface. This will cause some deviations between the model and the data, and subsequently increases some parameter uncertain-ties. However, the result will be more defensive. By discarding the first 33,000 generations as burn-in samples, we used the Python package, corner , to quantify the results and show the projected posterior probability distributions of the model/derived parameters (Figure 5). DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 4.1. Flare Eclipse Interpretation 

Flares of PSR J1048+2339 have been previously ob-served in both X-rays and optical bands (Yap et al. 2019; Cho et al. 2018). However, it remains unclear whether the X-ray and optical flares are related as no simultane-ous pairs have been observed yet. Since the optical flares concentrated on the heated side of the companion star, the origin was proposed to be an enhanced heating effect related to the magnetic activity of the companion. This is in line with the RIFT observations that show no flares in eclipses during which the heated side was invisible to Earth, meaning that the observed flare eclipses have the same duration as that of the pulsar eclipse (Figure 6). An alternative picture is that the optical flares were from the intrabinary shock between the pulsar and the companion star (Roberts et al. 2014; Takata et al. 2014; Li et al. 2014). In Miraval Zanon et al. (2021), they used Doppler tomography and found H α emissions near the companion surface and the inner Lagrangian point. These H α emitting materials were suggested to be re-lated to the intrabinary shock, and these regions could be a possible birthplace for the flares observed. Based on the two-dimensional velocity Doppler map of the H α line, Miraval Zanon et al. (2021) further claimed that the intrabinary shock is closer to the companion, contradicting to the X-ray observations (i.e., the shock is closer to the pulsar; Cho et al. 2018; Yap et al. 2019). Consequently, they concluded that the X-ray and H α

observations actually refer to the two sides of the shock that are closer to the pulsar and to the companion, re-spectively, indicating a very thick shock shell. Although a thin-shell approximation is often used in intrabinary shock models (e.g., van der Merwe et al. 2020), a thick-shell shock is not entirely impossible. However, as dis-cussed in Wadiasingh et al. (2017), the accelerated, ra-diative electron population should retain in a relatively thin shell. We therefore speculate that the H α gases are likely unshocked intrabinary materials illuminated by the shock X-ray emission. This interpretation is also consistent with the low velocities of the emissions in the rotating frame of the binary (if the proposed positions are correct). In this case, the H α regions should not be powerful enough to radiate the observed flares with brightnesses comparable to the star light of the compan-ion (i.e., the flare amplitude can be higher than 1 mag; Figure 1). In contrast, the thin-shell shock with radia-tive electrons possibly generate the flares. However, this is unlikely in the case of PSR J1048+2339 as no X-ray eclipse was observed in the Chandra X-ray observations (Cho et al. 2018; Yap et al. 2019; Appendix C), suggest-ing that the thin-shell shock was not completely blocked by the companion during the eclipses. The observed flares can be a consequence of increased particle/photon fluxes from the pulsar that reach the companion sur-face through the intrabinary shock. The discovery of the flare eclipses of PSR J1048+2339 and its application to mass measurement opens a new pathway for astronomers to enrich the demographics of neutron stars. Currently, the most accurate mass mea-surements for neutron stars rely on the general relativis-tic effects of massive systems, such as Shapiro delay and the rates of the periastron advances (Burgay et al. 2003; Demorest et al. 2010; Weisberg et al. 2010; Ferdman et al. 2014; Martinez et al. 2015; Fonseca et al. 2016; Stovall et al. 2018; Cromartie et al. 2020). Besides, high-resolution spectroscopy of the companions can make good constraints on the companion masses or orbital inclinations for fine pulsar mass measurements (Anto-niadis et al. 2013; Kennedy et al. 2022). Despite the re-markable results, competitive observing times from big telescopes are essential. By contrast, our investigation on PSR J1048+2339 has demonstrated a much more ac-cessible approach using a small optical telescope. Flar-ing episodes are not uncommon in redbacks and black widows (An et al. 2017; Cho et al. 2018; Pal et al. 2020; Halpern et al. 2022; Swihart et al. 2022), although some have not exhibited yet, perhaps owing to a lack of con-tinues optical observations. With high-cadence long-term monitoring even with small aperture telescopes, it is convinced that the flare eclipse measurement can be transferable to other spider systems that are viewed suf-ficiently edge-on. The immediate candidates would be the seven systems that have shown γ-ray eclipses in the 

Fermi Large Area Telescope (LAT) data (Clark et al. 2023). The possible differences between the γ-ray and optical eclipse durations can also be a probe for the lo-cation of the shock apex as well as the magnetic field 10 Li et al. 

strength of the spider companion. Moreover, the data of some large optical survey programs, like the Rubin Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), would be useful on the mass measurements for faint spi-ders. With more than eighty spider known systems to-day (Hui & Li 2019; Koljonen & Linares 2025), improved mass measures of some of them can already directly en-hance our understanding of neutron stars. CHANDRA OBSERVATIONS Using three Chandra archival observations of PSR J1048+2339 (DOI: 10.25574/cdc.657) with exposure times of 23.1 ks (ObsID 19037), 25.1 ks (ObsID 19038), and 22.9 ks (ObsID 19039), we checked if PSR J1048+2339 showed eclipses in X-rays. The observations were reduced and analysed with CIAO (version 4.13). The task dmcopy was employed to extract source photons from the three observations with circular regions of radii 3 .′′ 0, 2 .′′ 5, and 2 .′′ 3, respectively, based on visual inspection on the X-ray images. The time columns of the source events were then barycentric corrected by 

axbary . Figure C9 shows the source events in a plane of photon energy against orbital phase with the flare eclipse region indicated by two dashed lines. Five photons were detected during the flare eclipses, and three of them have energies higher than 1 keV. According to the source-free regions of the X-ray images, the expected number of the background counts is six in total. These strongly suggest that the five photons are not from the soft X-ray background but PSR J1048+2339, and we therefore conclude that there was no X-ray eclipse observed in the Chandra data.
