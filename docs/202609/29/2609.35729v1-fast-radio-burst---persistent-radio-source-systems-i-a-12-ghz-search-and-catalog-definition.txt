ABSTRACT 

Context. Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond-duration radio transients observed at extragalactic distances, whose origin remains uncertain. Given their extremely short duration and high implied luminosities, magnetars are commonly identified as sources of FRBs. A small ( ∼ 2%) fraction of FRBs are known to repeat, but it is still unclear whether repeaters represent a distinct population. Four repeating FRBs present a co-spatial persistent radio source (PRS) that is compact at parsec scales, and is interpreted as a dense and highly magnetized synchrotron-emitting nebula. 

Aims. We investigate the connection between FRBs and PRSs, in particular the PRS occurrence in a sample of localized FRBs. 

Methods. We carried out new 1 .26 GHz observations of 24 FRBs (9 repeaters and 11 one-o ff s), conducted with the upgraded Giant Meter Wave Radio Telescope (uGMRT) at arc-second angular scale resolution, and combined them with literature data to construct an extended sample of 75 sources with either persistent luminosity measurements or upper limits. Our catalog is complete down to a 10 29 erg s −1 Hz −1 luminosity threshold, which corresponds to the value of the first-discovered PRS, associated with FRB 20121102. 

Results. We detect compact radio emission in six FRB fields, two of which are previously known PRSs and four are new candidates. The four new candidates are associated with three new repeating FRBs and one apparently one-o ff source. We computed the PRS occurrence using the whole catalog of 75 FRBs and found that PRSs more luminous than 10 29 erg s −1 Hz −1 are rare. 1. Introduction 

Fast radio bursts (FRBs; Cordes & Chatterjee 2019; Petro ff et al. 2022; Pilia 2021; Zhang 2023) are short duration and lumi-nous radio flashes characterized by a large dispersion measure (DM) even at high Galactic latitude (e.g., Cordes & Chatterjee 2019). Approximately 100 distinct FRB sources have been lo-calized to their host galaxies (e.g., Gordon et al. 2023), and the implied, extremely high brightness temperatures, combined with their short duration, favor compact objects as their progenitors. The most invoked are magnetars, i.e., neutron stars (NSs) pow-ered by the decay of strong (10 14 –10 15 G) internal magnetic fields (Duncan & Thompson 1992; Thompson & Duncan 1995; Popov & Postnov 2013; Beloborodov 2020; Lyubarsky 2020; Sobacchi et al. 2022). Nowadays, ≈ 3600 di ff erent sources of FRBs are known; the majority of them are classified as one-o ff events, which means that, over tens of hours on-source, they have been detected only once. A small ( ∼ 2%) fraction of the whole FRB popula-tion has been seen to repeat (CHIME /FRB Collaboration et al. 2026). It is not clear whether the latter sources, known as "re-

Article number, page 1 arXiv:2609.35729v1 [astro-ph.HE] 28 Sep 2026 A&A proofs: manuscript no. aa61106-26corr 

peaters", originate from a distinct class of progenitors from one-off s. Bursts from repeaters appear statistically longer in duration and less extended in frequency (Pleunis et al. 2021; Sand et al. 2025), while population studies suggest that a large fraction of one-o ff s may eventually repeat (James et al. 2022a; James 2023; CHIME /FRB Collaboration et al. 2023). Di ff erences have been found among the population of repeating FRBs, especially in their level of activity, with burst rates ranging from 10 −3 hr −1 to 10 2 hr −1 (CHIME /FRB Collaboration et al. 2023) at Jansky mil-liseconds fluence threshold. Moreover, deep sensitivity observa-tions at ≈ 1 mJy ms fluence limits revealed that some FRBs, re-ferred to as “hyperactive repeaters”, can show periods of activity characterized by burst rates as high as 500 hr −1 (e.g., Li et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023). The precise localization of FRBs enabled four repeaters to be robustly linked with persistent radio sources (PRSs; Chatterjee et al. 2017; Marcote et al. 2017; Niu et al. 2022; Bhandari et al. 2023; Bruni et al. 2024; Ibik et al. 2024b; Moroianu et al. 2026). The latter resemble synchrotron-emitting nebulae and are too luminous ( Lν ≥ 10 29 erg s −1 Hz −1, Law et al. 2022) and compact (on linear scales of parsecs; Marcote et al. 2017; Bhandari et al. 2023) to originate from star formation pro-cesses. The first discovered PRS is the one associated with FRB 20121102A, hereafter R1, which was the first discovered re-peater (Chatterjee et al. 2017; Marcote et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2023). This is one of the most active FRBs known and has been studied for ∼ 14 yr up to now (e.g., Li et al. 2021; Hewitt et al. 2022; Jahns et al. 2023). R1 is located in a star-forming dwarf galaxy at z ≃ 0.19273 (Tendulkar et al. 2017) and its bursts show a time-varying rotation measure (RM) of ∼ 10 5 rad m −2, which suggests an extremely variable and highly magnetized environ-ment near the source producing the bursts (Michilli et al. 2018); this supports the hypothesis that a young magnetar (10 −40 yrs) is the central engine of R1 (Bassa et al. 2017; Margalit & Metzger 2018). The associated PRS has a position consistent with the burst’s position within ∼ 12 pc (Snelders et al. 2025), and presents a nonthermal emission with a flat spectral index, α =

−0.27 ± 0.24, similar to the spectral energy distribution (SED) of the nebula around PSR B0531 +21 (Crab pulsar), although with orders of magnitude higher luminosity (Resmi et al. 2021; Vohl et al. 2023). The largely accepted model for a PRS is a strongly magnetized nebula powered by a young, FRB-emitting magnetar (Margalit & Metzger 2018), but a hypernebula inflated by accretion onto a compact binary system (Sridhar et al. 2021, 2024) is also consistent with current observations. A second, similar PRS was found for FRB 20190520B, often referred to as R1-twin for the similarities it shares with R1. It is another active repeater localized in a dwarf galaxy at z ≈ 0.241 (Niu et al. 2022), with bursts showing RM values of the same orders of magnitude as R1 (Niu et al. 2022; Anna-Thomas et al. 2023). In this case as well, the emission is compact at physi-cal scales < 9 pc (Bhandari et al. 2023), and nonthermal, with a spectral index α = −0.41 ± 0.04 (Niu et al. 2022). More recently, two other PRSs have been discovered, both associated with two repeating FRBs: FRBs 20190417A (Ibik et al. 2024b; Moroianu et al. 2026) and 20240114A (Bhusare et al. 2025; Bruni et al. 2025). Both the new FRB-PRS systems reside in dwarf galaxies. Moreover, FRB 20190417A exhibits a large and strongly time-variable of RM (Mckinven et al. 2023), while FRB 20240114A is one of the most active FRBs known (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024) but shows bursts with a significantly smaller RM values (Bruni et al. 2025). Interestingly, the spectral luminosities of confirmed PRSs show a positive correlation with the FRB RMs (e.g., Bruni et al. 2024; Ibik et al. 2024b). This correlation is predicted under the hypoth-esis that the PRS is a magnetized nebula that is both responsible for the continuum radio emission and the Faraday rotation of the bursts (Yang et al. 2020, 2022; Bruni et al. 2024; Yang 2026). Significant observational e ff orts have been carried out to search for other PRSs (e.g., Vohl et al. 2023; Dong et al. 2024; Pelliciari et al. 2024; Bhusare et al. 2025; Ibik et al. 2024b). Re-markable cases are the repeating FRBs 20201124A (Bruni et al. 2024), 20181030A (Ibik et al. 2024b), and 20220912A (Pelliciari et al. 2024; Bhusare et al. 2025), for which co-spatial compact radio sources have been reported. We investigate the compactness of the candidate PRS associated with FRB 20181030A in a companion work (Paper II; Pelliciari et al. 2026). However, in these cases the PRS–FRB association remains tentative because the continuum sources have not been confirmed as compact at parsec scales (Nimmo et al. 2022; Hewitt et al. 2024), which implies they might not be related to the FRB. In this work, we present new observations conducted with the upgraded Giant Meterwave Radio Telescope (uGMRT) at 1.26 GHz, searching for PRSs in a sample of 24 FRBs that con-tain both repeating and one-o ff sources. We combined our re-sults with existing observations to construct the largest catalog of FRB–PRS systems to date and used it to study the PRS oc-currence rate. The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we present the FRB sources selected for our observational cam-paign and outline their properties, and in Section 3 we describe the uGMRT observations and data reduction. In Section 4 we present the extended FRB–PRS catalog and report the main re-sults of our analysis, including the detection of new candidate PRSs. In Section 5 we derive the PRS occurrence rate for the population of sources included in the extended catalog. We sum-marize our findings and draw conclusions in Section 6. 

2. Sample description 

We selected a sample of 24 FRBs, comprising 13 repeaters and 11 one-o ff sources, which we identify as S1, S2... up to S24. They have di ff erent localization accuracies and the position ac-curacy is better than 1 arcsec for 13 sources (S1, S5, S6, S9, S14, S15, S16, S17, S18, S19, S21, S22, and S24). Among them, 11 sources have a confirmed host galaxy association, with a spectro-scopic redshift measured for the latter, but two sources (S21 and S24) do not have a reported redshift. Four other sources (S2, S3, S4, and S8) have less accurate localizations (up to ≈ 1arcmin), but are still associated with host galaxies. The remaining seven sources have position accuracies better than 1 arcmin with no host association. Six of these were selected because of their mea-sured RMs, which span |RM | ≈ 180 −1.2×10 4 rad m −2 (see Paper III for the individual RM values). The FRB hosts (where known), span a large variety of galaxy types, including star-forming galaxies (SFGs), dwarf galaxies, and systems hosting active galactic nuclei (AGNs). The sources without a secure host galaxy association are S7, S10, S11, S12, S13, S20, S23, and S24. All the relevant properties of the sources of our catalog are reported in Table A.1, while their localization properties are reported in Table A.2. We estimated the redshift of the sources without ahost galaxy association by using the Macquart relation (Macquart et al. 2020a), which relates the DM due to the inter-galactic medium (IGM) DM IGM and the FRB redshift:

3. Observations and data reduction 

We present new continuum observations of a sample of 24 FRB sources, conducted with the uGMRT at band-5 (1060– 1460 MHz). Each observation has been processed following a standard data reduction using the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO) Common Astronomy Software Applica-tions ( CASA ; McMullin et al. 2007) v. 6.7. Primary and sec-ondary calibrators used for each source are listed in Table A.3. The flux density scale was set according to the Perley & Butler (2017) scale. We carried out flagging, delay, bandpass, and amplitude calibration for each calibration source. Calibration solutions were then applied to target visibilities that were then Fourier transformed into images using WSClean (O ff ringa et al. 2014; Off ringa & Smirnov 2017) v. 3.6, which accounts for wide-field and multifrequency synthesis. Images were deconvolved to gen-erate sky models that were then used to carry out phase and amplitude self-calibration to improve the image fidelity. After self-calibration, visibilities were averaged in time (30 s) and fre-quency (eight channels). We generated images with di ff erent briggs weighting schemes for each target, with robust parameters of -0.5, 0, 0.5. Images with -0.5 robust parameter provide the highest resolution (typically ∼ 2′′ ) and noise (typically ∼ 30 μJy beam −1), whereas the choice of robust parameter 0.5 degrades the resolution (typi-cally ∼ 4′′ ) but improves the noise (typically ∼ 10 μJy beam −1). In the remainder of the paper, we consider images obtained with 

briggs 0.5 as references for the analysis, unless stated other-wise. The noise levels and angular resolutions of each image are listed in Table A.3.

We assumed α = −0.27, which is the spectral index of the PRS as-sociated with R1 (Chatterjee et al. 2017). In order to compute luminosity ULs from flux density up-per limits, we propagated uncertainties using a Monte Carlo ap-proach. For each source, a luminosity realization was computed using Eq. 3, where the redshift value was drawn from a Gaus-sian distribution centered on the measured value and with stan-dard deviation equal to the measured uncertainty. The procedure was repeated 10 4 times in order to ob-tain a distribution of luminosity values, whose 95 th percentile was taken to be the luminosity UL (Table A.1). 

4. Results and discussion 

We first describe the construction of the extended FRB cata-log used throughout the analysis, which combines new uGMRT observations at 1 .26 GHz with literature data. We then high-light the main results of the uGMRT observational campaign, while a detailed description of individual FRBs is provided in Appendix A. Throughout this work, we only classify as confirmed FRB-PRS systems the four FRBs currently known to be associated with a persistent radio source that is com-pact on parsec (or sub-parsec) scales and whose association has been firmly established through Very Long Baseline Inter-ferometry (VLBI) observations. These are FRBs 20121102A, 20190417A, 20190520B, and 20240114A (Marcote et al. 2017; Bhandari et al. 2023; Bruni et al. 2025; Moroianu et al. 2026). By contrast, we define a “PRS candidate” as a persistent source localized within the 95% CL positional uncertainty of a given FRB, with the prescription that it is unresolved in the highest-resolution uGMRT cleaned images we obtained (see Table A.3 for details on the resolutions we reached for each source).

4.2. Results from uGMRT campaign 

We report the detection of persistent radio emission in six FRB fields of our uGMRT sample, namely S5, S11, S12, S13, S20, and S22. Among them, S5 and S22 correspond to FRBs 20190417A and 20240114A, for which a confirmed persistent counterpart was already known (Bruni et al. 2025; Ibik et al. 2024b; Moroianu et al. 2026). The remaining detections repre-sent new PRS candidates associated with repeating FRBs, with the exception of S13, which is an apparently one-o ff source (Pastor-Marazuela et al. 2025). In order to label a source as compact in our observations, we verify that its integrated and peak flux densities are consistent within 1 σ uncertainties. All detected compact radio sources are shown in Fig. 2, and their properties are listed in Table 1. We also derive constraints on the PRS radius using the semimajor axis of the synthesized beam of the uGMRT images obtained with 

briggs weighting and robust parameter −0.5 (Table 1; see Ta-ble A.3 for details). S5 and S22 were already constrained at the parsec scale (Bruni et al. 2025; Moroianu et al. 2026). For new PRS candidates, we estimate the chance coincidence probability, 

Pcc , that the detected persistent source is unrelated to the FRB. The values for Pcc are listed in Table 1, while we describe the method to obtain and evaluate this probability in Appendix A. In our sample, S12 and S13 have Pcc < 0.1, whereas the higher val-ues found for S11 and S20 are primarily driven by the compar-

atively poor localization of their corresponding FRBs (see Table A.2). While a low Pcc strengthens the association between the FRB and the persistent source, a high Pcc mainly reflects the limited localization accuracy of the FRB rather than providing evidence against a physical association. Therefore, Pcc should be regarded as an indicator of the association robustness and not as a criterion for selecting promising PRS candidates for future follow-up observations. Recently, Mfulwane et al. (2026) reported radio continuum detections spatially coincident with the positions of 14 FRB sources, including four in common with our uGMRT sample (S14, S15, S17, and S18). According to the criteria adopted in this work, only S14 qualifies as a candidate PRS. Beyond the uGMRT sample, we include R1 (Chatterjee et al. 2017) and R1-twin (Niu et al. 2022) (the other two confirmed PRSs, S5 and S22, are already part of the uGMRT sample), and the PRS candidates FRBs 20201124A (Bruni et al. 2024) and 20181030A (Ibik et al. 2024b). We detect two confirmed PRSs, FRBs 20190417A (S5) and 20240114A (S22). We measure a 248 ± 20 μJy flux density for S5, ≈ 3.6σ higher than Ibik et al. (2024b), although the discrepancy is less pronounced ( ≈ 1.3σ) when compared with Moroianu et al. (2026); the di ff erences are further discussed in Bruno et al. (2026). We measure a 53 ± 11 μJy flux density for S22, consistent with a previous measurement at comparable fre-quencies (Zhang et al. 2025). 

> 4.2.1. Detection of new candidate PRSs associated with repeating FRBs

We find compact radio emission within the 95% localization un-certainties in three other repeating FRBs: S11, S12, and S20. S11 has a large localization uncertainty (4 .8′ × 1.8′ at 1 σ level; CHIME /FRB Collaboration et al. 2023), and we find three com-pact sources within its 95% CL localization region. We label them S11-1, S11-2, and S11-3. The projected angular separations from the FRB localization center are 211 ′′ , 119 ′′ , and 105 ′′ for S11-1, S11-2, and S11-3, re-spectively. All these sources are detected with a confidence level between 5 σ and 6 σ, and present spectral luminosities consis-tent with the confirmed PRSs (see Table A.3 and Table 1). We searched in the Panoramic Survey Telescope & Rapid Response System Dara Release 1 (Pan-STARRS DR1; Flewelling et al. 2020) and in the Tenth Release of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI DR10; Dey et al. 2019) and found no optical counterparts. Other radio sources are present within the localization el-lipse, but they appear extended, and we therefore exclude them as potential PRS candidates. For S12 there is only one bright compact source just outside the 95% CL localization uncertain-ties of the FRB. We detected it at ∼ 20 σ confidence, resulting in a peak flux density of 360 ± 18 μJy. Like the PRS candidates we report for S11, this source also lacks an optical counterpart and has a spectral luminosity that is consistent with confirmed PRSs. Finally, there is a compact radio source that lies southeast with respect to the FRB localization ellipse center of S20, within its 68% CL region, with ≈ 90 ± 17 μJy flux density. Remarkably, a ≈ 10 29 erg s −1 Hz −1 persistent source was predicted, given the high RM of ∼ 1.2 × 10 4 rad m −2 of the FRB source (Zhou et al. 2025). Considering a redshift of ≈ 0.3 for this source, the flux density of the compact source we find converts to 1 .9(8) × 10 29 

erg s −1 Hz −1. However, a more precise localization of the FRB source is needed in order to associate it with this particular com-pact radio source. Although the small number of confirmed systems prevents us from defining a robust FRB–PRS archetype, their shared ob-servational properties (flat spectra, dwarf-galaxy hosts, and gen-erally large RM values) provide useful diagnostics for assessing the candidates. S11 and S20 are particularly interesting given the large RM of their associated FRBs, i.e., ≃ 1350 rad m −2

(Ng et al. 2025) and ≃ − 1.2 × 10 4 rad m −2 (Zhou et al. 2025) for S11 and S20, respectively. Moreover, their spectral luminosities are broadly consistent with confirmed FRB-PRS systems. How-ever, no information is available regarding the host galaxies of these sources. Follow-up VLBI observations as well as a char-acterization of their spectral properties will be important to test their spectral properties, which are needed to assess their com-pactness on parsec scales.

6. Summary and conclusions 

In this work we present new uGMRT observations at 1 .26 GHz of a sample of 24 FRBs, of which 13 are confirmed repeaters and the remaining 11 are one-o ff sources. Our images reach a noise level in the 10 − 40 μJy, corresponding to a 1 .4 × 10 27 −

3.9×10 29 erg s −1 Hz −1 luminosity range, with arc-second angular resolution. We identify three new candidate PRSs potentially associ-ated with the repeating FRBs 20201114A (S11), 20201130A (S12), and 20230607A (S20), and one candidate associated with the one-o ff FRB 20210317A (S13). As these FRB positions are known at ∼ arcmin precision, the association with PRS sources remains uncertain. Although the number of confirmed systems is still too small to define a robust FRB–PRS archetype, their shared observational properties (flat radio spectra, dwarf-galaxy hosts, and, in most cases, large RM values) provide useful di-agnostics to assess how closely the candidates resemble the con-firmed population. Among our candidates, S20 is particularly in-teresting in this respect, as the associated FRB has a large RM of ∼ 1.2 × 10 4 rad m −2, comparable to those of several con-

firmed systems. S11 and S12 are also promising targets given their persistent-source luminosities and, in the case of S12, the relatively low chance-coincidence probability. However, the un-certain localizations of the majority of these FRB sources cur-rently prevents a more detailed investigation. Multifrequency ob-servations of these sources will be particularly important to es-tablish whether their radio spectra are consistent with the ap-proximately flat spectra of the confirmed PRSs, while higher-angular-resolution radio observations (i.e., VLBI) are needed to constrain their compactness at parsec linear scales. We combined our FRB sample with literature data and ob-tained a catalog of 75 FRB–PRS systems, comprised of 50 one-off sources and 25 repeaters. We used this catalog to study the PRS occurrence, and find that PRSs luminosities Lν ≥ 10 29 erg s−1 Hz −1 are rare in the overall FRB population, without distinc-tion between repeating and one-o ff sources. For new PRS candidates, we estimate the probability that the detected persistent source is an unrelated to the FRB as: 

Pcc = 1 − e−μ , (A.1) i.e. we quantify the likelihood of a chance coincidence as the Poisson probability that a background radio source aligns with the FRB localization region. Here μ = Σ (> F)×Ω is the expected number of extragalactic radio sources brighter than a given flux density F within a solid angle Ω, and Σ(> F) is the sky surface density of radio sources brighter than F, and Ω = πab , where are the minor and major semi-axis of the FRB localization area, the latter listed for each FRB source in Table 1. > A.1. FRB 20180301A (S1)

This is a repeating source discovered during Breakthrough Listen (Isaacson et al. 2017; Worden et al. 2017) observations of the Galactic Plane (Price et al. 2019), and then localized at sub-arcsecond precision to a star-forming galaxy at z = 0.3304 via VLA observations (Bhandari et al. 2022). The RM of this source is observed to vary in time, from first observations in 2018 reporting −3163 ± 20 rad m −2 (Price et al. 2019), passing through ≈ 530 rad m −2 one year later (Luo et al. 2020) to −68 ± 13 rad m −2 (Kumar et al. 2023) during 2020-2022 observations with Parkes. As stated in Luo et al. (2020), this high absolute RM value could be due to a narrow signal band-width of 40 MHz. The source also presents a possible positive derivative of RM with time (Luo et al. 2020; Kumar et al. 2021). The high observed RM, combined with its variability with time makes S1 an interesting FRB source to be searched for a persistent counterpart. VLA observations at 1 .5 GHz ruled out the presence of a PRS for this source down to a 3 σ flux density limit of 63 μJy beam −1 (Bhandari et al. 2022). With our observations we are able to rule out the presence of a PRS by reaching a ∼ 2 deeper flux density limit at similar frequencies, i.e. a spectral luminosity 2 σ limit of L1.2 ≤ 7.5 × 10 28 erg s −1

Hz −1.

> A.2. FRB 20180814A (S2) and FRB 20190303A (S3)

These repeating FRB sources have been discovered by CHIME /FRB and localized at sub-arcminute precision (Michilli et al. 2023). The most probable host for S2 is the spiral galaxy J042256.01 +733940.7 found in the Panoramic Survey Telescope & Rapid Response System (PanSTARRS) data archive 1 (PS1; Flewelling et al. 2020), at redshift 

z = 0.006835. The bursts of this source show a relatively high RM value of 700 ± 1 rad m −2 (Mckinven et al. 2023) . Within the localization uncertainties we find an unresolved source, consistent with the position of 20180814A-S5 reported in Ibik et al. (2024b), however with a ≈ 2× higher flux density. Although the observations reported in Ibik et al. (2024b) are conducted with the VLA at a ≈ 3 times better angular resolution, and that the same source has been reported as extended in their work. Overall, 20180814A-S5 is localized to a galaxy di ff erent than the most probable FRB host associated with S2, and so can be excluded as a PRS (Ibik et al. 2024b). Ibik et al. (2024b) found a radio source (20180814A-S1 in their work) spatially coincident with PanSTARRS J042256.01 +733940.7, not detected in our observations. The authors reported a 5 σ flux density limit of 17 .5 μJy beam −1, which is consistent with the peak flux density of 20180814A-S1. From our non-detection we place a 2 σ UL of 24 μJy beam −1 on the presence of a PRS, giving 

L1.2 ≤ 2.8 × 10 27 erg s −1 Hz −1.Regarding S3, this source is spatially coinci-dent with a pair of merging galaxies (see Fig. 5 of CHIME /FRB Collaboration et al. 2025), with the FRB source located in the most south-west galaxy of the pair, i.e. J135159.86 +480714.04, at a redshift of z = 0.0644. From this procedure, we measure a 2 σ flux density limit of 76 μJy beam −1 which translates to a spectral luminosity of 

L1.2 ≤ 8 × 10 27 erg s −1 Hz −1.

We note that UGC 1714 is an almost completely edge-on galaxy, which could potentially result in a significant contribution to the DM. The redshift we derived assumed an average host contribution of DM host = 100 pc cm −3, but this value can be higher for edge-on galaxies (see, e.g., Orr et al. 2024) . Considering the total observed DM of this FRB, DM FRB = 351 ± 1 pc cm −3

(Curtin & CHIME /FRB Collaboration 2024), and our esti-mates along the line of sight of DM ISM ≈ 48 pc cm −3 and DM halo ≈ 36 pc cm −3 (see Section 2), the extragalactic contribu-tion is DM EG = DM IGM + DM host ≃ 267 pc cm −3. In the limiting case where the extragalactic DM is entirely due to the host galaxy (i.e. DM IGM = 0 pc cm −3), the implied redshift of the FRB would be z ≥ 0.02. Given the large positional uncertainties of the FRB and the low overall available information on it, we discard the possibility that the FRB is associated with UGC 12714, and we provide a 2 σ spectral luminosity limit of 5.6 × 10 28 erg s −1 Hz −1 for the presence of a PRS, given that no compact sources are present in the source field. S24 is a repeating FRB initially reported in Tian et al. (2025), which has recently showed hyperactivity and, inter-estingly, a frequency-dependent burst rate (Tian et al. 2025). While the FRB source has been localized at arcsecond precision, its host galaxy is still unknown /undetected (Tian et al. 2025). Two unresolved optical sources from the DESI-DR10 catalog (Dey et al. 2019) are found just outside the edge of the 1 σ FRB localization region (see Fig. 4 of Tian et al. 2025), not providing any secure FRB–host association. From the DM of S24 bursts, i.e. ∼ 470 pc cm −3, we calculate a redshift of z ≈ 0.34, at which our 2 σ flux density threshold of 36 μJy beam −1 provide a lumi-nosity UL of L1.2 ≤ 9.8 × 10 28 erg s −1 Hz −1.
