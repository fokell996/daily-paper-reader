ABSTRACT We report the serendipitous discovery of J155543.7 −563102 (hereafter LPT J1555 −5631) with a period of 62.2 min in MeerKAT and ASKAP survey data. The source emitted bright (10-30 mJy), steep-spectrum ( α ∼ − 1.5), highly polarized radio pulses during a 15-hr active window in May 2022, with no other quiescent or pulsed emission detected before or after over ∼ 5 years. The mean light curve exhibits two alternating pulses per cycle. A main pulse (MP) and an interpulse (IP) separated by ∆ ϕ = 165 .4◦ with similar amplitudes, but different polarization properties: the wider MP (3.9%) is partially linearly and circularly polarized and shows a constant polarization position angle, while the narrow IP (1.1%) is ∼100% circularly polarized. We classify LPT J1555 −5631 as a near-orthogonal rotator, for which emission from both magnetic poles is visible during one rotation and the magnetic and rotation axes are almost perpendicular. The 14.6 ◦ deviation from an antipodal separation and the nearly constant polarization position angle suggest that the emission does not arise from the standard dipolar polar-cap geometry of ordinary pulsars, but instead originates within a broader, more complex magnetosphere. The observed properties of LPT J1555 −5631 can be accommodated by extended emission regions, and favor coherent emission mechanisms such as the electron cyclotron maser instability, although they do not uniquely determine the progenitor or emission physics. If these inferences apply more broadly to the LPT population, they could account for why their beam widths are decoupled from rotation periods, the prevalence of flat polarization position angles, and may favor an elevated incidence of orthogonal rotators. 

1. INTRODUCTION GCRT J1745 −3009 stands as the enigmatic prototype of a newly emerging class of long-period radio transients (LPT; Rea et al. 2026). It was first discovered in September 2002 during a wide-field monitoring program of the Galactic center region with the Very Large Array (Hyman et al. 2005). Five bright (1 Jy) but short (10 min) bursts were detected with a period of 77.13 min. The pulsed emission was steep spectrum and highly polarized, and was likely coherent in origin. GCRT J1745 −3009 had no counterpart at either X-ray or near-infrared wavelengths (Hyman et al. 2005; Kaplan et al. 2008). The lifetime of the radio source is constrained to be approximately 1.5 years. This estimate is based on two short observations by the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope which re-detected bursts from GCRT J1745 −3009 on September 2003 and March 2004 (Hyman et al. 2007; Roy et al. 2010). Despite on-going monitoring of the Galactic center (e.g., Polisensky et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2022) and the analysis of archival data going back to 1989 (Hyman et al. 2006), there has been no renewed activity from GCRT J1745 −3009. Published upper limits for a quiescent radio source at this position are 0.4 mJy at 1.4 GHz and 15 mJy at 330 MHz (Hyman et al. 2006).

2While GCRT J1745 −3009 remained an isolated anomaly for nearly two decades, the landscape of long-period radio transients has recently undergone a dramatic shift. Initiated by the discovery of GLEAM-X J1627 −5235 (Hurley-Walker et al. 2022a), the large increase in the number of LPTs today is being propelled by new synoptic imaging surveys (e.g., Murphy et al. 2021; Hurley-Walker et al. 2022b) and by powerful search and analysis tools (e.g., Horv´ ath et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2026), designed to bridge the intermediate time domain phase space between fast pulsars and slow transients (Murphy & Kaplan 2026). The newer LPTs (Rea et al. 2026; Pritchard et al. 2026; Rose et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2026b) share some similarities with the original GCRT J1745 −3009. Most if not all LPTS exhibit narrow, steep-spectrum polarized pulses with periods from minutes to hours (Rea et al. 2026). Their active lifetimes however vary from less than a month to years, even decades for a few (Hurley-Walker et al. 2023, 2024). Whether LPTs can reactivate after entering dormancy remains unclear. GCRT J1745 −3009 exhibited multiple bursts over ∼1.5 years, but has not been detected despite continued monitoring. No LPT has been observed to transition from an active to a dormant state and back again, and no LPT has been detected in a quiescent, non-pulsed state. While some sources lack multi-wavelength counterparts (e.g., Hurley-Walker et al. 2022a), others show optical evidence of white dwarf (WD) and M dwarf binary companions (Rodriguez 2025; de Ruiter et al. 2025; Rodriguez et al. 2026). In other LPTs a link to neutron stars (NS) is suggested by pulsar-like behavior including pulse/interpulse, mode switching, glitches, and quasi-periodic sub-pulse structure (e.g., Caleb et al. 2024; Kramer et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2025; Dong et al. 2025a). Long-period transients (LPTs) are currently defined phenomenologically rather than physically and thus leave many questions unanswered. Are the progenitors of LPTs a single object class with diverse observational states, or do they represent distinct populations of WDs and NSs, that simply overlap in the radio time-domain? Can their pulsed emission properties be explained by a pulsar-like coherent curvature radiation, or electron cyclotron maser emission, or some other alternate plasma mechanism? Furthermore, what physical triggers dictate the onset and duration of their intense active phases before many lapse back into dormancy? And can estimates of the rotation measure (RM) and dispersion measure (DM) constrain the immediate magneto-ionic environment around LPTs? Progress in addressing these questions requires that we increase the sparse sample of LPTs (Lee et al. 2026). In this paper we report on the serendipitous discovery of LPT J155543.7 −563102 (hereafter LPT J1555 −5631) with a 62-minute period. A study of its pulse morphology establishes LPT J1555 −5631 as a near-orthogonal rotator with a large-scale dipolar field that is significantly distorted from a pure dipole. The broad, non-canonical polar-cap beam geometry and extreme circular polarization place constraints on the emission mechanism and magnetospheric geometry that we use to draw comparisons with the broader LPT population. In §2 and §3 we describe the observations and data reduction. In §4 we summarize the key observational results and then in §5 we look to interpret the properties of LPT J1555 −5631, compare it to the existing sample of LPTs, and suggest avenues for future progress.

RADIO OBSERVATIONS 2.1. MeerKAT and ASKAP Discovery 

LPT J1555 −5631 was discovered as part of a search for steep spectrum, circularly polarized point sources following the methodology outlined in Frail et al. (2024). The original data came from a public archive 1 (Cotton et al. 2024) of the South African Radio Astronomy Observatory (SARAO), taken by the 64-element MeerKAT Radio Telescope Array (Jonas & MeerKAT Team 2016). Cotton et al. (2024) observed 36 high-latitude supernova remnants (SNR) in full Stokes at L-band (856-1712 MHz). The composite remnant G 326.3 −1.8 (MSH 15 −56) was observed as part of the SNR survey over an 8 hour period on 2022 May 14, with a total of 2 hours (4 x 30 minutes) of time-on-source. On the initial Stokes I and V images LPT J1555 −5631 appeared as a 0.49 mJy, polarized ( |V/I| = 34%), steep spectrum ( α = −1.1) source with a distorted point-spread function - suggestive of short-term variability. The position in Galactic coordinates is ( l, b )=(326.36 ◦, −2.27 ◦), or 0.52 ◦ from the center of the SNR. As there is a known pulsar powering G 326.3 −1.8 (Temim et al. 2017), it is unlikely that there is a physical association between LPT J1555 −5631 and the SNR. See Fig. 1 for full-field Stokes I and V images. By good fortune, LPT J1555 −5631 was observed contemporaneously by the 36-element Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP) as part of the Evolutionary Map of the Universe (EMU; Norris et al. 2021) 144 MHz-wide pilot survey at 1.367 GHz. We fitted the profile with a model consisting of two Gaussian components on a periodic domain plus a constant baseline (seven free parameters in total) using nonlinear least squares (Virtanen et al. 2020). Initial centroid positions were determined automatically from the two tallest peaks in the baseline-subtracted profile. The component with the wider extent is designated the main pulse as the amplitudes of the two components are statistically indistinguishable, making width the better discriminant. Fitted values are found in Table 2. The Gaussian centroids provided an initial estimate of the pulse phase offset and the absolute phase of each pulse within each cycle. Using this phase information to predict the expected arrival time of each individual pulse, we then identified the brightest data point above a signal-to-noise threshold of 5-σ within a window of ±8% of the period centered on each predicted pulse time. A straight-line fit T (n) = T0 + nP to these observed peak times by ordinary least squares yielded a refined period and a precise reference epoch T0, with uncertainties estimated from the RMS scatter of the timing residuals. Finally, the light curve was re-folded at the refined period and the two-Gaussian model was re-fitted to obtain the final profile parameters. We derive a best-fit period of 0 .04320 ± 0.00001 d (62 .20 ± 0.02 min) and a MJD reference epoch T0 = 59713 .4266 ±

0.0001 d. A second pulse arrives 0 .540 ± 0.001 cycles after the main pulse, offset from strict half-period by 0 .040 in phase (2 .5 min). Pulse widths at 50% and 10% of peak amplitude ( W50 and W90 ) were computed analytically from the fitted Gaussian widths. The main pulse is wider ( W50 = 2 .44 ± 0.09 min, W90 = 4 .44 ± 0.16 min) than the second pulse (W50 = 0 .66 ± 0.04 min, W90 = 1 .21 ± 0.08 min), consistent with the alternating pulse widths that originally suggested a main pulse/interpulse identification. The folded ASKAP polarization profile reveals a clear difference in polarization between the two alternating pulses (Fig. 6). One pulse is ∼100% negatively circularly polarized, while the other pulse exhibits a peak of ∼90% positive circular polarization at the start of the pulse and ∼75% linear polarization midway through. We did not attempt to fold-in the three MeerKAT pulses due to the very different bandwidths of the two telescopes. With these data it is not possible to provide a strong distance constraint to LPT J1555 −5631. We attempted to derive a dispersion measure by looking at individual bright pulses and sub-structure within pulses (Fig. 9). Unfortunately we were not able to measure a dispersive delay for such broad pulses at these frequencies and bandwidths, and the resulting upper limit on DM is not useful for a distance estimate. The RM can in principle provide a secondary distance estimate, as there is a rough scaling of DM with RM but with large scatter (e.g., Rea et al. 2026). From the ATNF Pulsar Catalogue v2.8.0 (Manchester et al. 2005) we identified four known pulsars within 1 to 2.5 degrees of LPT J1555 −5631 which have RMs of comparable magnitude (10–25 rad m −2). The DM-distances for these four pulsar ranges from 3.7 to 6.5 kpc, with a mean of 5.4 kpc. While the RM may vary smoothly on angular scales of degrees, there are significant RM variations at the scale of several arcminutes, especially at low Galactic latitudes (Thomson et al. 2026). Thus this distance should be used with caution in deriving any physical quantities. 2.3. Individual Pulse Analysis

In order to constrain the active lifetime of the radio source we downloaded relevant MeerKAT datasets and ASKAP images from the archives covering from 2019 to 2026, listed in Table 1. The largest number of epochs come from the ASKAP archive, totaling about 60 epochs. The suitability of epochs varies. While all are observed within the spectral window that defines L band, some are short integrations, some are pointed far from LPT J1555 −5631 , and in all but one (2022 June 25) our ephemeris is too inaccurate to determine whether a pulse should lie within the observing window. 2.4.1. ASKAP 

Following the procedure described in Section 2.2, we downloaded, imaged, and produced dynamic spectra from all relevant PAF beam visibility datasets from CASDA. Where an individual observation comprised multiple beams, data from these beams were primary-beam-corrected and averaged together (weighted by the primary beam correction, to upweight measurements made closer to the primary beam center). After channel-based RFI flagging, Stokes I light curves were formed by averaging along the frequency axis. The resulting light curves are shown in Fig. 10, folded on the ephemeris derived in §2.2. Due to the short time baseline over which the ephemeris could be calculated, the uncertainty swiftly propagates, rendering almost every observation potentially able to contain a pulse (light green 10 shading). Nevertheless, no pulses are seen, likely indicating that the source had a very short active window. The lower limit on this activity window of 15 hrs comes from the total duration of the joint MeerKAT and ASKAP May 2022 observations. An upper limit on the activity lifetime of 10 months comes from observations taken on either side of this epoch (2021 Nov. 06 and 2022 Sept. 07) with sufficient sensitivity and phase coverage to detect pulses (Table 1 and Fig. 10). To determine if the individual archival 887 MHz VAST images would have detected LPT J1555 −5631 , we estimated the flux for a typical pulse occurring in a 12 min observation. The 900 MHz flux in our 2 hr MeerKAT average spectrum is 0.8 mJy. For the VAST epochs, though, the average flux would be 2 mJy after scaling by the ratio of the 31 min period to the 12 min observation. The typical RMS noise levels of the VAST images is 0.3 mJy beam −1, which would therefore easily have detected a typical pulse. The bottom panel of Fig. 10 plots the brightest detections and the upper limits of the archival observations at the 10-s ASKAP native time resolution in comparison. In addition to the detection in May 2022, there were 56 other ASKAP observations toward LPT J1555 −5631 with no evidence for any earlier or renewed pulsed activity. The 3-σ upper limit for any pulses from LPT J1555 −5631 for the year-long interval after May 2022 is typically 4 mJy (Fig. 10). 2.4.2. MWA 

The Galactic Plane Monitor (Hurley-Walker et al. accepted) is a 200-MHz 72-epoch survey of the Galactic plane undertaken with the Murchison Widefield Array (MWA; Tingay et al. 2013; Wayth et al. 2018). Four 30-minute integrations were taken on a pointing containing LPT J1555 −5631 across 2022 June 02 to 2022 June 23 inclusive. No detection was made on any timescale to which the survey is sensitive (4 s to 30 min) with RMS noise levels of 100 mJy beam −1 to 5 mJy beam −1. Several LPTs have steep spectral turnovers that have rendered them invisible at low frequencies (Lee et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025), but due to the lack of simultaneity in the observing, it is unclear whether this non-detection yields a spectral or activity constraint. 2.4.3. MeerKAT 

In addition to the 2022 May 14 discovery epoch, the MeerKAT archives contain 11 L-band 10-min observations pointed within 0.7 deg (the 33% point of the L-band primary beam) of LPT J1555 −5631from the 2021 MPIfR Galactic Plane Survey setup program. The nearest of these to the discovery epoch was made on 2021 December 22. None detect LPT J1555 −5631. Two 15-min observations from the ThunderKAT program were made on 2019 November 30 and December 7. The RMS noise level of each is 80 μJy beam −1.2.4.4. Quiescent Counterpart 

In addition to searching for new radio pulses, we used the archival data to put 3-σ constraints on a quiescent radio counterpart to LPT J1555 −5631at several frequencies. We combined 39 epochs of ASKAP-VAST 12 min observations (Murphy et al. 2013) taken at 887 MHz between 2022 and 2026 for a point-source 3-σ limit of 0.20 mJy. We combined 3 epochs of ASKAP-POSSUM observations (Gaensler et al. 2010) taken at 1367 MHz in 2022 September for a limit of 70 μJy. We combined 3 epochs of ASKAP-EMU observations (Norris et al. 2011) taken at 943 MHz for a limit of 80 μJy. Combining the latter three Stokes V images resulted in a limit of 45 μJy for both. We imaged the off-pulse portions of the 2022 May 14 detections for both telescopes; the lowest limits achieved were with MeerKAT: 70 μJy (Stokes I) and 53 μJy (Stokes V). RESULTS LPT J1555 −5631 was initially discovered in the image plane at L-band as a steep spectrum, circularly polarized source with an anomalous point-spread function ( §2.1). At higher time resolution the source emits radio pulses with a well-defined period P=62.2 minutes. These pulses were only seen during a 15-hour window in May 2022. No subsequent detections of pulsations were made in the archival data spanning 2019 to 2026 to a typical 10-sec (ASKAP) and 8-sec (MeerKAT) 3-σ level of 4 mJy after scaling to 1 GHz. Upper limits on quiescent radio emission are 33 μJy in Stokes I and 45 μJy in Stokes V ( §2.4 and Fig. 10). Two distinct alternating pulses were seen from LPT J1555 −5631 ( §2.2 and Fig. 5), a main pulse (MP) and an interpulse (IP), separated by ∆ ϕ = 165 .4◦ of phase. While there is significant pulse-to-pulse variation (see Figs. 2

15 and 9), the amplitude ratio of the mean pulse profile is IP/MP=1.0 ±0.1 (Fig. 5 and Table 2). The main pulse and interpulse each have different polarization properties and pulse profiles (see Fig. 6 and Table 2). The MP has a fractional pulse width (or duty cycle) of w50/P=3.9%. The degree of linear polarization varies in phase across the MP with values from 20% to 70%, while the circular polarization varies from 0% to 90%. The IP is narrower than the main pulse with a fractional pulse width of 1.1%. While the linear polarization of the IP is weak ( <10%), it is almost 100% circularly polarized. The circular polarization in the MP and the IP are always of the opposite sign. The polarization position angle of the MP for the mean pulse was measured to be 142 ±1◦ and does not change with pulse phase. Within several bright main pulses we observe regularly spaced sub-pulses superimposed upon several single pulses all with a similar widths and a quasi-periodicity of order 20 to 30 seconds (Fig. 9). The wide MeerKAT band enabled us to determine the spectral indices (Fig. 8) for two IPs ( α = −2.3, −1.3) and one MP (-1.3). We derived an upper limit on the presence of X-ray emission but were unable to identify an optical counterpart ( §3). There is a faint optical point source just outside the nominal error circle (Fig. 11) but owing to stellar crowding at this low Galactic latitude ( b = −2.3◦) there is a significant non-zero probability of a chance coincidence. We are not able to derive a meaningful DM limit since the expected dispersive delay across the observing bandwidth is small relative to the 62-min period. Likewise, since we lack a long time-baseline, we are unable to measure a period derivative. We determine a rotation measure toward the source of 4.5 ±0.7 rad m −2. A weak distance constraint of 5.4 kpc is derived by comparing this RM with the average DM-based distances of known pulsars in the vicinity with similar RM values (§2.2).

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 5.1. Classification, Activity Windows and Quiescence 

Collectively, the properties summarized in §4 and Table 2 place LPT J1555 −5631 firmly into the phenomenologically-defined class of long period transients. In terms of its period, LPT J1555 −5631 is most similar to the LPTs ASKAP J175534.9 −252749.1 and ASKAP J1935+2148 (Caleb et al. 2024; McSweeney et al. 2025). In the absence of a robust distance estimate, we can use the radio to X-ray luminosity ratio ( νLν,radio /L X ) to locate LPT J1555 −5631 relative to other parent populations (Wang et al. 2025). With a mean 1.4 GHz flux density of ∼0.5 mJy and fx ≲2×10 −14 erg s −1

cm −2, LPT J1555 −5631 lies along a line where νLν,radio /L X ≤ 3.5 × 10 −4. Some caution is warranted as the X-ray and radio data for most LPTs including LPT J1555 −5631 are rarely taken concurrently. However, many other LPTs occupy this unfilled phase space with radio to X-ray ratios similar to or lower than LPT J1555 −5631 (Anumarlapudi et al. 2025, 2026). LPTs appear radio bright compared to the joint radio and X-ray properties of radio stars, most magnetars and X-ray binaries. For example, the G¨ udel–Benz relationship established for incoherent gyro-synchrotron stars and for luminous (coherent) RS CVn binaries (Vedantham et al. 2022), would predict a ratio of νLν,radio /L X at least 2.3 dex lower than our constraint on LPT J1555 −5631. The transient nature of the radio emission is widely regarded as a defining characteristic of the LPT class, yet the known population exhibits a surprisingly diverse range of activity cycles (Rea et al. 2026). While sources such as GLEAM-X J0704 −36 and GPM J1839 −10 have remained active for at least a decade, most LPTs appear to undergo much shorter episodes of radio activity lasting only months. Even during these active intervals the phenomenology varies considerably. Pulse amplitudes may be highly sporadic (Dong et al. 2025b), remain relatively stable (Pritchard et al. 2026), decline steadily with time (Lee et al. 2025), or transition abruptly between distinct emission states (Caleb et al. 2024). Several LPTs also exhibit secondary periodicities that modulate pulse arrival times or their amplitudes (Horv´ ath et al. 2026; Anumarlapudi et al. 2026). These observations suggest that emission is governed intrinsically by changes in LPT magnetospheres or in some cases extrinsically by a binary environment. Finite activity windows and dormant states are not unique to LPTs. NSs such as magnetars, repeating FRBs, RRATs, intermittent pulsars, and mode-changing pulsars all exhibit long-term changes in radio activity that have been interpreted as transitions between different magnetospheric states (Keane & McLaughlin 2011; Kaspi & Beloborodov 2017; Philippov & Kramer 2022; Ma & Gao 2025). In contrast, the coherent radiation from WD systems such as AR Sco and J1912 −4410 show regular radio pulses modulated on an orbital period, and accompanied by a persistent low-level component (Marsh et al. 2016; Stanway et al. 2018; Marcote et al. 2017). We are not aware of any WD analog that exhibits the complete disappearance of coherent radio pulses on the timescales inferred for many LPTs. In this respect, LPT J1555 −5631 appears broadly representative of the LPT population. Its radio-loud phase was confined to a short interval lasting at least 15 hr but no more than 10 months ( §2.4.1). During this episode, while the pulse-to-pulse amplitude showed variations with an RMS of order 30%, there is no evidence for the secular fading 16 seen in some LPTs (e.g., Lee et al. 2025). Outside this interval the source was radio quiet, with no pulses detected over ∼5 yr above a typical 3-σ threshold of ∼4 mJy ( §2.4.1). We cannot exclude the possibility that LPT J1555 −5631 entered a weaker emission state analogous to GCRT J1745 −3009 (Hyman et al. 2007) or the mode switching reported for ASKAP J1935+2148 (Caleb et al. 2024), since pulses an order of magnitude fainter than those observed during the active phase would fall below the sensitivity of our archival data (Fig. 10). Likewise, our limits on steady, non-pulsed emission ( §2.4.4 and Table 2) do not yet reach the luminosities measured from AR Sco or J1912 −4410 unless LPT J1555 −5631 lies closer than 0.4 kpc. 5.2. Magnetospheric Geometry and Emission Processes 

The bright (20-30 mJy), steep-spectrum ( α ≃ − 1.6) pulses with short duty cycles (1.1–3.9%) and high degrees of both linear and circular polarization ( >70%) from LPT J1555 −5631 argue for a coherent emission process such as curvature radiation or the electron cyclotron maser emission, requiring a compact, beamed emission region rather than a distributed, isotropic emitter (Melrose 2017). The detection of a main pulse and an interpulse with opposite circular polarization provides a model-independent geometric constraint. It implies that the emission beam is sampled twice during each cycle. In pulsars, such pulse/interpulse pairs are generally associated with radiation from opposite magnetic poles, requiring a nearly orthogonal geometry between the rotation and magnetic axes (i.e., α◦ ≃ 90 ◦). Such objects are called orthogonal rotators (Johnston & Kramer 2019). As was first noted by Lee et al. (2025), if the interpulse interpretation is correct, it implies that the observed period originates from rotation and not orbital motion. Strong support for this interpretation comes from the sign reversals of the Stokes V signal between the MP and IP (Fig. 6). Among pulsars, Johnston & Kramer (2019) have shown that this is a common property of orthogonal rotators, as the line-of-sight component of the magnetic field points toward the observer at one pole and away at the other (e.g, PSR B0906 −49; Kramer & Johnston 2008). LPT J1555 −5631 is the second after ASKAP J183950.5 −075635.0 (Lee et al. 2025) to be classified as an orthogonal rotator, or ∼15% of the LPT sample. LPT J1555 −5631 and ASKAP J183950.5 −075635.0, with rotation periods of 62.2 min and 6.45 hrs respectively, demonstrate that orthogonal rotators can be found across nearly the full range of LPT periods. Among the known pulsars this fraction is only 2-3% (Weltevrede & Johnston 2008; Maciesiak et al. 2011). Apart from small number statistics, what might explain the greater incidence of orthogonal rotators among LPTs? We argue below that the answer may be that the structure of the emission beam and the location of the emission region differs from canonical pulsar magnetospheres. Given the orthogonal rotator geometry, we can use the pulse morphology to probe the structure of the emission beam. For most pulsars, the radio emission is thought to arise from the polar cap region relatively low in the magnetosphere, resulting in an opening angle that scales as ρ = 5 .8◦/pP(s) (Rankin 1983). For LPT J1555 −5631, with P = 3732 s, the Rankin relation predicts a polar-cap half-angle of only ρ ≈ 0.095 ◦. Instead, the observed duty cycles of 1.1% (IP) and 3.8% (MP) imply wider beam half-angles of order ∼2–6 ◦ (i.e., for a near-orthogonal rotator with sin α◦ ≈ 1). This discrepancy of more than an order of magnitude demonstrates that the emission does not originate from a standard polar cap. If the emission is from a non-canonical polar-cap beam geometry, what does the MP-IP morphology reveal about the magnetic field topology? In a pure dipole, an orthogonal rotator should produce two anti-podal pulses separated by exactly ∆ ϕ = 180 ◦. The observed separation of ∆ ϕ = 165 .4◦ ±0.2◦ deviates from this ideal by ∆ ϕs = 14 .6◦. For pulsars like the Crab, relativistic aberration and retardation can account for reduced phase separations (Blaskiewicz et al. 1991), but these effects scale as ∆ ϕs ∝ v/c = Rem /R LC , where Rem is the characteristic radius in the magnetosphere at which the radio emission originates and RLC = cP/ 2π is the light-cylinder radius, while v is the corotation velocity at Rem and c is the speed of light. For LPT J1555 −5631 , RLC ≃ 1 AU, a distance where the magnetic field is too weak to generate or sustain radio emission for either a NS or WD. Thus, if the emission originates much closer to the stellar surface ( Rem ≪ RLC ), relativistic effects are negligible and cannot explain the 14 .6◦ deviation. Instead, the phase offset suggests some sort of asymmetry in the system; a beam asymmetry between the MP and IP emission regions, or geometric deviations from a pure dipole geometry such as an offset dipole or a non-dipolar magnetic field component. The latter may be similar to the twisted field configurations suggested for magnetars (Beloborodov 2009; Zeng et al. 2026). Offset dipoles and multipole field topologies are commonly invoked to explain the Zeeman tomography of magnetized WD binaries (Beuermann et al. 2007; Kawka 2018). Whatever the origin of the asymmetry, it need not be a population-wide property of LPTs since it is not required for the other orthogonal rotator ASKAP J183950.5 −075635.0 (i.e., ∆ ϕ = 177 .8◦ ± 3.0◦; Lee et al. 2025). For future events, stronger constraints can be placed 17 on magnetospheric geometry and the emission regions by looking for temporal changes in ∆ ϕ or frequency variations (aka radius-to-frequency mapping). This hypothesis is partially supported by the polarization position angle (PA) of the MP. The PA was measured to be 142 ◦ ± 1◦ and shows no significant variation with pulse phase (Fig. 6). For a standard pulsar with narrow conal beams, observing both a main pulse and an interpulse with near-equal amplitudes requires a highly improbable, finely-tuned viewing geometry (i.e., one in which the observer’s line of sight cuts through the center of both emission regions (e.g., Maciesiak et al. 2011; Johnston & Kramer 2019). We discuss a mechanism below that explains the similar IP/MP amplitude without requiring this improbable alignment. Consistent with the atypically wide pulse duty cycles discussed above, we note that a flat PA could arise if the emission region is azimuthally extended, producing a sheet-like or fan beam in which the line of sight samples a nearly constant magnetic longitude (Karastergiou & Johnston 2007; Wang et al. 2014). In §5.3 we discuss the possibility that flat or slowly varying PAs are a population-level property of LPTs. The high degrees of circular polarization ( >70%), the nearly constant polarization PA, and the departure from the standard pulsar polar-cap beam geometry motivate us to consider electron cyclotron maser emission (ECM) as one plausible coherent emission mechanism. ECM is a well-established instability that powers radio emission from cataclysmic variables (Barrett et al. 2020; Ridder et al. 2026), magnetized early-type stars (Das et al. 2018), M dwarfs and brown dwarfs (Villadsen & Hallinan 2019; Kao & Pineda 2022), and has been invoked for the two known “WD pulsars” AR Sco and J1912 −4410 (Marsh et al. 2016; Pelisoli et al. 2023). ECM differs from coherent curvature radiation in several important respects. Most notably, ECM is emitted approximately perpendicular to the local magnetic field, producing radiation tangential to a thin hollow cone (Das et al. 2019, 2024), whereas coherent curvature radiation is emitted tangentially to the magnetic field lines along the particle trajectory (Melrose 2017). Another key property of ECM is that it grows exponentially but saturates at a maximum brightness temperature when the maser extracts all available energy from the local electron population (Melrose & Dulk 1982; Treumann 2006). Standard coherent curvature radiation does not impose such a hard brightness ceiling, nor does it typically produce such high degrees of circular polarization (Johnston & Kerr 2018). If ECM is indeed the dominant emission mechanism, it may help explain the puzzling properties of the MP and IP of LPT J1555 −5631. The MP ( W50 = 2 .44 min) is nearly four times wider than the IP ( W50 = 0 .66 min), yet both reach approximately the same peak flux (IP/MP ∼ 1). If both emission regions operate in the saturated regime, the maser intensity is limited by the available free energy in the local electron distribution rather than the size of the emitting region. Two emission components of different pulse widths could therefore exhibit similar peak brightnesses while differing substantially in duration. Our interpretation remains tentative because the mean pulse profile of LPT J1555 −5631 is based on only 11 rotations, whereas pulsar mean profiles are typically averaged over hundreds or thousands of pulses. Nevertheless, if future orthogonal-rotator LPTs exhibit similarly different pulse widths but comparable peak brightnesses, it would provide strong support for saturated ECM emission and could provide an observational diagnostic for distinguishing between the two coherent emission mechanisms. 5.3. Population-Level Comparisons and Future Work 

With the geometry, beam structure, and emission mechanism constrained, we look more generally to discriminants of LPT progenitor models, starting with quasi-periodic sub-pulse structure (QPSS; Wang et al. 2026a). We identified QPSS in three of the brighter pulses from LPT J1555 −5631 ( §2.3 and Fig 9) and measure quasi-periods P μ=20-30 s. As the relation predicts a value of Pμ = 2 .7 ± 1.1, LPT J1555 −5631 lies significantly above the extrapolation of the 

P –Pμ relation established by Kramer et al. (2024) for NSs. Similar deviations have been reported for other LPTs (Dong et al. 2025a). Since QPSS appears to be nearly ubiquitous among radio-emitting NSs, its detection in LPT pulses has been argued to provide strong evidence for a NS progenitor (Lee et al. 2025; Dong et al. 2025a). However, because the physical origin of QPSS remains poorly understood (Wang et al. 2026a, and references therein), its presence may not be unique to neutron-star magnetospheres (Rea et al. 2026). Indeed, GPM J1839 −10 illustrates this ambiguity: Hurley-Walker et al. (2023) reported QPSS and used it to place the source on the pulsar P –Pν relation, yet Pelisoli et al. (2025) and Horv´ ath et al. (2026) have instead argued that the system contains a WD, not a NS. Additionally, QPSS has been claimed from the LPT WD-M star binary GLEAM-X J0704 −37 (Hurley-Walker et al. 2024). If more examples of QPSS continue to be found among the WD LPT sub-class, and more are shown to deviate from the P –Pμ relation, 18 then it implies that the magnetospheric plasma microphysics in WDs is capable of producing small-scale emission structure tied to their rotation that is unrelated to QPSS in NSs. In aggregate, the pulse properties of LPT J1555 −5631 favor a magnetized compact object, slowly rotating with a period of 62.2 minutes, in a magnetospheric regime distinct from both standard pulsars and known WD pulsar systems. The detection of a MP and IP with opposite circular polarization implies a near-orthogonal rotator geometry. The large-scale field topology is dipole-like, but a significant multipolar or distorted component (e.g., an offset dipole) is required to account for the 165.4 ◦ phase separation and the asymmetric pulse widths. The emission does not originate from a polar cap but from elsewhere in the magnetosphere ( Rem ≪ RLC ), where it produces broad, saturated ECM beams. This magnetospheric picture explains the origin of the MP and IP, the reversal in the sign of Stokes V between them, their 165.4 ◦ phase offset, and their similar mean peak amplitudes. Note that to produce ECM at the first harmonic of the gyro-frequency at the observed 1.4 GHz requires magnetic fields of ∼300–500 G (G¨ udel 2002). Such fields can be achieved within 2–3 WD radii from the surface of a highly magnetized WD ( B ≃ 10 9 G; Ferrario et al. 2015), or alternatively at altitudes of ∼10 4 km in a NS magnetosphere with B ∼ 10 12 G surface field. Thus, ECM is viable for either progenitor. If this magnetospheric picture applies to LPTs generally, it may explain two intriguing population-level trends: the apparently high incidence of orthogonal rotators and the prevalence of flat polarization position angles. In ordinary pulsars, the Rankin relation ( ρ ∝P−1/2) predicts that long-period objects should have narrow polar-cap beams and therefore rarely be observed as orthogonal rotators (Rankin 1983; Weltevrede & Johnston 2008). Although the current LPT sample is still small and subject to significant discovery and classification biases, the known population appears to depart from this expectation. LPT duty cycles span ∼1.5%–15% with no obvious dependence on period (McSweeney et al. 2025), and two of the currently identified LPTs have been interpreted as orthogonal rotators (Lee et al. 2025). Whether this reflects a genuine physical difference, small-number statistics, or an observational selection effect remains unclear. If future discoveries confirm these trends, broad, non-canonical polar-cap beam geometry, whether from extended fan-like emission regions or from magnetic interaction zones in binaries would accommodate both the large duty cycles and the high fraction of orthogonal rotators, while removing the expected correlation between beam opening angle and rotation period. Likewise, flat or slowly varying PAs are equally widespread among LPTs. Sources such as ASKAP J075024 −205945, GLEAM-X J1627 −5235, and ASKAP J1935+218 show little or no PA swing (Hurley-Walker et al. 2022a; Caleb et al. 2024; Anumarlapudi et al. 2026). The two proposed WD systems GPM J1839 −10 and ASKAP J1745 −5051 both have flat PAs but with short-duration phase jumps (Hurley-Walker et al. 2023; Rose et al. 2026). The orthogonal rotator ASKAP J183950.5 −075635.0 exhibited U-shaped polarization swings similar to some pulsars (Lee et al. 2025). ARVM fit was made to ASKAP J142431.2 −612611 but it provided fit parameters atypical of most pulsars and it fails to account for the time-evolution of the circular and linear polarization (Pritchard et al. 2026). Two broad classes of explanation have been proposed for these flat PAs. In the first, the PA is determined by the magnetospheric geometry rather than by a dipole field: azimuthally extended (fan-beam) emission regions (Karaster-giou & Johnston 2007; Wang et al. 2014), twisted or multipolar fields (Kramer et al. 2007), or binary interaction regions (Qu & Zhang 2025) all produce a nearly constant projected magnetic orientation along the line of sight. In the second, the PA is set by the emission mechanism itself, i.e, saturated electron cyclotron maser emission yields highly beamed, nearly orthogonal polarization that is insensitive to the large-scale field geometry. Both explanations find support in other radio-loud compact objects. Flat PAs are common among FRBs, where the line of sight does not appear to cut through a changing magnetic field configuration (Liu et al. 2025), and among radio-loud magnetars such as XTE J1810 −97,which shows a MP-IP pair with a slowly rising PA (Camilo et al. 2007). Whether LPTs share the magnetar-like non-dipolar fields invoked for XTE J1810 −197 (Kramer et al. 2007) or instead represent a distinct electrodynamic regime remains to be determined. While no single observation conclusively identifies LPT J1555 −5631 as either a NS or a WD, its pulse properties serve as a stringent stress-test for models of LPT magnetospheres. The collected radio properties find closer analogs with the NS population (pulsars, RRATs, FRBs, and magnetars), but this may simply reflect the much larger and longer-studied NS population rather than any true physical distinction. The well-known difficulty for a NS model is that for any reasonable value of the B-field, the 62.2-min rotation period places LPT J1555 −5631 far beyond the pulsar death line where standard pair-production and coherent radio emission mechanisms are thought to cease (Rea et al. 2026). As we have shown here, the standard rotation-powered pulsar model also struggles to self-consistently explain all the pulse properties of LPT J1555 −5631. Sustaining beamed radio emission at such long periods is consistent 19 with either a highly twisted, multipolar magnetic field to locally enhance the electric field near the NS surface, or an alternative coherent emission mechanism that does not rely on a polar-cap accelerator (Cooper & Wadiasingh 2024). Conversely, a magnetic WD progenitor accommodates the hour-long rotation period and wide emission beams. However, there has been no detection of coherent emission from isolated WDs despite sensitive searches (e.g., Zhang et al. 2025). Thus the ECM mechanism in WD systems appears to require a binary interaction either through unipolar induction, wind coupling, or accretion to supply the energetic particles and current systems needed for maser amplification (Geng et al. 2016; Qu & Zhang 2025; Yang 2026; Ferrario 2026). For LPT J1555 −5631, no companion has been identified, and the 62.2 min spin period is below the ∼78 min orbital period minimum for hydrogen-burning, Roche lobe-filling cataclysmic variables (Paczynski & Sienkiewicz 1981; G¨ ansicke et al. 2009). A detached WD binary with a longer orbital period remains possible, but would require an unseen companion. A similar binary WD model was proposed for GPM J1839 −10 but in this case these was evidence for both a rotation period (P r =22 min) and an orbital period (P o=8.75 hrs) (Pelisoli et al. 2025; Horv´ ath et al. 2026). LPT J1555 −5631 has shown that regardless of whether the central engine is an ultra-long-period magnetar or a magnetic WD binary, the system operates in a distinct electrodynamic regime compared to known source classes. In this regime, the light cylinder is located at vast distances from the surface, the standard period/opening angle scaling laws break down, and the radio emission geometry is consistent with either local surface multipoles, offset dipoles, or extended binary interaction regions rather than simple dipoles. Several avenues appear promising for future research of LPT J1555 −5631. The first is continued radio monitoring to look for re-activation and to place deeper limits on any quiescent emission. Next, deeper X-ray monitoring (ideally concurrent with radio monitoring) is needed, as well as a proper study of the optical source closest to LPT J1555 −5631 to look for any orbital modulation and to better constrain its photometric and spectroscopic properties.
