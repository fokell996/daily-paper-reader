ABSTRACT 

Calvera (1RXS J141256.0+792204) is an isolated neutron star recently classified as a central compact object (CCO) after its association with the supernova remnant (SNR) candidate G118.4+37.0. In this work, we investigate the long-term evolution and descendants of Calvera and the CCOs with similar initial conditions in the fallback disc model. We show that the observed spin period, period derivative, and X-ray luminosity of Calvera can be reproduced simultaneously with a magnetic field strength of 

≃ 4 × 10 10 G at the pole of the neutron star at an age of ∼ 10 4 yr which is consistent with the estimated SNR age of the source. In the model, the lack of ordinary radio pulsations is due to ongoing mass accretion on to the star. From our simulations, we estimate that the source will become an ordinary radio pulsar (RP) after the termination of the accretion. The source will spin down under the weak dipole torque alone as an RP for a very long time ( ≳ 10 8 yr) after the inactivation of the disc. From our simulations, we find that most of the CCOs with initial conditions similar to those of Calvera also become RPs which remain above or close to the upper border of the pulsar death valley after the inactivation of their discs. In the period–period derivative diagram, there is indeed a cluster of RPs in the region where the descendants of Calvera-like CCOs are estimated to be located in our model. 

Key words: accretion, accretion discs–stars: neutron–pulsars: individual: 1RXS J141256.0+792204 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Central compact objects (CCOs) are isolated neutron stars (INSs) found near the centres of supernova remnants (SNRs). They are characterized by steady thermal X-ray emission with spectrum that can be fit by two blackbodies with temperatures in the range of 

∼ 200 − 500 eV with emitting radii ranging from ∼ 0.1 to a few km. Their typical X-ray luminosities are in the 𝐿 X ∼ 10 32 −34 erg s −1 range (De Luca 2017) 1. They have no associated pulsar wind nebulae and could not be detected at optical, infrared, and radio wavelengths. Recently, very faint and highly variable pulsed radio emission was detected from a CCO, 1E 1207.4–5209 (Zhang et al. 2026). No radio emission has been detected from the source in the archival data, which indicates that 1E 1207.4–5209 could be a transient radio source (Zhang et al. 2026). Among the ∼ 10 confirmed CCOs, only three have measured spin periods, 𝑃 ∼ 0.1 − 0.4 s, and period derivatives, 

¤𝑃 ∼ ( 0.73 − 2.2) × 10 −17 s s −1 (Gotthelf et al. 2005; Halpern & Gotthelf 2010; Gotthelf et al. 2013a). Their 𝐿 X is estimated to be one to two orders of magnitude higher than their rotational power,

In this dipole torque model, these 𝐵 0 values together with the measured periods are sufficient for pulsed radio emission (Chen & Ruderman 1993). However, none of the CCOs have radio counterparts (De Luca 2017). The lack of ra-dio pulsations is generally attributed to the beaming geometry in this model (Halpern & Gotthelf 2010). If CCOs are not intrinsically radio silent, their immediate descendants could be found among ordinary radio pulsars (RPs). There are RPs with 𝐵 0 values, inferred from their 𝑃 and ¤𝑃 , lower than and similar to those of the three CCOs. It was proposed that some of these sources could be disrupted recycled pulsars (DRPs) (Lorimer 2008). DRPs are estimated to be NSs that were mildly recycled through accretion in high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs), which were subsequently disrupted by a second super-nova explosion (Lorimer et al. 2004; Lorimer 2008; Tauris & van den Heuvel 2023). It was suggested that some of these RPs could be immediate descendants of CCOs instead of DRPs, and their cooling luminosity could be detected in X-rays (Gotthelf et al. 2013b). To test this idea, they analysed 14 RPs, but could not detect any CCO-like X-ray emission, which indicates that these RPs cannot be immediate descendants of CCOs (Gotthelf et al. 2013b). Another mechanism proposed to explain the weak dipole fields and the long-term evolution of CCOs is the magnetic field burial scenario (Ho 2011; Viganò & Pons 2012). In this scenario, the magnetic field of sources is initially buried by the fallback material from the SN, and subsequently re-emerges, that is, the dipole field strength grows gradually in time (Geppert et al. 1999; Geppert et al. 2013; Ho 2011; Viganò & Pons 2012). For Calvera, Rigoselli et al. (2024) suggested that the field growth with a shorter diffusion time associated with a smaller fallback mass relative to those of the other pulsating CCOs could explain both its position in the 𝑃 − ¤ 𝑃 diagram and its stronger dipole field. In this case, the young descendants of CCOs could possibly be among the RPs with dipole fields stronger than those of known CCOs (Gotthelf et al. 2013b). To test this idea, Luo et al. (2015) observed 12 RPs with 𝐵 0 ≲ 2 × 10 11 G and could not detect any thermal X-ray radiation. They concluded that these sources are not likely to be immediate descendants of CCOs either (Luo et al. 2015). In the fallback disc model, the torques acting on the NSs and the 

𝐵 0 values estimated from their observed rotational properties are rather different from those estimated in the dipole torque model. Fallback discs could form around newborn NSs after supernova ex-plosions (Colgate 1971; Michel 1988; Chevalier 1989; Perna et al. 2014). It was suggested that NSs evolving with fallback disc can explain the observed 𝐿 X levels and 𝑃 clustering of anomalous X-ray pulsars (AXPs) (Chatterjee et al. 2000). Alpar (2001) proposed that long-term evolutions of INSs with the initial conditions including the properties of fallback discs in addition to initial 𝑃 and 𝐵 0, could explain the emergence of not only AXPs, but also other INS popula-tions during the long-term evolution. Fallback disc emission has been investigated extensively for different INS systems (Perna et al. 2000; Ertan & Çalışkan 2006; Ertan et al. 2007; Ertan et al. 2017; Posselt et al. 2018). Characteristics of pulsed hard and soft X-ray radiation from AXPs were modelled by the spectral properties of emission from the accretion column and the poles of the NS (Trümper et al. 2010, 2013; Kylafis et al. 2014; Zezas et al. 2015). The fallback disc model was developed by including the effects of X-ray irradiation of the disc, the contribution of the cooling luminosity to the irradiation flux, and the inactivation of the disc at low temperatures to investi-gate the long-term evolution of different INS populations (Ertan et al. 2009, 2014; Benli & Ertan 2016, 2017, 2018a,b; Gençali & Ertan 2018, 2021; Gençali et al. 2022, 2023). Based on the results of these earlier work, the evolutionary links between the INS populations were studied in detail by Gençali & Ertan (2024). In the fallback disc model, the 𝑃 , ¤𝑃 , and 𝐿 X values of CCOs can be reproduced with relatively low 𝐵 0 ∼ ( 2–5) × 10 9 G, while there is currently an ongoing mass accretion on to the magnetic poles of NS (Benli & Ertan 2018b). The radio emission is expected to be quenched by the mass-flow on to the NS, which is the reason for the lack of radio pulsations from CCOs in this model. After the termination of the accretion, they could become RPs provided that their rotational power is still sufficient for the pulsed radio emission. In an earlier work, it was suggested that the descendants of CCOs could be RPs, rotating radio transients (RRATs) or they may not be observable in any energy band (Gençali & Ertan 2024). RRATs are INS systems that show repetitive short radio bursts with durations much shorter than their spin periods. The physical mechanism behind these bursts is not well known (McLaughlin et al. 2006). Penetration of the inner disc into the light cylinder could enhance the voltage across the magnetic poles (Parfrey et al. 2016). It was suggested that this voltage enhancement by the field-disc interaction may trigger RRAT bursts in INS sources that would actually be dead pulsars if they had no discs (see Gençali & Ertan 2024, for details). From the preliminary results of our ongoing work, we estimate that CCOs could evolve into RPs, RRATs, or cool below current detection limits without showing any radio or X-ray emission (Ş. Demirok, Ü. Ertan, A. A. Gençali, in preparation). Using the results of these analyses, in the present work, we will focus on the long-term evolution of Calvera and CCOs with initial conditions similar to those of Calvera. We will also discuss the possible descendants of these sources. Unlike the three other CCOs with measured 𝑃 and ¤𝑃 , Calvera lies well above the upper border of the pulsar death valley (Chen & Ruderman 1993), while the source, like other CCOs, shows no regular radio pulsations (see e.g. De Luca 2017; Rigoselli et al. 2024). From our model results, we estimate that Calvera is spinning down by the disc torques while the mass accretion on to the NS is going on quenching the radio pulses at present. The source is likely to become an RP after the accretion is switched off. In Section 2, we briefly describe the model. Our results are discussed in Section 3 and conclusions are summarized in Section 4. 

2 THE MODEL 

Here, we briefly describe the long-term evolution model applied ear-lier to different INS populations (see e.g. Ertan et al. 2014; Benli & Ertan 2016) and the analytical torque model (Ertan 2017, 2018, 2021). In the earlier work, the long-term evolution and evolutionary links of the INS populations, including CCOs, have been investigated in detail (Gençali & Ertan 2024). The three CCOs with known 𝑃 and ¤𝑃 

values are estimated to be evolving at points below or inside the death valley (delineated by the red diagonal solid lines in Fig. 2) and very close to the lower border when the WP phase terminates. Therefore, the other CCOs within the 𝑃 and ¤𝑃 ranges of these three sources are not likely to become RPs even after the termination of the accretion phase. The location of Calvera on the 𝑃 − ¤ 𝑃 diagram (filled star in Fig. 2) is rather different from those of the other three CCOs. Indeed, it could not be identified as a CCO until the discovery of the SNR around the source. This could indicate that there could be CCOs starting their evolutions with the initial conditions similar to that of Calvera. Pursuing this idea, we have investigated the long-term evolution of the sources starting their evolution within the red region seen in Fig. 2. We have chosen some illustrative initial conditions in the red region. The red region in Fig. 2 corresponds to a parameter space with a 𝐵 0 range of 3 × 10 9 − 1 × 10 11 G and 𝑃 0 range of 20 − 100 ms. The long-term rotational evolution of the sources is not very sensitive to 𝑀 d (Benli & Ertan 2016). We have obtained all these model curves with 𝑀 d ≃ 6.3 × 10 −7 𝑀 ⊙ . The evolution of these illustrative model sources with active disc terminates in the grey region as RPs. If the end point of the evolution is inside the death valley, the source could become an RP depending on the location of its death point inside the valley. It is seen in Fig. 2 that most of the sources initially in the red region find themselves either above the upper border of the death valley or inside and close to the upper border of the valley. This means that a large fraction of the CCOs born in the red region are likely to become RPs after termination of the accretion in this model. After the inactivation of their discs, they are estimated to remain observable as RPs without a significant change in their 𝑃 

and ¤𝑃 for a very long time ( > 10 8 yr). Indeed, there is a cluster of RPs in the grey region. Some of these sources were proposed earlier to be DRPs, which are NSs mildly recycled in HMXBs. Our results imply that some of these RPs could be descendants of CCOs, initially similar to Calvera. In our picture, Calvera is a very young source, while the descendant RPs (in the grey region) are likely to be very old sources. This means that their birth rate (Calvera-like CCOs) should be low considering that they should remain in the grey region as an RP for a long timescale. This is likely to be the reason for the lack of other CCOs in the red region around Calvera. Some of the RPs in the grey region could be the old ( 𝜏 > 10 8 yr) descendants of CCOs. In this region, which includes ∼ 15 old RPs, it is very unlikely to detect immediate descendants of CCOs in X-rays considering that their cooling luminosity could be detected at ages ∼

< 10 6 yr. Among these RPs, five sources were investigated in X-rays, and no X-ray emission was detected (Gotthelf et al. 2013b; Luo et al. 2015). For an order of magnitude estimate, let us assume that 10 out of 

15 RPs in the grey region are descendants of Calvera-like CCOs with lifetimes ∼ 10 8 yr. This gives a birth rate of 1 × 10 −7 yr −1. Taking the lifetime observable through cooling luminosity ∼ 10 6 yr and the RP lifetime ∼ 10 8 yr, the probability of observing a Calvera-like source in this sample is ∼ 1/10 . This is an interesting coincidence if there are indeed ∼ 10 Calvera-like sources among which only Calvera is detectable in X-rays. Note that the minimum birth rate of all CCOs estimated from SNR expansion time and the total number of CCOs is ∼ 0.04 century −1 (Kaspi 2010), much higher than we estimated for the Calvera-like CCOs. 

4 CONCLUSIONS 

We have investigated the long-term evolution and possible descen-dants of Calvera-like CCOs with the model applied earlier to other INS populations. We have shown that a NS evolving with a fallback disc and a dipole field strength 𝐵 0 ≃ 4 × 10 10 G can achieve the observed properties ( 𝑃 , ¤𝑃 , and 𝐿 X) of Calvera consistently with its estimated SNR age ( ∼ 10 4 yr). In the model, Calvera is evolving in the WP phase at present, and the accretion on to the star in this phase can account for the lack of radio pulses from the source. Our simulations indicate that the source will become an RP after the ter-mination of the WP phase at a time 𝜏 ≃ 2 × 10 4 yr. The source will remain as an RP for a very long time ( ≳ 10 8 yr) due to weak Γdip 

that will act on the source after the inactivation of the disc at an age 

𝜏 ∼ a few × 10 6 yr. Our simulations show that Calvera and similar CCOs evolve vertically downward in the 𝑃 − ¤ 𝑃 diagram and remain above or close to the upper border of the death valley emerging as RPs.
