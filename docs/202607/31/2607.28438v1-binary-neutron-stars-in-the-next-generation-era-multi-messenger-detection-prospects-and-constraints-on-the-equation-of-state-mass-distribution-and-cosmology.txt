ABSTRACT 

Next-generation gravitational-wave (GW) observatories will provide crucial insights into the nature of neutron star (NS) matter and the cosmological expansion history. We estimate the number of multi-messenger detections from binary neutron stars (BNS) with the Einstein Telescope (ET) and Cosmic Explorer (CE), and project the resulting constraints on the equation of state (EOS), BNS mass distribution, and cosmology via joint hierarchical Bayesian inference. Assuming a local merger rate of 106.6 Gpc −3 yr −1 and considering two different mass functions, a narrow one centred around 1.4 𝑀 ⊙ and a wide one ranging between 1.1–2 𝑀 ⊙ , we find that for ET, our mock follow-up algorithm results in at least ∼ 40 and up to ∼ 100 successfully identified electromagnetic counterparts per year, depending on the detector layout and mass distribution. In a joint network with CE, the number of multi-messenger detections can range from ∼ 200 to ∼ 500 . Additionally, several more afterglows from gamma-ray bursts or KNe could be found with dedicated late-time observations. Based on the identified multi-messenger events, we perform an injection campaign to hierarchically constrain the EOS, mass distribution, and cosmology in a fully Bayesian framework. Focusing on ET alone, we show how in an ideal scenario, GW signals, KNe, and host galaxy redshifts can constrain the canonical NS radius 𝑅 1.4 within ∼ 0.2 km and the Hubble constant 𝐻 0 within ∼ 1 km s −1 Mpc −1, while recovering the essential features of the mass distribution. By comparing inference results that rely solely on GW data and those that incorporate light curve information, we find that while KN light-curve posteriors have a negligible impact on the EOS constraints, they can benefit the inference of cosmological parameters. 

Key words: neutron star mergers – gravitational waves – equation of state – cosmological parameters – methods: data analysis. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-messenger signals from binary neutron stars (BNSs) provide the opportunity to constrain fundamental physical parameters such as the equation of state (EOS) for dense nuclear matter or the cos-mological expansion rate. While current gravitational wave (GW) detectors and electromagnetic (EM) telescopes successfully iden-tified GW170817 (Abbott et al. 2017a,c) together with the asso-ciated gamma-ray burst (GRB) GRB170817 (Abbott et al. 2017d; Savchenko et al. 2017; Goldstein et al. 2017), the kilonova (KN) AT2017gfo (Coulter et al. 2017; Tanvir et al. 2017; Valenti et al. 2017; Lipunov et al. 2017; Soares-Santos et al. 2017; Arcavi et al. 2017), and the GRB afterglow (Troja et al. 2017; Margutti et al. 

∗E-mail: hauke.koehn@uni-potsdam.de 

2017; Haggard et al. 2017; Hallinan et al. 2017; Alexander et al. 2017), a significant increase in multi-messenger BNS detections is expected to occur only with next-generation GW detectors (Punturo et al. 2010; Reitze et al. 2019; Evans et al. 2021; Maggiore et al. 2020; Branchesi et al. 2023). However, it remains uncertain how many bright BNS mergers can be anticipated. This is due to several factors, most notably the largely unknown merger rate and modelling systematics in the EM emission. The current estimate for the local BNS merger rate from the fifth GW catalogue is 5.1–154.7 Gpc −3yr −1 (Abac et al. 2026a), meaning detection prospects for BNS GW signals span almost two orders of magnitude. Furthermore, how many BNS GW signals will be accompanied by a successfully identified EM counterpart depends on the brightness of the KN, GRB, and afterglow emission. The emission of the quasi-thermal KN stems from the decay heat produced by the 

𝑟 -process nuclei synthesized in the mildly relativistic ejecta from the

merger, but the total amount of ejecta is highly sensitive to the neutron star (NS) masses, spins, and EOS (e.g. Radice et al. 2018b; Kiuchi et al. 2019; Krüger & Foucart 2020; Nedora et al. 2022; Neuweiler et al. 2026). Therefore, the uncertainty in the BNS mass distribution and EOS directly impacts estimates for the amount of observable KNe. Moreover, current models of the KN emission itself suffer from several unknown microphysical systematics (Sarin & Rosswog 2024; Brethauer et al. 2024), further complicating estimates of the peak flux and, hence, the total KN count. Likewise, the process behind the launch of the ultra-relativistic component as well as the microphysical processes leading to the GRB prompt and afterglow emission remain poorly understood (Wang et al. 2015; Aksulu et al. 2022; Ruiz et al. 2021; Miceli & Nava 2022; Hayashi et al. 2025). At the same time, estimating the frequency of multi-messenger detections is of high interest, since these events offer significant scientific potential. Among other results, GW170817 has proven how joint analysis of multi-messenger signals can deliver improved constraints on the EOS and Hubble constant compared to inference with one GW signal alone (e.g. Abbott et al. 2017b; Radice et al. 2018a; Hotokezaka et al. 2019; Dietrich et al. 2020; Wang & Giannios 2021; Raaijmakers et al. 2021a; Nicholl et al. 2021; Pang et al. 2023). It is expected that because of the increasing detection frequency and overall better sensitivity, next-generation GW interferometers can provide even better constraints on the BNS population (Singh et al. 2022), the EOS (Breschi et al. 2022; Gupta et al. 2022; Walker et al. 2024; Finstad et al. 2023; Iacovelli et al. 2023), and the Hubble constant (Zhang et al. 2020; Mitra et al. 2021; Chatterjee et al. 2021; Califano et al. 2023; Han et al. 2026). Reconstructing these universal physical parameters from an ensemble of observed events is not trivial though, as it requires careful statistical analysis to hierarchically infer them from the measured data. Since this method involves several computational challenges, it is not just the detection rates that are uncertain, but also the projections about statistical accuracies with which these general parameters can be constrained. Recently, several studies have assessed the prospects and chal-lenges of multi-messenger detections of BNSs in the next-generation era. For instance, Loffredo et al. (2025) investigates the number of optical counterparts that could be identified with the Vera Rubin tele-scope after GW detection from CE or ET, assuming different EOSs, mass distributions, and merger rates. Colombo et al. (2025) study how many neutron star-black hole and BNS systems produce suffi-ciently bright radio, optical, X-ray, or gamma-ray counterparts for detection. Bisero et al. (2026) focus on synergies between wide-field spectroscopic facilities and GW observations of BNSs. A projection of joint GW and high-energy EM detections is given in Ronchini et al. (2022), analysing also the prospect of mutual localization. Kaur et al. (2024) assess the efficiency of prompt GRB detections and broad-band afterglow searches in the upcoming O5 observing run of the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA Scientific Collaboration (LVK) collaboration and for a next-generation observing period, while Dobie et al. (2021) focus on radio follow-up of BNS mergers for next-generation GW as-tronomy. Steinle et al. (2025) model how stellar population synthesis affects the set of observable multi-messenger BNS events. Some works focus on the recovery of hierarchical physical param-eters from these multi-messenger events. Han et al. (2026) use Fisher information matrix (FIM) posteriors from their projected multi-messenger population to analyse to what extent cosmological pa-rameters can be inferred. Khadkikar et al. (2025) assess how well NS radii can be constrained after a hundred BNS detections with next-generation detectors. Several works are also concerned with joint Bayesian inference of the BNS mass distribution and EOS (Wysocki et al. 2020; Golomb & Talbot 2022; Golomb et al. 2025; Biswas & Rosswog 2025), but focus exclusively on the GW signal. Ghosh et al. (2025) perform joint hierarchical inference of the EOS, mass distribution, and cosmology from BNS signals in O5 without an EM counterpart. In this work, we provide a comprehensive overview of how many and which types of BNS multi-messenger signals can be detected with next-generation GW detectors. Subsequently, we also show how well hierarchical parameters such as the EOS, mass distribution, or 

𝐻 0 can be inferred from them. To this end, we create different mock catalogues of the merging BNS population for a one-year observa-tion run. Based on various assumptions, we forward-model the EM observables, i.e. generate predictions for the prompt GRB, KN, GRB afterglow, and KN afterglow for each BNS, and construct a mock observation algorithm to mimic how different EM telescopes would perform targeted follow-up observations. We compare the expected rates for two different BNS mass distributions, assuming different scenarios for the Einstein Telescope (ET) and Cosmic Explorer (CE) detectors. Thereafter, we use the resulting joint GW and KN “de-tections” in an injection-recovery campaign to project the possible constraints on the EOS, mass distribution, and cosmological param-eters using full Bayesian hierarchical inference. The article is structured as follows: Section 2 describes how we generate a mock population of BNS multi-messenger events. In Sec. 3, we implement a detection algorithm to assess how many joint GW and EM detections can be expected from our catalogues with different detector and telescope configurations. Section 4 then focuses on reconstructing general physical parameters from multi-messenger events using hierarchical inference. We provide a sum-mary and discussion of our results in Sec. 5. We consider four scenarios for the next-generation GW detector land-scape. In the first one, there is one triangle-shaped ET detector (Pun-turo et al. 2010; Maggiore et al. 2020; Branchesi et al. 2023; Abac et al. 2026b) with 10 km arm lengths located in the Meuse-Rhine Euregion. 3.3 Detection prospects 

In Fig. 5, we show the distribution of BNS multi-messenger detec-tions across redshift for the different mass distributions and for the different next-generation detector networks. Further, in Table 4 we list the total number of detections per instrument. For brevity, we will refer to the event catalogues by their mass distribution and detector network, e.g., “narrow+ET Δ+CE” for the detection catalogue with NSs drawn from the narrow mass distribution and ET Δ+CE as GW network. As expected from previous studies (Evans et al. 2021; Branch-esi et al. 2023; Regimbau et al. 2012; Singh et al. 2022; Walker et al. 2024), next-generation GW detectors will detect almost every merging BNS within 𝑧 ≲ 0.4 and occasionally catch signals out to 

𝑧 ≈ 2 − 3. Apart from the sensitivity of the detectors, the redshift cut-off additionally depends on the mass distribution. For instance, the wide distribution contains heavy BNSs with total source frame mass 𝑚 1 + 𝑚 2 ≳ 3 𝑀 ⊙ that can still be detected at 𝑧 ≳ 2.The most important difference between the different detector con-figurations with respect to prospective multi-messenger detections regards the number of events with tight sky localization. For ET alone, the number of BNS signals with ΔΩ GW ≤ 100 deg 2 is ∼ 70 

and ∼ 350 respectively for ET Δ and ETL. If CE is included, the increased base line between the detectors causes gwfish to estimate almost every sky localization to be within 100 deg 2, even for BNSs at high redshifts. In fact, for ET Δ+CE 10% of all detected GW events have ΔΩ GW ≤ 22 deg 2, 50% of events have ΔΩ GW ≤ 80 deg 2.These limits are reached by only 0.1% or 0.6% of events for a single ET Δ detector. Correspondingly, for ETL+CE, the 10% quantile in the ΔΩ GW distribution is at 13 deg 2, the median at 48 deg 2. In an ETL only network, again just 0.1% and 0.7% of events stay below these limits. The choice of mass distribution only has a minor ef-fect on the number of well-localized BNS signals, although the wide mass distribution tends to create slightly more such events, because heavier NSs tend to produce signals with higher SNR. While less relevant for good sky localization, the mass distribu-tion has a large impact on the detectability of the EM counterpart because it determines how many BNSs undergo prompt collapse. We emphasize again that we count an early UVOIR counterpart as detected if there are at least two different detection flags, either in different epochs, instruments, or bands and that the light curve could be from a KN or near-optical GRB afterglow, or both. Overall, the narrow mass distribution leads to more immediate counterparts, as we project 59 detections for ET Δ, 91 for ETL, 242 for ET Δ+CE, and even 497 for ETL+CE. In case of the wide mass distribution, the corresponding numbers drop to 43, 61, 107, and 195. The difference is mainly driven by the different number of bright KNe. Even though some BNSs from the wide mass model can occasionally produce very bright KNe when the component masses are asymmetric and not too large, the narrow mass model has a lower prompt collapse rate and thus produces more visible KNe and hence more imme-diate multi-messenger detections can be expected from the narrow mass model. As a consequence of the better sky localization in the CE networks, the narrow+ETL+CE catalogue therefore provides the most successful follow-ups, whereas wide+ET Δ delivers the fewest events. In any case, the Vera Rubin telescope dominates the counterpart search and provides the largest share of detections. However, there remain certain detections that are not observed by Vera Rubin at all, either because of sky accessibility or because of the more stringent threshold on ΔΩ we have set for this telescope to start a survey (see Appendix B). Especially for the narrow mass model and with CE, there are about ∼ 30 observed UVOIR counterparts not identified through Vera Rubin, though for the other catalogues, this number ranges between 5 and 20 . Conversely, our observation strategy causes Vera Rubin to sometimes flag transients just once and so these are not included as a full detection, as described in Sec. 3.2. Depending on the mass distribution and GW network, this happens 8 to 70 times and is roughly equally likely to happen in the 𝑔 or 𝑖 band. As mentioned, not all UVOIR detections correspond to KNe, but some can also arise just from near-optical GRB afterglows. This is visualized in Fig. 5, where the orange line indicates all detected early UVOIR counterparts and the orange panels represent those events with a noticeable KN contribution. The latter is a post-processing step, that is performed after running our mock detection algorithm. To mark those light curves with a KN contribution, we apply a simplistic criterion by selecting those light curves where at least once after detection 30% of the total flux arise from KN emission (i.e., there is a time point after detection where the KN is not outshined by more than 1 mag). In reality, the contribution of the different components would only become distinguishable through model comparison and a larger photometric (or spectroscopic) data set, including for instance radio or X-ray observations. In general, most UVOIR counterparts found within 𝑑 𝐿 ≲ 3500 Mpc have a noticeable contribution from a KN and for farther events, the UVOIR counterpart is likely from a GRB afterglow. Observation and confident classification of these afterglows, however, would require additional late time observations not included in our algorithm, since the peak times of the observable KN afterglows in our sample range from ∼200–11000 days after merger, with the median peak time be-ing around ∼ 2000 days. In our algorithm, GRB detection is independent of the GW network, although alerts from the early inspiral in the detector could lead to additional GRB detections from instruments with smaller FOV but higher sensitivity (Banerjee et al. 2023; Sachdev et al. 2020). On top of the previously discussed systematic issues in modelling the EM counterpart of BNS systems, our projection for the number of multi-messenger detections presented here suffers from additional short-comings. One issue is that for the large search areas in the follow-up, other transients will pollute the survey (Scolnic et al. 2018; Barna et al. 2025; Stevenson et al. 2026; Fulton et al. 2025). Additional pointings would be needed to confidently identify the one transient corresponding to the BNS merger. In our approach, we neglect this circumstance and simply claim detection once the transient has been in one of the tilings, thus assuming more confident detections than might be possible in a real follow-up scenario. This effect naturally applies more severely to GW detector configurations with worse sky localizations. On the other hand, there are some assumptions in our detection algorithm that might counteract an overestimation of possible counterparts. For one, we always search the tiles in random order, while other algorithms suggest focusing on the tiles with the highest posterior likelihood first, especially if there are multiple alerts or telescopes (Dobie et al. 2019; Coughlin et al. 2019b; Gupte & Bartos 2020). Moreover, a biased search towards the known galaxies within the search volume might enhance the detection probability as well, provided complete galaxy catalogues are available in direction and up to the distance of the event (Gehrels et al. 2016; Coughlin et al. 2018, 2019b; Dálya et al. 2018; Dobie et al. 2021). Beyond the systematics associated with the identification of EM counterparts, uncertainties also arise from the treatment of the GW detection. Specifically, by assuming a duty cycle of 100% for each detector, we overestimate the number of detections with tight sky lo-calization. While the ETL configuration usually provides tighter sky localizations due to its larger baseline than ET Δ, a single L-shaped detector is essentially blind to sky location. Therefore, in case only one L-shaped interferometer operates, e.g., through asynchronous duty cycles etc., no follow-up EM detections can be made, such that, over a full observation cycle, the ET Δ design might deliver better relative detection prospects than indicated in the present work (Ne-gri et al. 2026). The estimates presented here are therefore purely driven by the SNR, in which case the 2L-shaped layout has an ad-vantage over ET Δ in terms of sky localization. Moreover, the sky localization might be overestimated due to known shortcomings and limitations in the FIM formalism (Vallisneri 2008; Rodriguez et al. 2013; Dhani et al. 2025a). Further complications, e.g., PSD estima-tion (Goncharov et al. 2022), glitch mitigation (Narola et al. 2025), detector calibration (Wong et al. 2025), correlated noise (Janssens et al. 2022), overlapping GW signals (Baka et al. 2025), or the break-down of the long wavelength approximation (Virtuoso & Milotti 2025; Baker et al. 2025) should be addressed in the future. 

4 HIERARCHICAL RECOVERY OF PHYSICAL PARAMETERS 

Having constructed catalogues of multi-messenger BNS mock de-tections in Sec. 3, the aim in this section is to project how well these events could be used to constrain the EOS, mass distribution, and cos-mology. Since these parameters apply to each BNS simultaneously, they are hyperparameters that need to be inferred in a hierarchical setup. We introduce this method in the first subsection, and in the second subsection, we apply it to jointly constrain the EOS and BNS mass distribution. In the third subsection, we additionally incorpo-rate cosmological parameters in our hierarchical inference setup. To limit the scope, we restrict ourselves to ET-only catalogues and ig-nore multi-messenger events without a noticeable KN. Including CE events and EM-bright events without a KN may be investigated in future work.

4.4 Discussion 

Naturally, several caveats apply to the hierarchical analyses presented here. Most importantly, we assumed perfect knowledge about the GW waveform and KN emission by injecting and recovering the signals with the same models. Systematic uncertainties and hitherto missing physical features in current waveform models could lead to biased posteriors for tidal deformabilities, especially at next-generation ac-curacy (Wade et al. 2014; Samajdar & Dietrich 2018, 2019; Gamba et al. 2021; Kunert et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2026; Gittins et al. 2026). Although potentially this could be handled by introducing systematic waveform parameters (Kumar et al. 2026), there could be a severe impact on the recovered EOS and might lead to tensions with other NS observations or nuclear physics constraints. Even more prone to systematic modelling issues is the determination of the ejecta from KN observations. King et al. (2025) demonstrate how a mis-match between the modelled and true ejecta morphology can lead to significantly biased dynamical ejecta estimates. Brethauer et al. (2024) conclude that uncertainties in the atomic data and thermaliza-tion lead to typical ejecta uncertainties of ≳ 50% . Another layer of systematics is introduced when linking the ejecta to BNS component masses via semi-empirical fitting formulae from NR simulations. The difference between different fitting formulae is quantified in Henkel et al. (2023). Henkel et al. (2025) also show how different KN mod-els can over- or underestimate NS masses, when the ejecta are used to trace back the binary’s components. Although we broadened our KN posteriors by sampling over a systematic uncertainty parameter 

𝜎 sys (see Appendix E), a significant bias in the inferred ejecta masses cannot be excluded when analysing real data (Hussenot-Desenonges et al. 2025). While introducing more systematic parameters might mitigate biases, it also comes with an increase of the computational sampling cost (Jhawar et al. 2025). At the same time, it is not only the EOS, but also the inference of the mass distribution and the cosmol-ogy that could be affected by wrong a-priori assumptions or model mismatches. Biscoveanu et al. (2022) demonstrate how mismodeling the spin distribution can lead to biases in the recovered mass dis-tribution because of the well-known degeneracy between effective spin and mass ratio. Our results also show that the mass distribution is rather sensitive to the modelling choices used to account for the selection effect. Regarding the cosmological parameters, Canevarolo & Chisari (2024) investigate systematic biases caused by lensing and matter effects during the propagation of the GW. Müller et al. (2024) and Salvarese & Chen (2024) study how 𝐻 0-inference from EM bright BNSs could be biased if the orbital inclination and GRB jet are misaligned or the EM inference for the viewing angle is biased. Further, the bright siren approach requires accurate estimation of the peculiar galactic velocities to correctly extract the cosmological redshift (Mukherjee et al. 2021; Howlett & Davis 2020), although this becomes less important for farther events. We also note that in-clination and distance measurements for 𝐻 0 and Ω0 are dependent on accurate GW detector strain calibration (Capote et al. 2025) and noise characterization (Mozzon et al. 2022; Kumar et al. 2025). 

5 CONCLUSION 

In this study, we have estimated how many and which types of multi-messenger BNS events can be expected in the era of next-generation GW detectors. We also assessed how well these observations con-strain important physical parameters like the EOS, mass distribution, and Hubble constant. We considered two different mass distributions and overall four possible GW detector networks, finding relatively large differences between these distinct setups. 

Detection prospects 

Regarding the projected number of detections, we find that one can expect to observe 40–100 immediate UVOIR counterparts per year by following up BNS GW signals from ET Δ or ETL alone, assuming our specific value of 106.6 Gpc −3 yr −1 for the local merger rate. This number could increase to 200–500 if ET is joined by CE in a global GW detector network. Bearing in mind the spread of the local merger rate, this is in broad agreement with many other studies (e.g. Branch-esi et al. 2023; Loffredo et al. 2025; Steinle et al. 2025; Colombo et al. 2025). Further, we show that many of the multi-messenger BNSs produce an observable afterglow, either from a GRB or from the KN. Some afterglows might even be found without an immediate UVOIR counterpart through dedicated late-time afterglow surveys, especially in the radio. For the setups with ET alone, the total number of afterglows varies between 70–90, with CE it can go up as high as 160–350. We remark that these counts include long-term follow-ups that can span up to 10 years. Apart from the design for the GW detector network, the number of successfully identified EM counterparts also depends on which telescopes will be used and which detection strategy they adopt. Out of the observatories considered in this study, Vera Rubin generally identifies the most UVOIR counterparts. However, the observation strategy for Rubin’s target-of-opportunity strategy is still being fi-nalized (Andreoni et al. 2024a) and therefore the detection counts are subject to uncertainty. In particular, our observation strategy involved only the 𝑔 and 𝑖 filters, whereas Andreoni et al. (2022) ar-gue that observation strategies with five filters and longer exposure times improve the chance of distinguishing KNe from background transients. The results from Stevenson et al. (2026) show that KN detection is most likely in the 𝑟 and 𝑖 band. Moreover, additional telescopes could be deployed to follow up on GW alerts in the next-generation era, or some of the existing telescopes we listed in Table 2 will stop operating without a comparable successor. We refer to Abac et al. (2026b, Figure 4.16) for a comprehensive list on other possible telescopes, which could alter the multi-messenger detection numbers notably (Salafia et al. 2017).

We observe tighter constraints for the parameters of the wide mass distribution, mainly because it is inherently easier to fit. But for both mass models, individual distribution parameters might be inferred with a bias arising from selection events, since our analyses focus only on events with a KN. There remain several challenges to hierarchical BNS inference combining ten or more events. For one, this method requires accu-rate modelling of the detection probability. This is especially rele-vant when inferring mass distribution and cosmological parameters. While GW detection probabilities can be handled comparatively well (Farr 2019; Gerosa et al. 2020; Gerosa & Bellotti 2024), ad-dressing multi-messenger selection effects remains challenging. We have adopted a coarse approach and post-process our hyperposterior samples by reweighting them with a simplistic model for the de-tection probability. This systematically reduces the effective sample size of our hyperposteriors and also proves insufficient to reliably recover the correct mass distribution parameters. Future studies have to investigate improved methods to assess selection effects and how they might be applied directly during sampling. Furthermore, when analysing real data, the impact of outliers in the posterior set should be addressed. Even with improved sampling methods, posteriors will occasionally contain computational sampling errors and biases. We observed this when sampling the individual GW and EM parameter posteriors from our events. We would find that the true mass ra-tio, tidal deformability, or inclination was sometimes not contained within the posterior. We alleviated such pathologies by running with a different sampling seed or occasionally cutting some prior to a narrower range (without impairing the posterior). Obviously, this is not an option for a real data catalogue where the true values are not known. Future hierarchical inference algorithms could instead include statistical classification to flag certain posteriors as outliers during sampling (e.g. Martin & Mortlock 2025; Leeney et al. 2025). This seems especially relevant for inferring 𝐻 0, which is particularly sensitive to flawed inclination and distance posteriors. 

Concluding remarks 

Despite systematic issues and the general challenges discussed throughout the present article, multi-messenger BNS events provide uniquely powerful opportunities to obtain empirical constraints about the EOS, BNS mass distribution, and Hubble constant. Bayesian hi-erarchical inference provides the natural framework to recover the hyperparameters of interest from the observed data by utilizing the individual source parameter posteriors. The use of NFs and advanced sampling techniques enables us to efficiently sample the hyperpos-terior within hours on modern GPUs, further strengthening the case for adopting hardware acceleration in hierarchical sampling for GW problems at scale (Mastrogiovanni et al. 2024; Borghi et al. 2024; Talbot et al. 2025; Mould et al. 2025; Tagliazucchi et al. 2025; Pa-padopoulos et al. 2026; Tagliazucchi et al. 2026). Our results imply that there are going to be multiple occasions in the next-generation era where these methods could be applied to analyse real data from joint GW and EM observations, although the exact number of multi-messenger events will depend on the true mass distribution, EOS, GW network sensitivity, and most notably merger rate. Finally, we remark that although measuring the EOS and Hubble parameter ar-guably represent the most prominent science goals with respect to multi-messenger observations of BNSs, other highly relevant appli-cations exist. For instance, identifying the host galaxies could deliver crucial insights on structure formation and stellar history (Rose et al. 2021; Stevance et al. 2023), while joint GRB and GW detections would shine light on the nature of the GRB central engine (Beni-amini et al. 2020; van Putten & Della Valle 2023; Dall’Osso et al. 2023; Gottlieb et al. 2025). Similarly, it has been suggested that type Ia supernovae could be calibrated independently of cepheids when they take place in a galaxy before or after NSs merge there (Zhao & Santos 2019; Gupta et al. 2019). Thus, various avenues exist in which future multi-messenger BNS mergers have the potential to reshape our current understanding of astrophysics.

APPENDIX C: OBSERVATION ALGORITHM FOR WIDE-FIELD AFTERGLOW SEARCHES 

If no UVOIR counterpart is found in the immediate aftermath of the merger, we suppose that Vera Rubin and the telescopes listed in Table 3 consider a wide-field follow-up campaign for the after-glow, provided certain conditions are met. Concretely, we require 

𝑑 𝐿 ≤ 11 Gpc and ΔΩ ≤ 100 deg 2 if there is only ET Δ, ΔΩ ≤ 50 deg 2

if there is only ETL, and ΔΩ ≤ 10 deg 2 if there is CE. The difficulty in performing these follow-ups lies in the fact that the time at which the afterglow peaks depends sensitively on several factors such as in-clination, opening angle, interstellar medium density, kinetic energy, and eventually redshift. The inclination might be estimated from the GW signal, although even for high SNR the uncertainty remains at 

≳ 0.1 rad. Thus, although analytical estimates for the peak time of a GRB (Ryan et al. 2020) or KN afterglow (Kathirgamaraju et al. 2019) exist, their use for determining optimal afterglow observation times after a GW event is limited. For this reason, we simply spread ten observation periods log-uniformly between 10 days to 10 years. The last observation period serves specifically to explore the possibility for a KN afterglow detection. For radio telescopes, observations take place once the transient region is above 20 ◦ altitude and with the tiling according to Eq. (B1). This is the same for Vera Rubin, which here only observes in the 𝑔 band, except that additionally the tran-sient needs to be visible at astronomical night. If the sky location is inaccessible, we also allow later observation periods to be postponed by half a year and check whether the visibility conditions can be met then. Similarly, the space-based Einstein Probe has access to the entire night-side of the sky. If the transient is on the day side, we simply postpone the observation for half a year until the transient becomes accessible. An afterglow detection is noticed once the flux at one observing period exceeds the limits listed in Table 3.

APPENDIX G: ESTIMATING DETECTION PROBABILITIES 

To estimate the multi-messenger selection effects for the hyperpa-rameters in our hierarchical posteriors, we essentially repeat the steps from Secs. 2 and 3, but with a few simplifications to efficiently eval-uate the detection probability for the thousands of hypersamples. For a given hierarchical posterior, we first determine the GW detec-tion probability for each hyperparameter sample 𝜆 . This is done by sampling 2000 component masses from 𝑝 (𝑚 1, 𝑚 2 | 𝜆 pop ) and cal-culating the GW SNR for the corresponding detector network. For the SNR calculation, exterior parameters are drawn isotropically or uniformly. The redshifts are drawn from Eq. (2) within 𝑧 ≤ 1.2 using the same rate parameters as in Sec. 2.1. If the hierarchical posterior also contains samples for 𝐻 0 and Ω0, the conversion to luminosity distance is performed using those, otherwise this happens with the Planck18 values. To make the SNR calculation efficient, we follow the approach of Gerosa et al. (2020) and train a neural network to learn the relationship between SNR, 𝑚 1, 𝑚 2 and exterior parameters on 10 6 training data points. The GW detection probability is then proportional to the number of binaries where the SNR exceeds 12. In the next step, we also compute the EM detection probability for each hyperparameter sample. Using the EOS from the hyperpa-rameter sample and the prompt collapse criterion in Eq. Since the detailed ob-servation algorithm developed in Sec. 3 is too costly to run for each hyperparameter sample point, we simply assume the EM detection probability is proportional to the number of KN light curves where the brightness exceeds the 25.6 mag in the Rubin 𝑖 band. Typically, the detection probabilities for our hyperposterior sam-ples are very similar, though a few samples ( ∼10 out of normally 7000 hierarchical posterior samples) have a detection probability that is a factor of 2–3 lower than the typical detection probability. Since we reweight the hyperposterior according to 𝑝 det (𝜆 )−𝑛 , where 𝑛 ≳ 20 

is the number of events, this sometimes leads to situations where a single sample with low detection probability would make up 20% or more of the reweighted posterior. Such extreme distortions do not occur when we neglect the EM selection effect. Hence, this is a re-flection of our simplified treatment for the counterpart observations. In particular, EOSs with 𝑀 TOV ≲ 2.0 𝑀 ⊙ or mass distributions with a heavy tail at large masses are disproportionally disfavoured by our detection probability algorithm and become dominant after reweight-ing. For this reason, we clip their detection probability values to the 99% quantile of the overall set of values for 𝑝 det (𝜆 ).
